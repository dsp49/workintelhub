Span of Control

WH The WorkIntel Hub editorial team
September 3, 2026 · 7 min read

Span of control is how many people report directly to one manager. A wide span means many direct reports and fewer management layers. A narrow span means fewer reports and more layers between the top of the organisation and the work.

There is no correct number, and any source that gives you one without asking what the work is has skipped the question that decides it.

The trade-off

Wide span (many reports)Narrow span (few reports)
LayersFewer, flatterMore, taller
Decision speedFaster, fewer approvalsSlower, more escalation
AutonomyHigh, by necessityLower, more oversight available
Coaching per personLittleMore
Fails whenPeople need support they cannot getManagers create work to justify the role
CostLower management overheadHigher

Both failure modes are real and neither is obvious from an org chart. A manager with fifteen reports may be running a highly autonomous team well, or may be nominally responsible for fifteen people they never speak to. The number alone does not tell you which.

The four factors that actually decide it

  1. How similar the work is. Fifteen people doing near-identical work is a different job from eight people doing eight different things. Variety, not headcount, is what consumes a manager.
  2. How experienced the team is. A team of seniors needs unblocking. A team with several new joiners needs teaching, and teaching does not scale.
  3. How much the manager does besides managing. A player-coach carrying delivery work has far less capacity than the title suggests, and this is the factor most often left out of the calculation entirely.
  4. How much interruption the work carries. A team with a support rota generates escalations, and escalations land on the manager.

Commonly cited ranges sit somewhere around five to ten direct reports for complex or varied work, and considerably higher for standardised work. Treat those as a starting point to adjust from rather than a target, because the four factors above move the answer more than any benchmark does.

Signals the span is wrong

Better than a target number: the symptoms, which are specific in each direction.

Too wide. One-to-ones get cancelled routinely. Nobody has had feedback in months. Decisions wait on a manager who is a bottleneck. Reviews are written from memory. Problems surface only once they are serious.

Too narrow. Managers create process to fill the role. Approvals multiply. Small decisions travel further up than they need to. Capable people are supervised more closely than the work requires.

The first list is the more expensive of the two, and the first item is the reliable early signal. A manager who cancels one-to-ones is a manager past capacity, whatever the headcount says. Our guide to capacity planning covers the related arithmetic on the delivery side.

Changing it

Span is usually adjusted for cost, in one direction, quickly. Two cautions.

Widening spans by removing a layer moves work rather than removing it. The coaching, escalation handling and one-to-ones that layer was doing land on someone, and if nobody is given time for them they stop happening rather than move.

And the effect is not evenly distributed. Gallup reported in 2026 that global engagement declined for a second consecutive year with the sharpest decline among managers, so the layer absorbing a widened span is the one already under most pressure. If you widen spans, remove something else from those managers deliberately rather than hoping it fits.

Key points

  • Span of control is the number of direct reports one manager has.
  • Wide spans mean flatter structures and faster decisions; narrow spans mean more coaching and more layers.
  • There is no correct number. Variety of work, team experience, the manager's own delivery load and interruption volume decide it.
  • Player-coach managers have far less capacity than the title suggests, and this is the factor most often ignored.
  • Around five to ten reports is a common starting point for varied work, and considerably higher for standardised work.
  • Cancelled one-to-ones are the reliable early signal that a span is too wide.
  • Removing a management layer moves work rather than removing it, and it lands on the layer already under most pressure.

Frequently asked questions

What is span of control?

The number of employees who report directly to a single manager. A wide span means many direct reports and a flatter organisation with fewer layers; a narrow span means fewer reports and more layers between leadership and the work.

What is a good span of control?

There is no single correct figure. Around five to ten direct reports is a common starting point for complex or varied work, and standardised work supports considerably more. The factors that actually decide it are how similar the work is, how experienced the team is, how much delivery work the manager carries, and how much interruption the work generates.

What is the difference between span of control and layers?

They are inversely related. For a given headcount, widening spans reduces the number of management layers and flattens the organisation, while narrowing spans adds layers. Changing one necessarily changes the other.

How do you know if a span of control is too wide?

The symptoms are specific: one-to-ones cancelled routinely, nobody having had feedback in months, decisions waiting on a bottleneck manager, reviews written from memory, and problems surfacing only once serious. Cancelled one-to-ones are the earliest reliable signal.

What are the problems with a narrow span of control?

Managers with too few reports tend to create process to justify the role. Approvals multiply, small decisions travel further up the organisation than they need to, and capable people are supervised more closely than the work requires. It also costs more in management overhead.

Does a player-coach manager affect span of control?

Substantially, and it is the factor most often left out. A manager carrying their own delivery work has far less capacity for reports than the headcount suggests, so the same nominal span is a materially heavier job than it is for a manager who only manages.

What happens when you remove a management layer?

The work that layer performed moves rather than disappears: coaching, escalation handling and one-to-ones land on whoever remains. If those managers are not given time for it, the work stops happening rather than transferring, which usually surfaces later as a retention problem.
