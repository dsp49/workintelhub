Billable Hours

WH The WorkIntel Hub editorial team
September 8, 2026 · 8 min read

Billable hours are the hours of work that can be charged to a client at an agreed rate. Everything else a professional does in a working day, from internal meetings to invoicing to learning the new software, is non-billable.

The term belongs to professional services: law, accounting, consulting, agencies, engineering and any business that sells time. For those businesses billable hours are revenue, the ratio of billable to total hours is the profit margin in disguise, and how accurately they are recorded is the difference between what was earned and what gets paid.

What counts and what does not

BillableNon-billable
Work on a client matter, project or deliverableInternal meetings and firm administration
Client calls, meetings and correspondencePitches, proposals and business development
Research specific to the engagementGeneral training and professional development
Travel, where the engagement letter allows itTimesheets, invoicing and chasing payment
Reviewing and correcting work on the matterFixing errors the client should not pay for

The boundary is set by the engagement letter, not by the person doing the work. Two firms can treat the same hour of travel differently and both be right. What a firm cannot do is bill an hour the client was not told could be billed, which is why the letter needs to name travel, research and administrative time explicitly.

Utilisation and realisation

Two ratios turn billable hours into management information.

Utilisation rate is billable hours divided by total hours available, expressed as a percentage. Someone who bills 30 hours in a 40-hour week has a utilisation of 75 per cent. It measures how much of the paid capacity is being sold.

Realisation rate is the amount actually collected divided by the amount billable at standard rates. If 30 hours at 200 an hour is 6,000 of billable value and the client is invoiced 5,000 after write-downs and pays 4,500, realisation is 75 per cent. It measures how much of the sold time is turned into cash.

The two move independently, and a firm that watches only utilisation will push hours up while realisation falls, because tired people write more hours that get written off. The first ratio needs an accurate timesheet; the second needs the billing and collections data alongside it. Our guide to reporting capacity to leadership covers how to present both without inviting the wrong conclusion.

Tracking rules that decide whether the hours get paid

  • Increments. Law and accounting mostly bill in six-minute units, a tenth of an hour, so that 0.1 is six minutes and 1.5 is ninety. Agencies and consultancies often use fifteen minutes. The unit is a term of the engagement and should be stated in it. Our timesheet conversion chart gives the minutes for every tenth and quarter.
  • Record contemporaneously. Hours reconstructed on Friday from memory are both lower and less defensible than hours recorded as they happen. Every hour that is not written down at the time is lost at a fairly predictable rate.
  • Narrative with every entry. "Research" is written off; "research on limitation period for the counterclaim, 0.8" is paid. The description is what the client reads when deciding whether to query the bill.
  • One client code per entry. Split entries are how the same hour gets billed twice, which is the finding that ends relationships.
  • Non-billable time gets recorded too. Otherwise utilisation is calculated against a denominator nobody knows. It is also where the case for hiring or for cutting internal meetings comes from.

A timesheet template handles this for a small practice. Above a handful of fee earners, the comparison in our manual versus automatic tracking guide applies, and the case for capture that runs in the background is strongest exactly here, because every unrecorded six minutes has a price on it.

Targets, and what they do to behaviour

Most professional services firms set an annual billable target per fee earner, and the target drives more behaviour than any policy. Three effects are worth naming.

Inflation. Rounding up, generous narratives and billing for thinking time all rise as the target approaches. The realisation rate is where this shows up, six months later.

Hoarding. Work that should be delegated is kept, because delegating it gives the hours to someone else. Junior utilisation falls while senior hours rise and the client pays senior rates for junior work.

Hours as presence. A high target makes visible time the currency, and the pattern described in our presenteeism entry follows. Firms that publish utilisation league tables get more hours and less of everything else.

The correction is not to abandon targets but to report realisation next to utilisation and to treat the pair as the measure. Hours that are billed and paid are the business; hours that are billed and written off are a cost wearing a revenue label.

Key points

  • Billable hours are the hours that can be charged to a client at an agreed rate. The engagement letter, not the fee earner, sets the boundary.
  • Utilisation is billable hours over total hours. Realisation is cash collected over billable value at standard rates. Watch both or one will be gamed.
  • Law and accounting bill in six-minute tenths; agencies often use quarters. State the unit in the engagement.
  • Record as you go with a narrative and one client code per entry. Reconstructed hours are lower and get written off.
  • Record non-billable time as well, or utilisation has no denominator and the case for hiring has no evidence.
  • Targets drive inflation, hoarding and presence. Reporting realisation alongside utilisation is the correction.

Frequently asked questions

What are billable hours?

The hours of work that can be charged to a client at an agreed rate under the terms of the engagement. Work on the client's matter, calls and correspondence about it, and engagement-specific research are billable; internal meetings, business development, training and invoicing are not.

What is a good utilisation rate?

It depends on the role and the firm. Fee earners in law and consulting are often expected to bill somewhere between two thirds and four fifths of available hours, with partners and managers lower because of business development and supervision. A rate close to 100 per cent usually means non-billable time is not being recorded rather than not happening.

What is the difference between utilisation and realisation?

Utilisation measures how much of available time was billable. Realisation measures how much of the billable value was actually invoiced and collected after write-downs and discounts. A firm can have high utilisation and poor realisation, which means it is working hard and giving the work away.

How are billable hours rounded?

Most law and accounting firms record in six-minute increments, one tenth of an hour, so a four-minute call is entered as 0.1. Some firms use quarter hours. The increment should be stated in the engagement letter, and rounding every entry up is the most common cause of disputed bills.

Do billable hours include travel?

Only if the engagement letter says so. Some firms bill travel at full rate, some at half, and some not at all. The mistake is billing it without having agreed it.

Should non-billable hours be tracked?

Yes. Without them utilisation cannot be calculated, and the firm has no evidence of how much time internal meetings, administration and business development actually consume, which is the evidence needed to decide whether to hire or to cut.
