Pay Compression

WH The WorkIntel Hub editorial team
November 20, 2026 · 6 min read

Pay compression is a narrowing of the pay difference between employees with different levels of experience, skill or responsibility, typically when new hires are paid close to, or more than, long-serving staff or their supervisors.

When the gap disappears entirely and a newer or junior employee earns more than a more senior one in the same job, or a subordinate earns more than their manager, it is called pay inversion. Both are common after periods of fast wage growth, because the market rate for new hires moves faster than annual raises for existing employees.

Compression is a retention problem first: experienced employees who discover that a new hire earns nearly the same often leave for a new job, which is the only way they can capture the market rate. Pay transparency laws, which put ranges in public job postings, have made it much easier to discover.

Causes

  • Hiring at market while existing staff receive merit raises of a few percent a year; after three or four years, the gap closes.
  • Minimum wage increases that lift entry pay to, or past, the pay of employees who had been earning a little more; see minimum wage by state 2027.
  • Overtime and hourly pay above a supervisor's salary, when hourly staff work heavy overtime and salaried supervisors do not get it.
  • Small merit budgets spread evenly, so top performers are not differentiated.
  • Salary bands that are not updated with the market, so new hires are placed above the old structure.
  • Counteroffers and one-off adjustments that raise some employees and not their peers.

How to measure it

  • Supervisor-to-subordinate ratio: supervisor pay / highest-paid direct report pay. Many organizations target at least 1.10 to 1.15; below 1.05 is compression, below 1.00 is inversion.
  • Tenure-pay slope: plot pay against tenure within each job; a flat or downward line is compression.
  • New-hire compa-ratio vs incumbent compa-ratio: if new hires average 1.02 and five-year incumbents 0.95, the structure is compressed. See salary band for the formula.
  • Range penetration by tenure: long-tenured, well-performing staff should sit higher in the band than new hires.

How employers fix it

  1. Budget a separate equity adjustment pool, distinct from merit, and apply it to the most compressed roles first.
  2. Update salary bands annually with market movement and move incumbents within them.
  3. Tie placement in range to experience with a written policy, so new hires and incumbents with the same experience land at the same point.
  4. Differentiate merit increases by performance and position in range.
  5. Raise supervisor pay or introduce supervisor differentials where hourly overtime pushes subordinates above their managers.
  6. Phase the cost over two or three cycles if needed, communicating the plan to affected employees.

The pay raise calculator and retention strategies guide cover the follow-through.

Compression and pay equity law

Compression itself is not illegal. It becomes a legal problem when it falls unevenly: if long-serving women or employees of color are compressed while others received market adjustments, the result can support claims under the Equal Pay Act, Title VII and the stronger state equal pay laws in California, New York, Massachusetts, Washington, Colorado and others, which generally require pay differences between employees doing substantially similar work to be justified by factors such as seniority, merit or production. Running a compression analysis by demographic group alongside the overall analysis is good practice; see pay transparency laws by state.

Key points

  • Pay compression is a shrinking pay gap between more and less experienced employees or between managers and their reports; pay inversion is when the gap reverses.
  • Main causes are hiring at market while incumbents get small raises, minimum wage increases, overtime pushing hourly staff above salaried supervisors, and outdated salary bands.
  • Measure it with supervisor-to-subordinate pay ratios (target 1.10 to 1.15), tenure-pay slopes, and new-hire versus incumbent compa-ratios.
  • Fix it with a separate equity adjustment budget, annually updated bands, experience-based placement in range, differentiated merit and supervisor differentials.
  • Compression is lawful, but uneven compression by sex or race can support equal pay claims; analyze it by demographic group.

Frequently asked questions

What is pay compression?

A situation in which the pay difference between employees with different experience or responsibility becomes small, usually because new hires are brought in at market rates close to what long-serving employees earn after years of modest raises.

What is the difference between pay compression and pay inversion?

Compression is a narrowing of the gap; inversion is when it reverses, so a newer or junior employee earns more than a more experienced one in the same role, or a subordinate earns more than their supervisor.

What causes pay compression?

Usually the market rate for new hires rising faster than annual raises for existing staff. Minimum wage increases, overtime paid to hourly staff but not salaried supervisors, flat merit budgets, salary bands not updated with the market, and counteroffers also contribute.

How do you measure pay compression?

Compare supervisor pay with their highest-paid direct report (a ratio below about 1.05 signals compression), plot pay against tenure within each job, and compare the compa-ratios of recent hires with those of experienced incumbents in the same band.

How do companies fix pay compression?

By setting aside an equity adjustment budget separate from merit raises, updating salary bands annually, placing new hires and incumbents in the range according to the same experience rules, differentiating merit increases, and adding supervisor differentials where needed.

Is pay compression illegal?

No, but it can contribute to unlawful pay discrimination if it affects protected groups unevenly, for example if long-serving women are compressed while men received market adjustments. Equal pay laws require differences in pay for similar work to be justified by legitimate factors.
