Data Loss Prevention (DLP)

WH The WorkIntel Hub editorial team
November 20, 2026 · 6 min read

Data loss prevention (DLP) is a set of tools and policies that identify sensitive data, such as customer records, health information, payment card numbers or source code, and detect or block its movement to places it should not go, such as personal email, USB drives, unapproved cloud storage or AI tools.

DLP is a security control first and a monitoring tool second, but it watches what employees do with data, so it sits squarely inside employee monitoring policy and law. Most insider incidents are accidental rather than malicious: the Ponemon Institute's 2026 study found negligence behind about 54% of insider incidents, at an average of about $747,000 each. DLP's main job is to catch the mistaken email attachment or the customer list pasted into a chatbot before it leaves.

Modern DLP is usually part of a larger platform (Microsoft Purview, Google Workspace, cloud security suites, or insider-risk products such as Teramind's DLP tier) rather than a standalone product.

Types of DLP

TypeWhere it runsWhat it controls
Endpoint DLPAn agent on laptops and desktopsCopying to USB, printing, screenshots, clipboard, uploads from the browser, local file moves
Network DLPGateways, proxies, email serversEmail attachments, web uploads, file transfers leaving the network
Cloud DLP (often via a cloud access security broker)SaaS and cloud storage APIsSharing links, external sharing, files in sanctioned and unsanctioned cloud apps
Email DLPThe mail platformOutbound messages with sensitive content or to external domains

How DLP policies work

  1. Classify data: by content (patterns such as Social Security or card numbers, keywords, document fingerprints, machine-learning classifiers) or by labels applied by users or systems.
  2. Define rules: what data, going where, by whom, triggers what action.
  3. Act: log only, warn the user with a policy tip, require a business justification, encrypt, quarantine or block.
  4. Review: alerts go to security or compliance analysts, ideally with context from UEBA rather than raw events.

Most programs start in monitor-only mode for several weeks to tune rules, because untuned DLP produces floods of false positives that teams learn to ignore.

What DLP sees about employees

To inspect data in motion, DLP necessarily sees file names, destinations and often content of emails, uploads and transfers on managed devices and accounts, including personal webmail and personal cloud uploads made from a work device. Endpoint agents may capture screenshots or clipboard contents when a rule fires. That makes DLP subject to the same rules as other monitoring: written notice in Connecticut, Delaware and New York (see monitoring laws by state), data protection rules for UK and EU staff (see UK monitoring law), and the employee's reasonable expectation of privacy in personal communications. For the employee view, see using a work laptop for personal things.

Deploying it proportionately

  • Protect specific data classes (customer, health, payment, source code, trade secrets) rather than monitoring everything.
  • Prefer warnings and justifications to silent logging for low-risk events; most leaks are mistakes that a prompt prevents.
  • Limit who can see captured content, log their access, and set short retention for event data.
  • Publish the policy and the categories protected; the monitoring policy template has a DLP section.
  • Add rules for AI destinations to address shadow AI instead of blocking AI outright.
  • Revoke access and review DLP events at offboarding, when exfiltration risk peaks (see the offboarding checklist).

Key points

  • DLP identifies sensitive data and detects or blocks its movement to personal email, USB, unapproved cloud storage, AI tools and other unsafe destinations.
  • It comes in endpoint, network, cloud and email forms, usually as part of a larger security or insider-risk platform.
  • Policies classify data, define rules and choose actions from log-only to block; untuned rules generate noise, so programs start in monitor-only mode.
  • Because DLP inspects data in motion, it sees employee activity and content on managed devices and is covered by monitoring notice and data protection laws.
  • Deploy it proportionately: protect named data classes, prefer warnings to silent logging, restrict and log access to captured content, and publish the policy.

Frequently asked questions

What is data loss prevention?

Software and policies that recognize sensitive information, such as customer records, payment card or health data and source code, and monitor, warn about or block attempts to send or copy it to unauthorized places such as personal email, USB drives, unapproved cloud apps or AI tools.

What are the types of DLP?

Endpoint DLP runs on computers and controls USB, printing, clipboard and uploads; network DLP inspects traffic and email leaving the network; cloud DLP controls sharing in SaaS and cloud storage, often through a cloud access security broker; and email DLP checks outbound messages.

Does DLP monitor employees?

It monitors data movement, which means it records what employees send, upload or copy on managed devices and accounts, and may capture content or screenshots when a rule is triggered. For that reason it falls under employee monitoring notice laws and data protection rules.

Can DLP see personal email on a work computer?

If you use personal webmail on a managed work device, endpoint or network DLP may see attachments or content you upload through it, and can block them. It does not have access to your personal email account itself.

What is the difference between DLP and UEBA?

DLP enforces rules about specific sensitive data and destinations. UEBA builds baselines of normal behavior for users and devices and flags unusual activity. They are often combined: UEBA gives context to DLP alerts and helps prioritize them.

How do companies use DLP without over-monitoring?

By protecting defined categories of sensitive data rather than all activity, using warnings and business-justification prompts for routine events, limiting and logging who can view captured content, keeping event data briefly, and telling employees what the program covers.
