Human in the Loop

WH The WorkIntel Hub editorial team
November 20, 2026 · 6 min read

Human in the loop (HITL) is a design in which an AI system's recommendation does not take effect until a person reviews it and makes the final decision, with the authority, information and time to override the system.

In HR and employee monitoring the term matters because many laws and policies require it for significant decisions about people: hiring, promotion, pay, discipline and dismissal. A résumé screener that ranks candidates for a recruiter to review, or a productivity tool that flags an employee for a manager to investigate, is human in the loop; a system that automatically rejects applicants or issues warnings is not.

The phrase is easy to claim and hard to deliver. A reviewer who approves 400 recommendations an hour, or who cannot see why the system made them, is not exercising judgment, and regulators increasingly look at whether the review is meaningful.

In, on and out of the loop

ModelHow it worksExample
Human in the loopA person approves each decision before it takes effectA manager reviews an AI-flagged attendance issue before any discipline
Human on the loopThe system acts automatically; a person monitors and can intervene or reverseAutomated shift assignment with a scheduler who reviews exceptions
Human out of the loopThe system acts with no routine human reviewAutomatic rejection of applicants who fail a knockout question

Low-stakes, high-volume, reversible decisions (routing a ticket, suggesting a schedule) can reasonably be on or out of the loop. Decisions that affect someone's job, pay or legal rights generally should be in it.

What the law requires

  • EU AI Act: AI systems used for recruitment and for decisions on promotion, termination, task allocation and monitoring of workers are high-risk; Article 14 requires them to be designed for effective human oversight by people who understand the system's capabilities and limits, can interpret its output, and can decide not to use it or override it.
  • UK and EU GDPR: individuals have rights in relation to solely automated decisions with legal or similarly significant effects; in the UK, the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 allows such decisions for non-special-category data only with safeguards including human intervention. See UK monitoring law.
  • United States: no general requirement, but Colorado's SB 26-189, which replaced the 2024 AI Act and applies to consequential decisions from January 1, 2027, requires pre-use notice, an adverse-outcome letter within 30 days and human review by a person with authority to override who considers the primary evidence; New York City's Local Law 144 regulates AI that "substantially assists or replaces" hiring decisions; California's civil rights regulations on automated decision systems in employment took effect in October 2025. See AI hiring laws by state and California AI workplace laws.
  • Anti-discrimination law: a human sign-off does not cure a biased tool. Employers remain liable for disparate impact under Title VII whether or not a person approved the output.

What makes review meaningful

  1. Authority: the reviewer can override without penalty, and overrides are expected, not exceptional.
  2. Information: the reviewer sees the inputs and reasons behind a recommendation, not just a score.
  3. Time: review volume is realistic; track decisions per reviewer per hour.
  4. Competence: reviewers are trained on the tool's limits and on automation bias, the tendency to accept machine output uncritically.
  5. Evidence: the override rate is tracked. An override rate near zero suggests rubber-stamping; near 50% suggests the tool is not helping.
  6. Recourse: the affected person can see the decision and ask for reconsideration.

Examples at work

  • Hiring: AI ranks applicants; a recruiter reviews every candidate the tool would reject before rejection.
  • Performance: AI drafts review language; the manager rewrites and owns the final review (see AI in performance reviews).
  • Monitoring: a tool flags unusual data transfers; a security analyst investigates before any HR action (see UEBA).
  • Payroll: anomaly detection flags timesheets; payroll staff confirm before adjusting pay.

Key points

  • Human in the loop means a person reviews an AI recommendation and makes the final decision before it takes effect, with authority, information and time to override.
  • Human on the loop means the system acts and a person monitors and can reverse; out of the loop means no routine human review.
  • The EU AI Act requires effective human oversight of high-risk AI, which includes most AI used in hiring, task allocation, monitoring and termination decisions.
  • In the US, Colorado's SB 26-189 (from 2027), NYC Local Law 144 and California's automated decision system regulations (October 2025) shape human review in employment; a human sign-off does not remove liability for a biased tool.
  • Meaningful review needs override authority, visible reasons, realistic volume, training on automation bias, tracked override rates and a route for the affected person to appeal.

Frequently asked questions

What does human in the loop mean?

An approach to AI in which the system makes a recommendation but a person reviews it and makes the final decision before anything happens. The person must have the authority, information and time to disagree with the system.

What is the difference between human in the loop and human on the loop?

In the loop, a person approves each decision before it takes effect. On the loop, the system acts automatically and a person monitors it and can intervene or reverse decisions. Out of the loop, there is no routine human review.

Is human in the loop required by law?

For high-risk AI in the EU, the AI Act requires effective human oversight. UK and EU data protection law restricts solely automated decisions with significant effects. In the US there is no general requirement, but Colorado's SB 26-189 (from January 1, 2027) requires human review with authority to override for consequential decisions, and NYC Local Law 144 and California's regulations set further expectations.

What is automation bias?

The tendency of people to accept recommendations from automated systems without enough scrutiny, even when other information suggests the system is wrong. It is the main reason nominal human review can fail in practice.

Does human review protect an employer from AI bias claims?

Not by itself. If an AI tool produces discriminatory outcomes, the employer can be liable under anti-discrimination law even if a person approved each decision. Human review helps only if reviewers can and do correct the tool's errors.

How do you know human review is working?

Track the override rate, the number of decisions each reviewer makes per hour, and outcomes by demographic group. Very low override rates or very high review volumes suggest rubber-stamping; reviewers should also be able to explain why they accepted a recommendation.
