Regular Rate of Pay

WH The WorkIntel Hub editorial team
November 20, 2026 · 7 min read

The regular rate of pay is the hourly rate, including most bonuses, commissions and premiums, on which a non-exempt employee's overtime must be calculated under the Fair Labor Standards Act; it is total includable pay for the workweek divided by total hours worked.

It is not the same as the base hourly rate. An employee paid $20 an hour who also earns a $2 night differential on some shifts and a $100 weekly production bonus has a regular rate above $20, and overtime at time and a half must be paid on that higher figure. Getting this wrong is one of the most common overtime violations the Department of Labor finds, because payroll systems often apply the 1.5 multiplier to base pay only.

The rule comes from section 7(e) of the FLSA (29 U.S.C. 207(e)) and the Department of Labor's interpretive regulations in 29 CFR Part 778.

The formula

Regular rate = total includable compensation for the workweek / total hours actually worked in the workweek. Overtime due = 0.5 × regular rate × overtime hours, on top of straight-time pay for all hours already included in the total. Equivalently, for an hourly employee whose only extra is a weekly bonus: overtime = 1.5 × base rate × overtime hours + 0.5 × (bonus / total hours) × overtime hours.

The calculation is done for each workweek separately; the FLSA does not allow averaging across two weeks (29 CFR 778.104). The regular rate can never be lower than the applicable minimum wage.

What must be included

Section 7(e) starts from the premise that all remuneration for employment is included unless it falls into one of the listed exclusions. Commonly included items:

  • Hourly wages, salary (for non-exempt salaried employees) and piece rates.
  • Shift differentials and hazard, on-call and other premiums paid for the nature of the work, not for working long hours.
  • Nondiscretionary bonuses: production, attendance, safety, quality, retention and sign-on bonuses that are announced in advance or promised by a formula or agreement (29 CFR 778.211).
  • Commissions, allocated back over the workweeks in which they were earned if paid later (778.117 to 778.120).
  • Retroactive pay increases, which raise the regular rate for the weeks they cover (778.303); see the retro pay calculator.
  • Lump sums that are in substance pay for hours worked, such as a bonus for working a holiday shift that is not a true premium.

What can be excluded

  • Gifts and discretionary bonuses, where the employer retains discretion over both whether and how much to pay until near the end of the period (778.211). Calling a promised bonus "discretionary" does not make it so.
  • Pay for hours not worked: vacation, holiday, sick pay and similar leave pay.
  • Expense reimbursements for reasonable business expenses, such as mileage at or below the IRS rate (see the mileage calculator).
  • Overtime premiums already paid at 1.5 times or more for hours over 8 a day or 40 a week, on weekends or holidays, or outside the normal schedule; these are credited toward overtime due rather than added to the rate (778.201 to 778.207).
  • Profit-sharing, savings and qualifying benefit plan contributions, and payments to bona fide retirement, health and insurance plans.
  • Since a 2019 final rule, a range of perks: wellness programs, gym memberships, tuition benefits, employee discounts, and payments for unused leave, among others.

Worked examples

Hourly with a weekly bonus. $20 an hour, 46 hours, $92 production bonus. Straight time 46 × $20 = $920, plus bonus $92 = $1,012 total. Regular rate $1,012 / 46 = $22.00. Overtime premium 6 × 0.5 × $22 = $66. Total pay $1,078. Paying 6 × 1.5 × $20 = $180 on top of 40 × $20 + $92 gives $1,072, which is $6 short for the week.

Shift differential. $18 an hour, 44 hours, 20 of them on nights at a $2 differential. Total $18 × 44 + $2 × 20 = $832. Regular rate $832 / 44 = $18.91. Overtime premium 4 × 0.5 × $18.91 = $37.82. Total $869.82.

Annual bonus paid later. A $1,200 nondiscretionary annual bonus must be allocated over the year's workweeks; for an employee who worked 200 overtime hours across 2,280 total hours, the extra overtime owed is 0.5 × ($1,200 / 2,280) × 200 = $52.63, a calculation often done as a single percentage of the bonus (here 4.4%).

Salaried, piece-rate and two-rate employees

  • Non-exempt salaried: divide the weekly salary by the hours it is intended to cover (usually 40); if it is meant to cover all hours worked under a fluctuating workweek arrangement, divide by actual hours and pay a half-time premium (778.114; see the fluctuating workweek calculator).
  • Piece-rate: total piece earnings plus any other includable pay, divided by total hours; overtime is the half-time premium on that rate (778.111).
  • Two or more rates in a week: use the weighted average of all straight-time earnings divided by total hours (778.115), or, by prior agreement, time and a half of the rate for the work done in the overtime hours (778.419).
  • States: California and several others have their own regular-rate rules, including daily overtime and specific treatment of flat-sum bonuses (Alvarado v. Dart Container, 2018). See overtime laws by state.

Key points

  • The regular rate is total includable pay for a workweek divided by total hours worked, and overtime is owed at 1.5 times that rate, not merely 1.5 times base pay.
  • Include shift differentials, nondiscretionary bonuses, commissions and retroactive raises; exclude gifts, truly discretionary bonuses, leave pay, expense reimbursements, overtime premiums and qualifying benefit contributions.
  • Bonuses paid after the fact must be allocated back to the workweeks they cover and the extra half-time premium paid for overtime hours in those weeks.
  • Each workweek stands alone; no averaging across weeks, and the regular rate cannot fall below minimum wage.
  • Applying the multiplier only to base pay is among the most common overtime violations; California and other states add their own regular-rate rules.

Frequently asked questions

What is the regular rate of pay?

Under the FLSA, the hourly rate on which overtime must be calculated: all includable pay for the workweek, such as wages, shift differentials, nondiscretionary bonuses and commissions, divided by all hours worked that week. It is often higher than the base hourly rate.

How do you calculate the regular rate of pay?

Add all includable pay for the workweek and divide by total hours worked. For $20 an hour over 46 hours plus a $92 bonus, the regular rate is ($920 + $92) / 46 = $22. Overtime adds 0.5 × $22 for each of the 6 overtime hours, $66, to the straight-time total.

Is a bonus included in the regular rate?

Nondiscretionary bonuses are: production, attendance, safety, retention and sign-on bonuses promised in advance or paid by formula. Truly discretionary bonuses, where the employer decides both whether and how much near the end of the period, and gifts such as a modest holiday bonus not tied to work, are excluded.

Is a shift differential part of the regular rate?

Yes. Night, weekend and other differentials paid for the nature of the shift are part of the regular rate and increase the overtime rate for the week. Only premiums paid specifically as overtime at 1.5 times or more can be excluded and credited.

What is the difference between regular rate and base rate?

The base rate is the stated hourly wage. The regular rate is the base rate plus most other compensation for the week, spread over the hours worked. When there are no bonuses, differentials or commissions, they are the same.

Can the regular rate be averaged over two weeks?

No. The FLSA measures overtime and the regular rate one fixed workweek at a time. Averaging hours or pay across a biweekly period is not permitted, even when payroll is biweekly.
