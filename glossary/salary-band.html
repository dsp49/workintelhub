Salary Band

WH The WorkIntel Hub editorial team
November 20, 2026 · 7 min read

A salary band, also called a pay range or pay grade, is the span of base pay an organization will pay for a job or group of similar jobs, defined by a minimum, a midpoint and a maximum.

Bands turn pay from a series of individual negotiations into a structure. Jobs of similar value are placed in the same grade, the grade has a range tied to market data, and individuals move through the range as they gain skill and performance. Bands also make pay transparency possible: a growing list of US states and cities now require employers to disclose the range in job postings, which is only practical when the ranges already exist.

This entry covers how bands are built, the formulas used to manage them, the difference between traditional grades and broadbands, and where they are legally required.

Minimum, midpoint and maximum

  • Minimum: the lowest base pay for a fully qualified new incumbent; pay below it usually signals someone still learning the role or a band that is out of date.
  • Midpoint: normally pegged to the market rate for the job, often the 50th percentile of survey data for the organization's labor market. It is the pay for a fully competent, experienced performer.
  • Maximum: the most the organization will pay for the job, reserved for sustained top performance or rare skills. Employees at the maximum receive lump sums rather than base increases until the band moves or they are promoted.

Range spread, compa-ratio and range penetration

  • Range spread = (maximum − minimum) / minimum. Typical spreads are 30 to 40% for hourly and entry roles, 40 to 60% for professional roles, and 60 to 100% or more for senior leadership. A band of $60,000 to $90,000 has a 50% spread.
  • Midpoint = (minimum + maximum) / 2: $75,000 in the example.
  • Compa-ratio = employee's pay / band midpoint. 1.00 means paid at midpoint; 0.90 is 10% below. Organizations track average compa-ratio by team, gender and ethnicity to spot inequities.
  • Range penetration = (pay − minimum) / (maximum − minimum). An employee earning $69,000 in the band above is 30% through the range.
  • Midpoint progression: the percentage gap between midpoints of adjacent grades, commonly 10 to 15% at lower levels and 15 to 20% at higher levels, so a promotion is a meaningful step.

How bands are built

  1. Job evaluation: describe each job and group jobs of similar value (by market data, point-factor evaluation or both).
  2. Market pricing: match benchmark jobs to salary surveys for the relevant geography and industry.
  3. Set midpoints at the chosen market position (lead, match or lag) and apply the range spread.
  4. Place employees and identify anyone below minimum (green-circled, raise them) or above maximum (red-circled, freeze base pay).
  5. Maintain: move bands annually by the market movement, which is not the same as the merit budget, and recheck for pay compression.

Geographic differentials (separate bands or a percentage adjustment by location) are common for remote workforces.

Traditional grades and broadbanding

Traditional structures use many narrow grades (often 15 to 25) with spreads of 30 to 60%. Broadbanding collapses them into a few wide bands (often four to eight) with spreads of 100% or more, giving managers flexibility to reward growth without a promotion. Broadbands simplify structure but make pay decisions less constrained and harder to explain, which matters under pay transparency laws; many organizations that adopted them in the 1990s and 2000s now use bands with defined zones inside them.

Pay transparency laws

Colorado began requiring salary ranges in job postings in 2021; California, Washington and New York followed in 2023; Illinois and Minnesota from January 1, 2025; New Jersey from June 2025, Vermont from July 2025 and Massachusetts from October 2025, along with cities including New York City and Jersey City. Requirements differ on employer size, whether internal promotions are covered, and how "good faith" a range must be. A range of $40,000 to $200,000 posted to satisfy the letter of the law invites enforcement. Key points

  • A salary band is the minimum-to-maximum base pay for a job or grade, with the midpoint usually set at the market rate for a fully competent performer.
  • Range spread = (max − min) / min; compa-ratio = pay / midpoint; range penetration = (pay − min) / (max − min).
  • Typical spreads run 30 to 40% for entry roles, 40 to 60% for professionals and 60 to 100% or more for executives; adjacent midpoints are usually 10 to 20% apart.
  • Build bands from job evaluation and market pricing, move them with the market each year, and track compa-ratios by group to find inequities.
  • Colorado, California, Washington, New York, Illinois, Minnesota, New Jersey, Vermont and Massachusetts require pay ranges in job postings; ranges must be in good faith.

Frequently asked questions

What is a salary band?

A defined range of base pay, with a minimum, midpoint and maximum, that an organization pays for a job or group of similar jobs. Employees are placed within the band based on experience and performance and move through it over time.

How do you calculate a salary range midpoint?

Add the minimum and maximum and divide by two. A band of $60,000 to $90,000 has a $75,000 midpoint. Most organizations set the midpoint first, at the market rate for the job, and derive the minimum and maximum from the target range spread.

What is a compa-ratio?

An employee's pay divided by the midpoint of their salary band. A compa-ratio of 1.00 means pay at midpoint; 0.92 means 8% below; 1.10 means 10% above. Average compa-ratios by team or demographic group help identify pay inequities.

What is a typical salary range spread?

About 30 to 40% for hourly and entry-level roles, 40 to 60% for professional roles, and 60 to 100% or more for senior leadership. Wider spreads give more room for growth within a job; narrower spreads keep pay more consistent.

What is broadbanding?

A pay structure that replaces many narrow grades with a few very wide bands, often with spreads of 100% or more. It gives managers more flexibility to reward growth without promotions, but makes pay decisions harder to explain and control.

Do employers have to publish salary bands?

In a growing list of states and cities, yes, in job postings: including Colorado, California, Washington, New York, Illinois, Minnesota, New Jersey, Vermont, Massachusetts and New York City. Rules vary on employer size, internal postings and what counts as a good-faith range.
