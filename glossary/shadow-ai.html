Shadow AI

WH The WorkIntel Hub editorial team
November 20, 2026 · 6 min read

Shadow AI is the use of artificial intelligence tools, such as public chatbots, AI browser extensions and AI features in personal apps, for work without the knowledge, approval or governance of the employer.

It is the AI-era version of shadow IT, and it has grown faster. Verizon's 2026 Data Breach Investigations Report found 67% of employees who used AI services on corporate devices did so through non-corporate accounts, and that more than 15% of corporate users had unauthorized AI browser extensions installed. IBM's 2026 Cost of a Data Breach report found shadow AI involved in 43% of breaches, up from 20% a year earlier, at an average cost of $5.39 million.

Most shadow AI is well-meant: employees using a faster tool to draft, summarize, code or analyze. The risk is what they paste into it.

What counts as shadow AI

  • Pasting customer data, code or internal documents into a public chatbot through a personal account.
  • AI note-takers that employees add to meetings without the organization's approval; see AI notetaker consent.
  • Browser extensions that read page content to summarize or rewrite it.
  • AI features switched on inside approved software (a design tool, a CRM) without a review of where data goes.
  • Managers using an AI tool to screen résumés or draft performance reviews outside any approved process; see AEDT.

The risks

  • Data exposure. Cyberhaven's 2026 research found 39.7% of employee interactions with AI tools involved sensitive data; its 2025 data put source code at 18.7% of the sensitive data entered. Consumer AI terms may permit training on inputs or retention outside the company's control.
  • Confidentiality and privilege: client data under contract, health information under HIPAA, and attorney-client material can lose protection once shared with a third party.
  • Regulatory: AI use in hiring, promotion and discipline triggers bias-audit rules in New York City, notice rules in Illinois, notice and human-review rules in Colorado from 2027, and the EU AI Act for EU staff, whether or not IT approved the tool.
  • Quality: unreviewed AI output with errors in customer-facing work.
  • Governance gap: the Ponemon/DTEX 2026 study found only 18% of organizations had integrated AI governance into insider-risk programs. See insider threat statistics.

What employers can detect

On managed devices and networks, security tools can usually see which AI sites and apps are visited (web proxies, DNS logs, cloud access security brokers), which browser extensions are installed, and, with data loss prevention, whether sensitive data was pasted or uploaded to an AI service. On personal devices on cellular data, they generally cannot. The employee view is in can my employer see if I use ChatGPT.

How to reduce it

  1. Provide an approved tool with enterprise terms (no training on inputs, retention controls, single sign-on). Banning AI without an alternative drives use to personal accounts.
  2. Write a short AI use policy stating approved tools, prohibited data types and review requirements; see the AI use policy template.
  3. Train on what not to paste; see AI training for employees.
  4. Use data loss prevention rules for AI destinations rather than blanket blocking; see data loss prevention.
  5. Review AI features in existing software before they are enabled.
  6. Keep an inventory of AI tools in use, sanctioned or not, and review it quarterly.

Key points

  • Shadow AI is unsanctioned use of AI tools for work, most often public chatbots, browser extensions and note-takers accessed through personal accounts.
  • It is widespread: 67% of employees using AI on corporate devices used personal accounts (Verizon 2026), and shadow AI was involved in 43% of breaches in IBM's 2026 study.
  • The main risk is data exposure: about 40% of employee AI interactions involve sensitive data (Cyberhaven 2026), with confidentiality, privilege and AI-hiring-law consequences.
  • Employers can see AI site visits, extensions and data uploads on managed devices and networks, not on personal devices on cellular data.
  • Reduce it by providing an approved enterprise tool, a short policy, training, targeted data loss prevention rules and an AI tool inventory; bans without alternatives push use underground.

Frequently asked questions

What is shadow AI?

The use of AI tools at work, such as public chatbots, AI browser extensions or meeting note-takers, without the employer's knowledge or approval, typically through personal accounts. It is the AI equivalent of shadow IT.

Why is shadow AI a risk?

Because employees often paste confidential data into tools whose terms allow retention or training on inputs and which the employer cannot audit. Research in 2026 found about 40% of employee AI interactions involved sensitive data, and IBM found shadow AI involved in 43% of breaches.

How common is shadow AI?

Very. Verizon's 2026 report found 67% of employees using AI services on corporate devices did so through non-corporate accounts, and more than 15% of corporate users had unauthorized AI browser extensions.

Can employers detect shadow AI?

On company devices and networks, usually: web filtering, DNS logs and cloud access security brokers show which AI services are used, and data loss prevention tools can flag sensitive data pasted or uploaded to them. Use on personal devices over cellular data is generally invisible to the employer.

Should companies ban AI tools?

Outright bans tend to increase shadow AI, because employees switch to personal accounts. Most security teams recommend providing an approved enterprise AI tool with data protections, a clear policy on what data may be used, and targeted controls on high-risk data.

What is the difference between shadow AI and shadow IT?

Shadow IT is any technology used without IT approval, such as personal cloud storage or unapproved apps. Shadow AI is the subset involving AI tools, with the added risks that inputs may be retained or used for training and outputs may be inaccurate.
