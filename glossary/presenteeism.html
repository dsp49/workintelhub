Presenteeism

WH The WorkIntel Hub editorial team
September 2, 2026 · 7 min read

Presenteeism is being at work but not fully functioning, usually because of illness, injury or a health condition. The employee is present, attendance records look normal, and output is materially reduced.

It is the counterpart to absenteeism and the more expensive of the two, for a reason that is structural rather than medical: absence is recorded, and presenteeism is not.

Why it costs more than absenteeism

The foundational treatment is Paul Hemp's 2004 Harvard Business Review article, "Presenteeism: At Work — But Out of It", which put the cost to US companies at over $150 billion a year and argued the productivity loss exceeded that from absenteeism by a wide margin.

That figure is now more than twenty years old and is often quoted as though it were current, which it is not. Treat it as establishing the shape of the problem rather than its present size: the research that followed has consistently found presenteeism to be the larger cost, while the specific dollar estimates vary considerably by method.

The mechanism is straightforward. An absent employee produces nothing and everyone knows it, so work is redistributed. A present but impaired employee produces less, makes more errors, and nobody adjusts anything, because the attendance record says they were there. The loss is real and invisible at the same time.

Where the condition is infectious, the arithmetic is worse still, because one person's presenteeism becomes several people's absence the following week.

What causes it

Very little of it is enthusiasm. Most presenteeism is produced by policy.

  • No paid sick leave, or too little. Where a sick day costs money, people work sick. This is the single largest driver.
  • Attendance point systems. A no-fault policy that counts occurrences rather than reasons makes staying home expensive in a different currency. Our page on attendance tracking covers designing one that does not do this.
  • Thin coverage. If nobody can cover, people come in rather than leave colleagues stranded.
  • Visible-hours culture. Where being seen is how contribution is judged, being absent is a risk.
  • Remote work with no boundary. Working from home has removed the threshold that once made staying home a decision. People now work through illnesses they would previously have taken a day for, because logging on is frictionless.

Why monitoring tools tend to reward it

This is the part relevant to anyone measuring productivity, and it is uncomfortable.

Activity monitoring measures presence and input: hours logged, applications open, keyboard and mouse activity. An employee working while unwell scores well on every one of those, because they are present and active. An employee who takes the day off scores zero.

So a system built on activity signals does not merely fail to detect presenteeism, it actively rewards it, and the reward is visible to everyone being measured. Our page on why productivity metrics fail knowledge teams covers the general form of this problem, and the privacy line covers why health signals should not be collected as a fix.

The alternative is not a better sensor. It is measuring outcomes over a long enough window that a week of reduced capability shows up as reduced output rather than as normal attendance.

What reduces it

  1. Paid sick leave that is genuinely usable. Available in policy is not the same as usable in practice. If people do not take it, ask why rather than assuming they are well.
  2. Exclude protected and sick absence from attendance counts. A point system that penalises illness is a presenteeism policy.
  3. Make cover explicit. Most people come in sick out of obligation to colleagues. Naming who covers removes the reason.
  4. Managers who visibly go home when unwell. This does more than any policy document, and costs nothing.
  5. Stop measuring presence. Any metric that rewards being logged on rewards working while sick.

Key points

  • Presenteeism is being present at work but not functioning fully, usually because of illness or a health condition.
  • It costs more than absenteeism, largely because absence is recorded and presenteeism is not.
  • The widely quoted $150 billion figure comes from a 2004 Harvard Business Review article and is not a current estimate.
  • Most presenteeism is produced by policy rather than by dedication: no usable sick leave, attendance point systems, thin cover.
  • Remote work increased it by removing the threshold that once made staying home a decision.
  • Activity monitoring rewards presenteeism, because a person working while unwell scores well on presence and input.
  • The fix is outcome measurement over a long enough window, plus sick leave people can actually use.

Frequently asked questions

What is presenteeism?

Working while unwell or otherwise impaired, so the employee is physically or digitally present but not functioning at full capacity. Attendance records look normal while output, accuracy and judgement are reduced.

Why does presenteeism cost more than absenteeism?

Because it is invisible. An absence is recorded and work gets redistributed. A present but impaired employee produces less and makes more errors while the attendance record shows nothing unusual, so no adjustment is made. Where the illness is infectious, one person's presenteeism also becomes several people's absence later.

How much does presenteeism cost?

The most quoted figure is over $150 billion a year in the US, from a 2004 Harvard Business Review article. It is more than twenty years old and is often repeated as if current. Later research consistently finds presenteeism to be the larger cost than absenteeism, though specific estimates vary widely by method.

What causes presenteeism?

Mostly policy rather than dedication. Insufficient or unpaid sick leave, attendance point systems that count occurrences regardless of reason, thin staffing that leaves nobody to cover, and cultures where being seen is how contribution is judged.

Has remote work made presenteeism worse?

It has removed the threshold. Going into an office while ill required a decision; logging on from bed does not. People now work through conditions they would previously have taken a day for, and it is even less visible than before.

Can software detect presenteeism?

Activity monitoring does the opposite: it rewards it. Someone working while unwell registers as present and active, while someone taking a sick day registers as absent. Any metric built on presence or input signals encourages working while ill.

How do you reduce presenteeism?

Make sick leave paid and genuinely usable, exclude sick and protected absence from attendance counts, name who covers so people are not held by obligation to colleagues, have managers visibly go home when unwell, and stop using presence as a productivity measure.
