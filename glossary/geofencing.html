Geofencing

WH The WorkIntel Hub editorial team
November 20, 2026 · 6 min read

Geofencing is the use of a virtual boundary around a real location, defined by GPS coordinates, Wi-Fi networks or Bluetooth beacons, so that software can trigger an action when a device enters or leaves it, such as allowing an employee to clock in only on site.

In workforce software, geofences are mostly used to stop clock-ins from the parking lot or from home, to log arrival and departure at job sites automatically, and to remind field staff to clock in when they arrive. Most clock-in and time tracking apps now offer it; see clock in clock out apps for how vendors compare.

Geofencing is not the same as continuous GPS tracking. A well-configured geofence checks location only at the moment of clocking in or out, or at the boundary, and records whether the device was inside, not a breadcrumb trail of the employee's day.

Workplace uses

  • Clock-in restrictions: block or flag punches outside the boundary, reducing early clock-ins and buddy clocking. See time theft statistics.
  • Automatic time entries: start and stop job timers on arrival and departure for crews moving between sites.
  • Reminders: prompt workers to clock in or out when they cross the boundary, which reduces missed punches.
  • Job costing: attribute hours to the right site or client.
  • Safety: confirm lone workers arrived and left a remote site.

Accuracy and edge cases

GPS drift indoors and in dense urban areas can place a worker who is inside the building just outside the fence, so most deployments set a radius generous enough to avoid false rejections and allow a flagged punch with a reason rather than a hard block. Large sites need polygons rather than circles. Workers whose first task is off site (picking up materials, a client visit) need an exception path. Missed or rejected punches must still be paid for actual time worked; a geofence that stops someone clocking in does not stop the clock under the FLSA. See time clock rounding rules and the time tracking discrepancy template.

Law and privacy

  • Notice: Connecticut, Delaware and New York require written notice of electronic monitoring, and several states regulate location tracking specifically; New Jersey requires notice before tracking an employee's vehicle with a tracking device. See employee GPS tracking and the GPS tracking policy template.
  • Personal phones: requiring a time clock app on a personal phone can trigger expense reimbursement in California and other states and raises privacy expectations; collect location only at punch time and only while on the clock. See the BYOD policy template.
  • Off-duty tracking: continuous tracking outside working hours is where most privacy complaints and claims arise; a geofence should not run in the background off the clock.
  • Data: keep location records with time records for the payroll retention period, restrict access, and do not repurpose them for unrelated monitoring.
  • Biometrics: geofencing is often combined with face verification at clock-in, which brings biometric privacy laws such as Illinois BIPA into play; see biometric time clock.

Key points

  • Geofencing draws a virtual boundary around a location using GPS, Wi-Fi, Bluetooth beacons or IP addresses and triggers an action, such as allowing clock-in, when a device is inside it.
  • Workplace uses include restricting clock-ins to the site, automatic job timers, punch reminders, job costing and lone-worker safety.
  • GPS is imprecise indoors and in cities, so radii should be generous and out-of-fence punches flagged with a reason rather than hard-blocked; rejected punches do not change pay owed for time worked.
  • A geofence should check location at clock-in and clock-out or at the boundary, not track employees continuously or off the clock.
  • Notice laws, vehicle tracking rules, personal-phone reimbursement and biometric privacy laws can all apply; publish a location policy and limit retention and access.

Frequently asked questions

What is geofencing?

A virtual boundary around a real-world location, set with GPS coordinates, Wi-Fi networks or Bluetooth beacons. Software can then react when a device enters or leaves the area, for example by allowing an employee to clock in only when they are on site.

How does geofencing work for clock-in apps?

The app checks the phone's location, or the network it is connected to, when the employee taps clock in. If the device is inside the approved boundary the punch is accepted; if not, it is blocked or flagged for a manager, depending on settings.

Is geofencing the same as GPS tracking?

No. A geofence typically records only whether the device was inside the boundary at clock-in, clock-out or when crossing it. GPS tracking records a continuous trail of locations. Many apps offer both, and employers should enable only what they need.

Is geofencing employees legal?

Generally yes for on-the-clock use with notice. Connecticut, Delaware and New York require written notice of electronic monitoring, some states regulate vehicle tracking, and California and others may require reimbursing personal phone costs if a phone app is required. Tracking employees off duty is where legal risk concentrates.

How accurate is geofencing?

GPS geofences are accurate to a few meters outdoors but can drift by much more indoors or among tall buildings. Wi-Fi and beacon geofences are more reliable inside buildings. Most employers set a radius of a few hundred feet and allow flagged punches with a reason.

What happens if a geofence stops an employee clocking in?

The employer still owes pay for all time actually worked. A blocked punch should be corrected through a timesheet edit or exception process, not left unpaid. Repeated false rejections usually mean the boundary is too tight.
