Algorithmic Management

WH The WorkIntel Hub editorial team
November 20, 2026 · 6 min read

Algorithmic management is the use of software, often driven by data and AI, to perform functions traditionally done by human managers: assigning work, setting pace, monitoring performance, rating workers and deciding on pay, discipline or dismissal.

The term was coined to describe ride-hailing and delivery platforms, where an app dispatches jobs, sets prices, tracks acceptance rates and deactivates drivers with little human involvement. It now covers a much wider range of workplaces: warehouse rate systems that set picks per hour and flag workers who fall behind, call-center tools that score every call, scheduling software that builds rosters from demand forecasts, and productivity platforms that rank employees by activity.

It sits between two neighboring concepts: workforce analytics, which informs human decisions, and automated employment decision tools, which the law regulates in hiring and promotion.

What it does

  • Allocation: dispatching jobs, routes and tasks; building schedules (see workforce management).
  • Direction: setting targets and pace, such as picks per hour or average handle time.
  • Monitoring: tracking location, activity, keystrokes, idle time and output.
  • Evaluation: rating performance, often from customer ratings or productivity scores.
  • Discipline and pay: automated warnings, deactivation, dynamic pay rates and bonus eligibility.

Effects on workers

Research and worker surveys consistently report higher work intensity, less discretion, reduced trust and difficulty contesting decisions when systems are opaque. Pew Research found in 2023 that 81% of Americans thought AI monitoring would make workers feel inappropriately watched. Quota systems have also been linked to injury risk in warehouses, which led California (AB 701, 2021) and several other states, including Washington, New York and Minnesota, to require warehouse employers to disclose quotas and prohibit quotas that prevent breaks or bathroom use. The productivity effects depend on design: transparent targets that workers understand can improve coordination, while hidden scores tend to produce gaming and turnover. See why output metrics fail.

How it is regulated

  • European Union: the Platform Work Directive (adopted 2024, to be transposed by member states by December 2026) gives platform workers rights to information about automated monitoring and decision systems, a ban on processing certain personal data such as emotional state, human oversight of significant decisions and a right to review. The EU AI Act classifies AI used for recruitment, task allocation, monitoring and evaluation of workers as high-risk.
  • United Kingdom: the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 allows significant automated decisions only with safeguards including human intervention; see UK monitoring law.
  • United States: no general federal law. Warehouse quota laws in several states; New York City's Local Law 144 for hiring and promotion tools; Colorado's SB 26-189, from January 1, 2027, requiring notice, adverse-outcome letters and human review for consequential employment decisions; California's Civil Rights Council regulations on automated decision systems (October 2025); Illinois's amendment to its Human Rights Act on discriminatory AI use; state monitoring notice laws in Connecticut, Delaware and New York. Anti-discrimination law (Title VII, ADA, ADEA) applies to algorithmic decisions as to any others. See AI hiring laws by state.
  • Labor law: in the US, monitoring or algorithmic rules that chill concerted activity can violate the NLRA, and algorithmic systems are a bargaining subject in unionized workplaces.

Using it responsibly

  1. Tell workers what is measured, how scores are computed and what decisions they affect.
  2. Keep a human in the loop for discipline, pay and dismissal; see human in the loop.
  3. Give workers a way to see and contest their data.
  4. Test targets for safety and for disparate impact before rollout and periodically after.
  5. Measure outcomes, not just activity; see the ethical monitoring playbook.

Key points

  • Algorithmic management is software performing management functions: allocating work, setting pace, monitoring, rating and deciding on pay, discipline or dismissal.
  • It began with gig platforms and now covers warehouses, call centers, scheduling and productivity scoring in ordinary employment.
  • Opaque systems are linked to work intensification, low trust and difficulty contesting decisions; warehouse quota laws in California and other states respond to injury risk.
  • The EU Platform Work Directive and AI Act regulate it most directly; the US relies on warehouse quota laws, AI hiring laws, monitoring notice laws and anti-discrimination law.
  • Responsible use means transparency about metrics, human review of significant decisions, a way to contest data, and testing for safety and bias.

Frequently asked questions

What is algorithmic management?

The use of software and data, often AI, to carry out management functions such as assigning tasks, setting work pace, monitoring and rating performance, and making decisions about pay, discipline or deactivation, with limited human involvement.

What are examples of algorithmic management?

Ride-hailing and delivery apps that dispatch jobs and deactivate drivers, warehouse systems that set picks-per-hour quotas and flag slow workers, call-center software that scores every call, automated scheduling tools, and productivity platforms that rank employees by activity.

Is algorithmic management legal?

Yes, in general, but it is increasingly regulated. The EU's Platform Work Directive and AI Act set transparency and human-oversight rules. In the US, warehouse quota laws, NYC Local Law 144, Colorado's SB 26-189 (from 2027), California's automated decision system regulations and state monitoring notice laws apply, and anti-discrimination laws cover algorithmic decisions.

What is the difference between algorithmic management and workforce analytics?

Workforce analytics produces insight for human managers to act on. Algorithmic management acts directly, assigning work or triggering decisions automatically. The same data can support either, depending on whether a person makes the final call.

What are the risks of algorithmic management for workers?

Higher work intensity, less control over how work is done, opaque scoring, difficulty contesting decisions, safety risks from aggressive quotas, and potential bias if the system's data or design disadvantages protected groups.

How can employers use algorithmic management responsibly?

By disclosing what is measured and how, keeping humans responsible for discipline, pay and dismissal, letting workers see and challenge their data, testing targets for safety and disparate impact, and measuring outcomes rather than raw activity.
