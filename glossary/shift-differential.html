Shift Differential

WH The WorkIntel Hub editorial team
September 3, 2026 · 7 min read

Shift differential is additional pay for working a shift people would rather not work: nights, evenings, weekends or holidays. It is usually a percentage of base pay or a flat amount per hour.

No federal law requires it. The rule that does apply, and that catches employers regularly, is what happens to it when the same employee works overtime.

How it is usually structured

MethodExampleSuits
Percentage of base10% extra for nightsMixed pay bands, keeps the premium proportional
Flat rate per hour$2.50 per hour on nightsSimple to explain, easy to budget
Flat amount per shift$20 per weekend shiftShort shifts, or a recruitment incentive

Reported ranges vary by source and industry, and most published figures come from vendor surveys rather than official statistics, so treat them as indicative. Commonly cited: roughly 5 to 15 per cent of base pay overall, with evening premiums at the lower end and overnight premiums higher, and dollar equivalents often in the range of one to five dollars an hour. Healthcare and manufacturing tend to sit above retail.

The percentage method has one practical advantage worth noting: it holds its value as wages rise, where a flat rate set years ago quietly becomes a smaller incentive every year without anyone deciding to reduce it.

No federal law requires it

There is no federal requirement to pay extra for nights, weekends or holidays. The FLSA requires overtime for hours beyond 40 in a workweek, and it is indifferent to which hours of the day or week those were.

Shift differentials exist because the labour market requires them, not because the law does. They are a recruitment and retention instrument, and where a differential is in a collective agreement or an employment contract it becomes contractually owed even though no statute imposes it.

Which means the differential is yours to design, with one significant constraint that is not optional.

The overtime rule that catches employers

This is the reason this entry exists. A shift differential must be included in the regular rate when overtime is calculated.

29 CFR 778.109 defines the regular rate as total remuneration for the workweek, less the statutory exclusions, divided by hours actually worked. 29 U.S.C. 207(e) lists those exclusions, and shift differentials are not among them. So an employee who worked nights and also worked overtime is owed overtime on a rate higher than their base wage.

Worked example

$20 base, 46 hours, $2 per hour night differential on all hours.

Straight time: 46 × $20 = $920
Differential: 46 × $2 = $92
Total remuneration: $1,012
Regular rate: $1,012 ÷ 46 = $22.00
Overtime premium: 0.5 × $22.00 × 6 = $66.00
Total due: $1,078.00

Paying the overtime premium on the $20 base instead would produce $1,072, underpaying by $6 for the week. Small per week, systematic, and it only ever runs one way, which is what makes it the kind of error an investigator finds quickly. Our overtime calculator handles the regular rate properly.

Designing one

  1. Define the qualifying hours precisely. "Nights" is not a definition. A shift starting at or after a stated time, or hours worked between two stated times, is.
  2. Decide whether it applies to the whole shift or only the qualifying hours. Both are workable; ambiguity is not.
  3. Write down how it interacts with overtime, so payroll does not have to infer it.
  4. Review the amount against wages annually if it is a flat rate.
  5. Make sure the timesheet captures which hours qualified, which our timesheet template covers.

Key points

  • Shift differential is extra pay for working nights, evenings, weekends or holidays.
  • No federal law requires it. It exists because the labour market does, though a contract or collective agreement can make it owed.
  • Commonly cited ranges are roughly 5 to 15 per cent of base, but most published figures come from vendor surveys rather than official statistics.
  • It must be included in the regular rate when overtime is calculated, because it is not among the statutory exclusions.
  • Paying overtime on the base wage instead of the regular rate underpays systematically, and only ever in one direction.
  • A flat rate set years ago shrinks in real terms every year without anyone deciding to reduce it.
  • Define the qualifying hours by clock time, not by the word nights.

Frequently asked questions

What is shift differential pay?

Additional pay for working a less desirable shift, typically nights, evenings, weekends or holidays. It is usually expressed as a percentage of base pay, a flat amount per hour, or a flat amount per shift.

Is shift differential required by law?

Not by federal law. The FLSA requires overtime beyond 40 hours in a workweek and is indifferent to which hours of the day those were. Differentials exist because the labour market requires them, though one promised in a contract or collective agreement becomes contractually owed.

How much is a typical shift differential?

Commonly cited figures put it around 5 to 15 per cent of base pay, with evening premiums lower and overnight premiums higher, or roughly one to five dollars an hour. Most published figures come from vendor surveys rather than official statistics, so treat them as indicative rather than authoritative.

Does shift differential count toward overtime?

Yes, and this is where employers most often get caught. The regular rate is total remuneration divided by hours worked, and shift differentials are not among the statutory exclusions, so overtime must be calculated on a rate higher than the base wage.

How do you calculate overtime with a shift differential?

Add the differential to straight-time earnings to get total remuneration, divide by hours actually worked to get the regular rate, then pay the overtime premium on that rate. For $20 base, 46 hours and a $2 differential, the regular rate is $22.00 rather than $20.00.

Should shift differential be a percentage or a flat rate?

A percentage keeps its value as wages rise and stays proportional across pay bands. A flat rate is simpler to explain and budget, but one set several years ago quietly becomes a smaller incentive each year unless someone reviews it.

Does shift differential apply to the whole shift or only the qualifying hours?

Either is workable, and the policy should say which. Ambiguity here is the most common source of pay disputes about differentials, and it is entirely avoidable by defining qualifying hours as clock times rather than as a word like nights.
