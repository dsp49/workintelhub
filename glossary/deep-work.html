Deep Work

WH The WorkIntel Hub editorial team
September 2, 2026 · 8 min read

Deep work is cognitively demanding work done in a state of uninterrupted concentration. Its opposite is shallow work: logistical, low-intensity tasks performed while distracted, which anyone could do and which rarely create much value.

The term was coined by Cal Newport, a computer science professor at Georgetown, in his 2016 book of the same name. It has since become the standard way of naming a problem most organisations have but do not measure: the working day has been broken into pieces too small to think in.

What counts, and what does not

Deep workShallow work
Designing a system or an approachClearing the inbox
Writing something that requires an argumentFormatting a document
Analysing a problem with no known answerCopying figures between systems
Learning a genuinely new skillAttending a status meeting
Hard to replicate, creates new valueEasy to replicate, keeps things moving

Two clarifications, because both are commonly missed. Shallow work is not worthless. Most of it has to happen, and someone doing only deep work would be an unreliable colleague. The distinction is about proportion, not virtue.

And deep work is not simply difficult work or long hours. It is defined by the state, not the effort. Four hours of hard work interrupted every ten minutes is not deep work; it is four hours of restarting.

Why it has become rare

The obstacle is structural rather than personal. Microsoft Work Trend Index reported in June 2025 that knowledge workers are interrupted roughly every two minutes across meetings, email and chat, about 275 times a day, and that 48 per cent of employees describe their work as chaotic and fragmented.

A day arranged that way cannot contain deep work, no matter how disciplined the individual, because the resource deep work consumes is not willpower but uninterrupted duration. Attention takes time to settle into a hard problem, and every interruption restarts that process rather than pausing it.

This is why individual productivity advice tends to fail here. Telling someone to focus harder does not change the number of times they will be interrupted. Changing the meeting schedule does.

The conditions that actually produce it

Four, in order of how much they matter and how rarely they are done.

  • Blocks of at least ninety minutes. Below about an hour, most of the block is spent getting back to where you were. Two or three genuine blocks a week is more than most knowledge workers currently get.
  • A shared agreement about response time. The block only works if not replying is acceptable. An organisation that expects replies within minutes has decided against deep work, whatever its stated values.
  • A protected place in the calendar, defended by the manager. Individually blocked time is the first thing cancelled when something urgent arrives. Team-level protection survives.
  • A defined output. "Focus time" with no stated objective usually becomes catching up on email in a quieter setting.

Our guide to setting focus blocks that survive a busy calendar covers the scheduling mechanics, and improving employee productivity covers removing friction before adding effort.

Measuring it without ruining it

Deep work is unusually easy to destroy by measuring it badly, because the obvious measures all reward the wrong thing.

Activity scores, keyboard and mouse intensity, and application time are the clearest example. A person thinking hard about a difficult problem registers as idle, so a system built on those signals penalises exactly the work you are trying to protect. Our page on why productivity metrics fail knowledge teams covers what happens next.

What can be measured usefully is the calendar, which is the input rather than the output. How many uninterrupted blocks of ninety minutes or more does each person have this week? That number is objective, easy to obtain, and directly actionable, and in most teams the answer for a typical week is zero or one.

Frequently asked questions

What is deep work?

Deep work is professional activity performed in a state of distraction-free concentration that pushes cognitive capability to its limit. It produces work that is hard to replicate and creates new value, as opposed to shallow work, which is logistical, performed while distracted, and easy for anyone to do.

Who came up with the term deep work?

Cal Newport, a computer science professor at Georgetown University, in his 2016 book Deep Work: Rules for Focused Success in a Distracted World. The term named a distinction that already existed rather than describing a new practice.

What is the difference between deep work and shallow work?

Deep work is cognitively demanding and done without distraction: designing something, analysing a problem with no known answer, writing something that requires an argument. Shallow work is logistical and can be done while distracted: clearing an inbox, formatting a document, attending a status meeting. Both have to happen; the question is the proportion.

How long should a deep work block be?

At least ninety minutes. Below about an hour, a disproportionate share of the block is spent recovering the context lost since the last session. Two or three genuine blocks a week is more than most knowledge workers currently manage.

Is deep work the same as flow?

They overlap but are not the same. Flow describes a psychological state of absorbed engagement that can occur in many activities, including ones that are not cognitively demanding. Deep work describes a working practice defined by concentration on difficult professional work, and it can be productive without ever feeling effortless.

Why is deep work so difficult now?

Because the working day has been fragmented. Microsoft's 2025 research found knowledge workers interrupted roughly every two minutes across meetings, email and chat, with 48 per cent describing their work as chaotic. The resource deep work needs is uninterrupted duration, and a day arranged that way does not contain any.

How do you measure deep work?

Measure the calendar rather than the person. Count how many uninterrupted blocks of ninety minutes or more each person has in a week. Activity monitoring is actively counterproductive here, because someone thinking hard registers as idle and the system penalises the work you were trying to protect.
