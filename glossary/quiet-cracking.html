Quiet Cracking

WH The WorkIntel Hub editorial team
September 2, 2026 · 7 min read

Quiet cracking describes a persistent, low-level unhappiness at work that gradually erodes engagement and performance while the employee stays put. Unlike quitting, it produces no obvious event, which is why it tends to go unnoticed until output has already fallen.

The term came out of research published by the learning platform TalentLMS in 2025 and was picked up widely through that year. It describes something specific to a cooling labour market: people who would have left in better conditions, staying and disengaging instead.

What the research actually found

The TalentLMS survey covered 1,000 US employees across industries in March 2025. The headline most coverage repeated was that 20 per cent experience quiet cracking frequently or constantly. The fuller finding is more useful.

FindingFigure
Experience quiet cracking at some level54%
Frequently or constantly20%
Occasionally34%
Feel secure in their current role82%
Feel secure about their future with the company62%
Of those affected, say managers do not listen to their concerns47% (vs 20% overall)
Of those affected, feel valued and recognised26% (vs 80% overall)

Two of these are worth pausing on. The gap between feeling secure now (82 per cent) and secure about the future (62 per cent) is where the condition lives: not fear of imminent dismissal, but an absence of visible direction.

And the recognition figure, 26 per cent against 80 per cent, is the widest gap in the data. It is also the cheapest to act on.

How it differs from quiet quitting and burnout

Quiet quittingBurnoutQuiet cracking
DriverA deliberate decision to stop over-contributingSustained overload and exhaustionStagnation, and feeling unheard
EffortHeld deliberately at the job descriptionCollapses despite effortDrifts down without a decision being made
AwarenessThe employee knows and often intends itUsually recognised eventuallyFrequently unrecognised by anyone
Visible in metricsSometimesUsually, eventuallyRarely, until late

The distinction that matters operationally is the third row. Quiet quitting is a choice, and a person who has made it can usually tell you so. Burnout is a state most people eventually name. Quiet cracking is characterised by nobody naming anything, which is precisely why it does not surface in a one-to-one where the manager asks whether everything is fine.

Why it appeared when it did

The term arrived alongside a labour market in which fewer people were moving jobs, sometimes called the Big Stay. That matters, because the mechanism is not new: what changed is that the usual escape route closed.

In a hiring market, an employee who feels stuck leaves, and the organisation registers the problem as turnover. When leaving feels risky, the same person stays, and the organisation registers nothing at all. The cost moves from a visible line, replacement hiring, to an invisible one.

This fits the wider picture. Gallup reported in 2026 that global engagement declined for a second consecutive year, with the sharpest fall among managers. And the American Psychological Association's 2024 Work in America survey, conducted among 2,027 employed US adults, found 43 per cent reporting lower psychological safety at work.

What actually addresses it

The research points at three levers, and they are not the ones organisations usually reach for.

  • Training and visible development. Employees who had received no training in the past year were markedly more likely to feel insecure, and those experiencing quiet cracking were considerably less likely to have been trained. Development is the clearest available signal that someone has a future.
  • Managers who demonstrably act on what they hear. 47 per cent of affected employees said managers do not listen to their concerns. Listening here means a visible response, not a survey.
  • Specific recognition. The largest gap in the data. Recognition that names a particular piece of work carries a signal that a general thank-you does not.

What does not address it is monitoring. A person who is present, compliant and quietly disengaging looks entirely normal in activity data, and adding surveillance to a group already reporting that nobody listens is likely to accelerate the problem. Frequently asked questions

What is quiet cracking?

A persistent feeling of unhappiness at work that gradually erodes motivation and performance while the employee remains in the job. It produces no resignation and no obvious incident, so it usually goes unnoticed until output has already declined.

Where did the term quiet cracking come from?

From research published by the learning platform TalentLMS in 2025, based on a survey of 1,000 US employees conducted in March of that year. Trade and business media picked it up through 2025, often alongside coverage of a cooling labour market.

How common is quiet cracking?

In the TalentLMS survey, 54 per cent of employees reported experiencing it at some level: 20 per cent frequently or constantly, and 34 per cent occasionally. Most coverage quotes only the 20 per cent figure.

How is quiet cracking different from quiet quitting?

Quiet quitting is a deliberate decision to limit effort to the job description, and the person is usually aware of it. Quiet cracking involves no decision at all: engagement drifts downward without anyone naming it, which is why it rarely surfaces in a routine conversation.

Is quiet cracking the same as burnout?

No. Burnout is driven by sustained overload and exhaustion, and effort collapses despite the person trying. Quiet cracking is driven by stagnation and feeling unheard, and effort declines gradually without the person reaching a breaking point.

What causes quiet cracking?

The research points at three things: no visible development or training, managers who do not act on concerns raised, and an absence of specific recognition. Underlying all three is uncertainty about whether the person has a future at the organisation.

Can monitoring software detect quiet cracking?

No, and attempting it tends to make matters worse. Someone quietly disengaging is present, compliant and unremarkable in activity data. Adding surveillance to a group already reporting that nobody listens to them addresses none of the causes and reinforces one of them.
