Overtime Statistics 2026: Hours, Pay, Violations and Health

P Pavan
November 13, 2026 · 11 min read

About one in five American workers puts in more than 40 hours in a typical week, but far fewer are paid overtime for it. The Bureau of Labor Statistics' 2025 averages show 21.6% of people at work in non-farm jobs worked 41 or more hours, and 5.4% worked 60 or more; analysis for Congress estimates that of roughly 98 million workers eligible for overtime pay under the Fair Labor Standards Act, only 10 to 11 million actually work paid overtime in a year. Unpaid overtime is the most common wage violation the Department of Labor finds: it recovered $146 million in overtime back wages for 110,551 workers in fiscal 2025, the most in years.

This page gathers the current figures on overtime hours, who works them, what the law requires and how often it is broken, the new federal tax deduction for overtime pay, and the research on long hours and health. Each figure carries its source and date. For the rules, see overtime laws by state and mandatory overtime laws; for the math, the overtime calculator.

Headline figures

  • 21.6% of non-farm workers worked more than 40 hours a week in 2025; 5.4% worked 60 or more (BLS Current Population Survey).
  • 4.1 hours: average weekly overtime for manufacturing production workers, October 2026, up from 3.7 a year earlier.
  • $146.4 million: overtime back wages recovered by the Wage and Hour Division for 110,551 workers in fiscal 2025.
  • About 10 to 11 million of roughly 98 million overtime-eligible workers actually work paid overtime (Yale Budget Lab, via the Congressional Research Service).
  • 29 million+ 2025 tax returns claimed the new overtime deduction, averaging more than $3,100 (Treasury, June 2026).
  • 61% higher injury hazard in jobs with overtime schedules (Dembe and colleagues, 2005).
  • 42.9 hours: average week for US full-time employees in 2024, down from 44.1 in 2019 (Gallup).

Average hours were 38.2 for all workers and 41.8 for those who usually work full time. Gallup's survey data puts full-time employees at 42.9 hours a week in 2024, down from 44.1 in 2019, with the largest drop among workers under 35 (43.6 to 41.8); the often-quoted 47-hour figure is from 2014. The working hours statistics page has the longer series.

Manufacturing overtime hours

  • Production and nonsupervisory employees: 4.1 hours a week in October 2026 (preliminary), 4.0 in September, 4.1 in August, against 3.7 in October 2025 (BLS Employment Situation, table B-7, November 6, 2026).
  • All manufacturing employees: 3.1 hours in October 2026, up 0.1; the average manufacturing workweek was 40.5 hours and the all-private workweek 34.4. The two overtime series differ by coverage; cite the one you mean.
  • Annual averages for production workers: 3.8 hours in 2025, 3.6 in 2023 and 2024, against a peak of 4.6 in 2018. Transportation equipment manufacturing averaged the most, 5.3 hours, and machinery the least, 2.5 (BLS, March 31, 2026).

Manufacturing overtime is a leading economic indicator: employers add hours before they add people, and cut hours before they cut people.

Who actually gets overtime pay

  • The Yale Budget Lab estimated about 98 million workers were eligible for FLSA overtime in 2023 (82 million hourly, 16 million salaried non-exempt), of whom only about 10 to 11 million worked FLSA overtime in a year (as summarized by the Congressional Research Service, July 2026).
  • About 8% of hourly workers and 4% of salaried workers receive overtime pay regularly.
  • The gap between "works more than 40 hours" (about 21% of workers) and "receives overtime pay" (about 8% of hourly workers) is explained mainly by exempt salaried employees, who work long hours without overtime pay by law, and partly by unpaid overtime.

Overtime accounts for about four-fifths of FLSA back wages. In fiscal 2025 food services had the most violations (4,088, $42 million) and health care the most money ($53 million across 2,370 violations). Common causes are off-the-clock work, misclassification of employees as exempt or as contractors, failure to include bonuses and differentials in the regular rate, and rounding or automatic meal deductions that shave time; see time clock rounding rules and wage theft statistics. Private litigation recovers far more than the Department of Labor; FLSA collective actions are the most common federal employment class actions.

The salary threshold

  • The federal exempt salary threshold is $684 a week ($35,568 a year), set in 2019. The 2024 rule that would have raised it to $844 in July 2024 and $1,128 ($58,656) in January 2025 was vacated by a federal court in Texas in November 2024 and formally rescinded on May 15, 2026.
  • The Department of Labor estimated the 2024 rule would have extended overtime protection to about 4 million salaried workers in its first year (figure not re-checked against the Federal Register).
  • The Economic Policy Institute found the share of salaried workers below the federal threshold fell from 65% in 1975 to 11% in 2013, the long erosion that the 2016, 2019 and 2024 rules tried to reverse.
  • States set higher thresholds; see the 2027 exempt threshold guide.

The no-tax-on-overtime deduction

  • For tax years 2025 to 2028, workers may deduct the premium half of FLSA overtime pay, up to $12,500 ($25,000 joint), phasing out above $150,000 of income ($300,000 joint).
  • Treasury reported in June 2026 that more than 29 million 2025 returns claimed it, about 20% of returns received by May 8, 2026, with an average deduction of more than $3,100.
  • The Joint Committee on Taxation scored the deduction at $89.6 billion over fiscal 2025 to 2029, about $22 to 23 billion a year.
  • The Tax Policy Center estimated only about 9% of households benefit.
  • From 2026, Form W-2 box 12 code TT reports qualified overtime compensation. The no tax on overtime calculator estimates the deduction.

Overtime, injuries and health

  • In a study of 110,236 job records from the National Longitudinal Survey of Youth, Dembe and colleagues found jobs with overtime schedules had a 61% higher injury hazard than those without, working 12 or more hours a day a 37% higher hazard, and 60 or more hours a week a 23% higher hazard, after controlling for age, gender, occupation and industry (Occupational and Environmental Medicine, 2005).
  • A WHO and ILO analysis estimated that working 55 or more hours a week is associated with a 35% higher risk of stroke and 17% higher risk of death from ischemic heart disease compared with 35 to 40 hours (2021).
  • Gallup and other burnout research consistently links long hours and unpredictable overtime to burnout; see employee burnout statistics.

Nurses and mandatory overtime

  • A JAMA Network Open study of 70 US hospitals (2019 to 2022, published April 2, 2025) found hospitals averaged 63.6% above safe overtime-hour thresholds, and overtime above the threshold was associated with a 2.09% increase in pressure ulcers (6.44% for agency nurse hours above threshold).
  • In a 2025 survey of 1,000 US registered nurses by Joyce University (not a representative sample), 74% said they had worked mandatory overtime three or more times in the past month.
  • Eighteen states restrict mandatory overtime for nurses; the American Nurses Association told a House subcommittee in May 2026 that excessive mandatory overtime is a main driver of burnout and attrition. See nurse burnout statistics and the state list in mandatory overtime laws.

Key takeaways

  • 21.6% of US non-farm workers worked more than 40 hours a week in 2025 and 5.4% worked 60 or more (BLS); full-time employees averaged 42.9 hours in 2024, down from 44.1 in 2019 (Gallup).
  • Manufacturing production workers averaged 4.1 hours of overtime a week in October 2026, up from 3.7 a year earlier; the 2025 average was 3.8, below the 2018 peak of 4.6.
  • Of roughly 98 million FLSA overtime-eligible workers, only 10 to 11 million work paid overtime in a year; about 8% of hourly workers receive overtime pay regularly.
  • The Wage and Hour Division recovered $146.4 million in overtime back wages for 110,551 workers in fiscal 2025, about four-fifths of all FLSA back wages; food service had the most violations and health care the most money.
  • The federal exempt threshold remains $684 a week after the 2024 rule was vacated and rescinded in May 2026.
  • More than 29 million 2025 returns claimed the new overtime deduction, averaging over $3,100; the Joint Committee on Taxation scores it at $89.6 billion over five years.
  • Overtime schedules carry a 61% higher injury hazard, and 55-hour weeks are linked to higher stroke and heart disease risk; hospitals average well above safe nurse overtime thresholds.

Frequently asked questions

What percentage of Americans work overtime?

About 21.6% of people at work in non-farm jobs worked more than 40 hours a week on average in 2025, according to the BLS Current Population Survey: 7.9% worked 41 to 48 hours, 8.2% worked 49 to 59, and 5.4% worked 60 or more. Many of them are exempt salaried employees who are not paid overtime.

How many workers get paid overtime?

Analysis summarized by the Congressional Research Service estimates that about 10 to 11 million of roughly 98 million overtime-eligible workers work paid overtime in a year, with about 8% of hourly and 4% of salaried non-exempt workers receiving it regularly.

What is the average overtime in manufacturing?

Production and nonsupervisory manufacturing employees averaged 4.1 hours of overtime a week in October 2026, up from 3.7 in October 2025. The 2025 annual average was 3.8 hours, with transportation equipment at 5.3 and machinery at 2.5. The all-employee manufacturing series was 3.1 hours.

How much unpaid overtime does the Department of Labor recover?

In fiscal 2025 the Wage and Hour Division recovered $146.4 million in FLSA overtime back wages for 110,551 workers, up from $127.0 million for 101,043 workers in fiscal 2024. Total back wages under all laws it enforces were $259 million for 176,957 workers.

How many people claimed the no tax on overtime deduction?

More than 29 million 2025 tax returns claimed it, about 20% of returns received by May 8, 2026, with an average deduction of more than $3,100, according to Treasury. The deduction covers the premium half of overtime pay, up to $12,500 ($25,000 joint), for 2025 to 2028.

Is overtime bad for your health?

Long hours carry measurable risk. Dembe and colleagues found a 61% higher injury hazard in jobs with overtime schedules, 37% for 12-hour days and 23% for 60-hour weeks. The WHO and ILO linked 55-hour weeks to a 35% higher stroke risk. For nurses, overtime above safe thresholds is associated with more patient harm.

Do Americans work more hours than other countries?

More than most rich countries. OECD data for 2025 show about 1,800 hours a year per US worker, above the OECD average of 1,736 and well above Germany (1,332), the UK (1,533) and Japan (1,598), but below Mexico (2,205) and South Korea (1,833).

What is the overtime salary threshold in 2026?

$684 a week, or $35,568 a year, under federal law, with $107,432 for highly compensated employees. The 2024 rule that would have raised it to $58,656 was vacated in November 2024 and rescinded in May 2026. Several states set higher thresholds.
