Attendance Tracking Methods Compared: Kiosk, Mobile, Biometric, Web

P Pavan
November 26, 2026 · 9 min read

There are five ways to record attendance: a shared kiosk, a phone app, a wall clock, a web or desktop punch, and automatic capture from a work computer. Each answers the same question, who worked and when, with a different trade-off between cost, how well it confirms the right person punched, and the legal obligations it brings. A biometric option sits across two of them, as a wall clock or a kiosk that reads a face or fingerprint.

Choose the method before the tool. Once you know how people will punch, the shortlist of software shrinks quickly. This page compares the methods side by side, explains where each one fails, and ends with a chooser that recommends a method from your workforce, number of sites, budget and state.

The short answer

Shift staff at a fixed site: a shared tablet kiosk with a PIN and photo. It costs the price of a tablet and deters buddy punching.

Field and multi-site staff: a phone app with a GPS stamp or geofence, with a written policy for personal phones.

Office and remote staff: a web punch, or automatic capture from the computer if you accept that it is a form of monitoring.

Biometric clocks are the strongest identity check and carry the most obligations; in Illinois, get written consent before the first scan.

Shared kiosk

A kiosk is a tablet at the entrance running the attendance app, where each employee punches with a PIN, a QR code, a photo or a face scan. It does most of what a wall clock does for the price of a tablet, and it can be moved. With photo capture on, a manager can check that the face matches the name, which deters most casual buddy punching without any biometric data. Face recognition goes further but makes the kiosk a biometric system. Kiosks suit any fixed site where people arrive at the same door. They need a stand, power, a network and a plan for when the tablet goes missing.

Mobile app with GPS or geofence

A phone app lets people punch wherever the work is, which is the only workable option for field crews, home care, drivers and staff who move between sites. On its own, a mobile login is easy to share. A GPS stamp records where each punch happened, and a geofence accepts the punch only inside a boundary around the site. Our geofencing attendance guide covers the settings and the notice laws. Two rules keep it out of trouble: location only during working hours, ideally only at the punch, and an alternative for anyone who will not use a personal phone.

Biometric wall clocks and kiosks

A fingerprint, hand, face or iris clock identifies the person rather than a credential, which is why it is the strongest defense against buddy punching. It is also the method with the most law attached. Illinois' Biometric Information Privacy Act requires written notice of the purpose and retention period, a written release before collection and a published destruction schedule, and is enforced by private lawsuit with damages of $1,000 per negligent and $5,000 per intentional violation. Texas and Washington require notice and consent too. Fingerprint readers also struggle with worn or dirty fingers in manual trades, and some employees cannot enroll or will refuse, so a non-biometric fallback is needed in practice. The biometric time clock entry covers the types and the consent elements. A badge clock is not biometric, which is why our RFID tracking page suggests considering badges first.

Web and desktop punches

A web punch is a clock-in button in a browser or desktop app. It costs nothing beyond the software and suits staff who start the day at their own computer. It is the weakest identity check, since a login can be shared, and the easiest to forget. IP restriction, which accepts punches only from the office network, helps on site and breaks the moment someone works from home; see how to clock in remotely for the options there.

Automatic capture from the computer

Some tools record attendance with no punch at all: a desktop agent logs when the employee signs in and out, late starts against the shift, and idle and break time. It removes the friction of clocking in for knowledge workers and cannot be buddy punched for desk work. It is also a form of employee monitoring, so a written policy and notice apply; Connecticut, Delaware, New York and Maine have monitoring notice laws, set out in our monitoring laws by state guide. It does not suit a shop floor or anyone without a work computer.

Running two methods at once

Most organizations above a few dozen people end up with two methods: a kiosk at the site and a phone app for the people who are rarely there, or a web punch for office staff and a kiosk for the warehouse. That is normal, and it works if three things hold. First, one account: the kiosk, the app and the web punch should feed the same timesheet, so payroll sees one record per person rather than two exports to reconcile. Second, one policy: state which method each group uses, what each one records, and what happens when someone punches the wrong way. Third, the same rules everywhere: overtime, breaks and rounding should not change because a person clocked in on a phone instead of a tablet. When you compare tools, check that every method you need sits on the same plan, because kiosks, geofencing and photo capture are often on different tiers.

Which method fits your workforce

A starting point, not legal advice. Biometric obligations apply wherever you collect biometrics, and several states require notice of electronic monitoring; check the rules for each state you employ in.

Whatever the method, the records are the same

For non-exempt employees, the Fair Labor Standards Act requires accurate hours for each workday and workweek, with payroll records kept three years and the time cards they came from two. Any method can produce those. The differences are in what else it records and what has to be disclosed. Two rules apply to every method: rounding is lawful only where it is neutral over time (covered in time clock rounding rules), and break records matter in the states that mandate meal breaks (see meal break laws by state).

Key takeaways

  • There are five attendance methods: shared kiosk, mobile app, wall clock, web or desktop punch, and automatic capture from the computer.
  • A tablet kiosk with PIN and photo is the default for shift staff at a fixed site; photo capture deters buddy punching without biometric data.
  • Mobile apps with a GPS stamp or geofence suit field and multi-site staff, with location only during working hours and an alternative for personal phones.
  • Biometric clocks are the strongest identity check and carry written notice and consent duties under Illinois BIPA, with Texas and Washington rules too.
  • Web punches are cheapest and weakest; automatic capture removes the clock-in but is monitoring, so notice applies.
  • Choose the method before the tool. Most organizations above a few dozen people run two methods.

Frequently asked questions

What are the main methods of attendance tracking?

Shared kiosks, usually a tablet with PIN, photo or face scan; mobile apps with GPS or geofencing; hardware wall clocks with badge, PIN, fingerprint or face; web or desktop punches; and automatic capture from a work computer.

Which attendance method is best for preventing buddy punching?

A biometric clock, because the credential is the person. A kiosk with photo capture and a mobile app with a GPS stamp or geofence are strong alternatives with fewer legal obligations. PINs, badges and web logins alone cannot prevent it.

Is a kiosk or a mobile app better for attendance?

A kiosk for staff who arrive at the same site door; a mobile app for staff who work in the field or across sites. Many employers use both, with the kiosk on site and the app for everyone else.

Do I need consent for a biometric time clock?

In Illinois, yes: BIPA requires written notice and a written release before the first scan, plus a published retention schedule. Texas and Washington require notice and consent. Elsewhere it is still good practice, with a non-biometric fallback for people who cannot enroll.

What is the cheapest way to track attendance?

A web punch or a mobile app costs nothing beyond the software, and several attendance tools have free plans. A kiosk adds the price of a tablet per site, and a wall clock adds hardware.

Is automatic attendance tracking legal?

Generally yes on company computers with notice. It is a form of electronic monitoring, so Connecticut, Delaware, New York and Maine notice laws apply, and a written policy is good practice everywhere.
