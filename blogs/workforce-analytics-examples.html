Workforce Analytics Examples: 10 Questions the Data Answers

P Pavan
November 26, 2026 · 8 min read

Good workforce analytics examples start with a decision, not a dashboard: is turnover concentrated under one manager, can this team absorb the next project, is pay equitable. Each of the ten below gives the question, the data needed, the metric to compute and the decision the answer should change.

Most of them can be answered from data an organization already holds, in a spreadsheet, before any platform is bought. The formulas follow our HR metrics guide, which explains the definitional choices that make one company's number incomparable with another's. Every example is aggregated to a team, a cohort or a process. That is what keeps it analytics rather than monitoring.

Examples 1 to 4: turnover and hiring

1. Is turnover concentrated in one team or under one manager?

Data: every exit from the HRIS with date, reason (voluntary or involuntary), manager, team, location and tenure, plus monthly headcount. Metric: turnover rate (leavers divided by average headcount, times 100), split into voluntary and involuntary, and regretted versus non-regretted, where the manager records at exit whether they would rehire the person. Cut it by manager first. Decision: where to put management support, coaching or a closer look. The company-wide rate hides the team with the problem, and turnover by manager is usually the most concentrated pattern in the data.

2. Are we losing people in their first year?

Data: hire and exit dates by cohort, the hiring manager and the onboarding owner. Metric: first-year turnover, the share of hires from twelve or more months ago who left within twelve months. Use the cohort from a year back, not this year's hires. Decision: high first-year turnover is a selection, expectation-setting or onboarding problem rather than a retention problem, so the fix sits in the job description, the interview loop or the onboarding checklist, not in a retention bonus.

3. Which hiring sources produce people who stay?

Data: source of every hire from the applicant tracking system, external and internal recruiting costs, and later exits. Metric: cost per hire and first-year turnover side by side, by source: agency, job board, referral, internal move. Decision: where next year's recruiting budget goes. Referrals are often cheapest and stay longest and agencies the reverse, but the point of tracking is to find out whether that holds in your organization.

4. Where does hiring stall?

Data: timestamps for each ATS stage, from requisition through screening, interviews and offer. Metric: median days in each stage, and time to fill from requisition to acceptance. SHRM's 2025 benchmarking put the median time to fill at 44 days for non-executive roles, as our HR metrics guide records; compare your own stages over time before comparing levels. Decision: which step to fix. A long gap between final interview and offer is an approvals problem; a long screening stage is a recruiter capacity problem.

Examples 5 to 8: capacity, meetings, burnout and absence

5. Can this team absorb the next project?

Data: contracted hours, booked leave and public holidays, the support or on-call load, and recurring commitments such as maintenance and meetings. Metric: available capacity for the period, after those deductions, against hours already committed. Decision: take the project on, cut scope or defer something, or make the case for a hire. Our team capacity planning guide works through the arithmetic, and the capacity reporting guide shows how to put the answer to leadership.

6. Are meetings crowding out focused work?

Data: calendar metadata: number, length and attendee count of meetings, with no content. Microsoft Viva Insights produces this for Microsoft 365 organizations at aggregate, above privacy thresholds. Metric: meeting hours as a share of the working week by team, and how that share has moved over the last two quarters. Decision: a meeting policy, a no-meeting block, or a change to a team's recurring rituals. Report it for the team, never as "this person attended 14 hours of meetings".

7. Which teams show burnout risk?

Data: after-hours and weekend work from time records, calendar or activity data, overtime hours, and leave balances. Metric: the share of a team's working time outside core hours, sustained over several weeks, alongside leave balances that keep growing because no one takes time off. Decision: a workload change and a conversation. Our guide to spotting burnout in workload data sets out five patterns and the rule that matters: the data indicates risk, it does not diagnose anyone, and the response is never a performance process.

8. Is absence rising, and where?

Data: unplanned absence by day, with occurrences counted separately from days, and the team and manager. Metric: absence rate (unplanned days absent divided by headcount times working days, times 100), and occurrences by team and day of week. The Bureau of Labor Statistics reported a 3.2 percent absence rate and a 1.7 percent lost worktime rate for full-time workers in 2025. Decision: where to look at scheduling, workload or manager support. Rising absence often precedes rising turnover on the same team; our absenteeism guide covers the pattern measures and the absences that cannot be counted against anyone.

Examples 9 and 10: pay and structure

9. Is pay equitable?

Data: base pay and total compensation, grade, role, location, tenure, performance rating and protected attributes. Metric: the adjusted pay gap: the difference in pay by gender or race that remains after controlling for the legitimate factors. The raw gap tells you about representation; the adjusted gap tells you about pay decisions. Decision: individual adjustments, band changes, and a review of how offers and raises are set. Several states now require employers to analyze and in some cases report pay data; our pay transparency laws guide lists them. Small groups need minimum cell sizes, or the analysis identifies people.

10. Is the structure too deep or too narrow?

Data: reporting lines and roles from the HRIS, plus cost. Metric: span of control (direct reports per manager) and the number of layers between the front line and the top, by function. Decision: whether to restructure, merge teams or leave things alone. Very narrow spans add cost and slow decisions; very wide ones leave people without a manager who has time for them. Orgvue and ChartHop model these scenarios; a restructure in Europe also brings consultation duties.

How to run any of these

  1. Write the decision first. "If first-year turnover in sales is above last year's, we change the interview loop." A finding with no decision attached is a known problem, expensively documented.
  2. Agree the definition. Average headcount or monthly average, whether temps count, whether transfers are separations. Write it down so next quarter's number is comparable.
  3. Aggregate to the smallest group that is still safe. A team of five is often identifiable. Set a minimum group size and report the next level up below it.
  4. Compare against yourself. Your own trend and the spread between your own teams say more than a cross-industry benchmark.
  5. Pair measures that pull against each other. Capacity with burnout signals; cost per hire with first-year turnover. One headline number can be improved at the expense of something unmeasured.
  6. Show it to the people it describes. If the analysis could not be shown to the team without difficulty, it has crossed from analytics into monitoring.

When a spreadsheet stops being enough

Examples 1 to 4 and 8 to 10 run on HR-system exports and work in a spreadsheet or Power BI for most organizations under a few hundred people. Examples 5 to 7 need time, calendar or activity data, which is where activity-side and collaboration tools come in, along with the monitoring policy and the notice several states require before activity data is collected.

When the data lives in several systems that disagree, or the questions multiply, a platform earns its price. If you are unsure which side your questions sit on, the workforce analytics vs people analytics comparison helps, and our explainer on workforce intelligence and monitoring covers where examples 6 and 7 can go wrong.

Key takeaways

  • Start every workforce analytics example with the decision it should change; a dashboard with no decision attached becomes surveillance by default.
  • Turnover by manager, first-year turnover and source-of-hire quality answer the most common people questions from HRIS and ATS data alone.
  • Capacity, meeting load and burnout risk need time, calendar or activity data, aggregated to the team.
  • Burnout signals in workload data indicate risk, not a diagnosis; the response is a workload change and a conversation.
  • Pay equity analysis uses the adjusted gap after controlling for role, grade, location, tenure and performance, with minimum cell sizes.
  • Compare against your own trend and between your own teams before reaching for a benchmark.

Frequently asked questions

What is an example of workforce analytics?

Calculating turnover by manager to find where voluntary exits concentrate is the most common example. Others include first-year turnover by hiring source, a team's available capacity against committed work, meeting hours as a share of the week, and span of control by function.

What data does workforce analytics use?

HR-system data (exits, hires, pay, reporting lines), applicant tracking data, time and attendance, absence records, calendar and collaboration metadata, and in some deployments activity data from work devices. Each example needs only a slice of it.

What are the most useful workforce metrics?

Turnover rate split by voluntary and regretted, first-year turnover, cost per hire, time to fill, absence rate, available capacity, meeting share of the week and span of control. Each is useful only when cut by team or cohort and tied to a decision.

Can workforce analytics predict turnover?

Platforms such as Visier and One Model produce attrition risk scores, which are probabilities for populations. They are a prompt to look at a team and talk to people, never a basis for a decision about an individual.

Do I need software for workforce analytics?

Not to start. Most HR-record examples run on exports in a spreadsheet or Power BI. Software pays when data sits in several systems, definitions need governing across a large organization, or the questions need activity or planning data.

Is workforce analytics legal?

Yes, within the rules that follow the data: privacy law, pay transparency laws, AI-in-employment rules for models that affect decisions, and monitoring notice laws for activity data attributed to individuals. Aggregating to teams with minimum group sizes keeps most analysis on safe ground.
