Employee Monitoring Laws in the EU: GDPR and Works Councils

P Pavan
November 20, 2026 · 13 min read

Employee monitoring is lawful in the European Union, but every element of it is regulated. Monitoring data is personal data under the General Data Protection Regulation, so an employer needs a lawful basis (almost never consent), must tell employees in advance, must keep the monitoring proportionate to a specific purpose, usually needs a data protection impact assessment, and must respect national labor law, which in Germany, the Netherlands, Italy and elsewhere gives works councils or unions a say before any monitoring system is switched on. Regulators enforce this: France's CNIL made employee surveillance one of its top sanction themes in 2025, and the CMS Enforcement Tracker counted 191 GDPR fines involving employee data, totaling more than €360 million, by May 2026.

This guide, for US and UK employers with EU staff and for EU employees, covers the GDPR framework, the European Court of Human Rights tests, the main national rules country by country, works council rights, the AI Act ban on workplace emotion recognition and its high-risk rules, the Platform Work Directive, the leading fines, and a compliance checklist. For the UK, which now has its own regime, see employee monitoring laws in the UK; for the US, laws by state.

The GDPR framework

  • Lawful basis (Article 6). Consent is rarely valid for employees because of the imbalance of power between employer and worker, as the Article 29 Working Party's Opinion 2/2017 on data processing at work and the EDPB's consent guidelines both say. Employers rely on legitimate interests (6(1)(f)), with a documented balancing test, or on legal obligation or contract.
  • Special category data (Article 9). Biometrics used for identification, health data inferred from monitoring and trade union membership need an additional condition.
  • Transparency (Articles 12 to 14). Employees must be told what is monitored, why, how, by whom and for how long, before monitoring starts. The EDPB chose transparency as its 2026 coordinated enforcement topic.
  • Data protection impact assessment (Article 35). Required for high-risk processing; regulators list systematic monitoring of employees, who are treated as vulnerable data subjects, as a standard trigger.
  • Minimization and storage limitation (Article 5). Collect only what the purpose needs and keep it no longer than necessary.
  • Employment-specific national rules (Article 88). Member states may adopt more specific rules for the employment context, which must include safeguards for dignity, legitimate interests and fundamental rights, "with particular regard to transparency" and "monitoring systems at the workplace." In C-34/21 (30 March 2023) the Court of Justice held that a national rule that merely restates the GDPR's general principles does not qualify under Article 88, which has unsettled Germany's main employee data provision.

Opinion 2/2017 remains the most detailed EU-level guidance. Its core message is that monitoring must be proportionate and transparent, that blanket monitoring of communications is disproportionate, and that less intrusive alternatives (blocking sites rather than reading traffic, for example) should be preferred.

Bărbulescu and López Ribalda: the human rights tests

Article 8 of the European Convention on Human Rights protects private life and correspondence, including at work. In Bărbulescu v. Romania (Grand Chamber, 5 September 2017) the European Court of Human Rights found a violation where an employer monitored an employee's messenger account without prior notice, and set out the questions courts ask: was the employee notified in advance of the possibility and extent of monitoring; how intrusive was it; was there a legitimate reason; could less intrusive means have been used; what were the consequences for the employee; and were there adequate safeguards. In López Ribalda v. Spain (Grand Chamber, 17 October 2019) the court found no violation where a supermarket used covert cameras for ten days on cashier areas after reasonable suspicion of serious theft. Together they mark the boundary: covert monitoring is possible, but only for serious, specific suspicion, limited in time and space.

Works councils and co-determination

For a US employer, works councils are the biggest practical difference. In Germany the Federal Labor Court reads section 87(1) no. 6 broadly: what matters is whether a system is objectively suitable for monitoring, not whether the employer intends to use it that way, so time tracking, email filtering, endpoint security and collaboration analytics can all need agreement. Introducing such a system without it can be stopped by injunction, and data collected may be unusable. The usual instrument is a works agreement (Betriebsvereinbarung) that defines purposes, data, access, retention and a ban on performance evaluation from certain data. The Netherlands, Austria and France have comparable consultation or consent rights, and Italy routes approval through unions or the labor inspectorate. Global rollouts of productivity software therefore usually run months later in these countries, with narrower configurations.

The AI Act and the Platform Work Directive

  • Banned since 2 February 2025: AI systems that infer the emotions of people in the workplace, except for medical or safety reasons (AI Act article 5(1)(f)), with fines up to €35 million or 7% of global turnover. Sentiment analysis of employees' faces, voices or typing falls squarely within it.
  • High-risk employment uses: AI for recruitment, task allocation, and monitoring and evaluation of workers is high-risk under Annex III. The Digital Omnibus on AI (Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, in force 27 July 2026) moved the application date for these obligations from 2 August 2026 to 2 December 2027. They will include risk management, human oversight, logging and information to workers.
  • Platform Work Directive ((EU) 2024/2831): member states must transpose it by 2 December 2026. Its algorithmic management chapter limits processing of data such as emotional state and private conversations, requires human oversight of automated monitoring and decision systems and gives workers a right to review of significant decisions. Several member states had not published draft legislation by late 2026. See algorithmic management.

Leading fines

  • H&M (Hamburg, October 2020): €35.3 million, for managers recording details of employees' health, family and religious beliefs at a service center.
  • Amazon France Logistique (CNIL, December 2023): €32 million, for warehouse scanner indicators including "idle time" and scan-speed metrics and retention of all indicators for 31 days. On 23 December 2025 the Conseil d'État cut the fine to €15 million, annulling the findings on three indicators but upholding the excessive retention and transparency and security failings.
  • Foodinho, Glovo group (Italy, November 2024): €5 million, for rider geolocation and biometric identity checks, after €2.6 million in 2021.
  • A French real-estate company (CNIL, December 2024): €40,000, for software that took screenshots every three to fifteen minutes and tracked "non-productive" sites, without a DPIA.
  • CNIL simplified procedure, 2025: ten sanctions totaling €104,000, six of them for employee surveillance.

A compliance checklist

  1. Define the purpose of each tool and document necessity and proportionality.
  2. Choose the lawful basis (not consent) and complete the legitimate interests assessment.
  3. Run a DPIA before deployment.
  4. Consult or obtain agreement from the works council, union or labor inspectorate where national law requires it, and sign a works agreement in Germany.
  5. Publish a specific monitoring notice in the local language.
  6. Configure for minimization: disable keystroke logging, screenshots and webcam features; prefer aggregates; set short retention.
  7. Remove any emotion-recognition functionality.
  8. Plan for the AI Act high-risk obligations from December 2027 and the Platform Work Directive where it applies.
  9. Restrict and log access, and route any decision based on monitoring data through a person.
  10. Prepare for access requests, which employees use to obtain monitoring data.

Key takeaways

  • Monitoring EU employees requires a lawful basis (legitimate interests or legal obligation, almost never consent), prior specific notice, proportionality, minimization and usually a data protection impact assessment.
  • The Court of Justice's C-34/21 ruling (2023) means national employee-data rules must add real safeguards; Germany now relies on GDPR directly while a new employee data act is prepared.
  • Bărbulescu (2017) and López Ribalda (2019) set the human rights tests: notice, intrusiveness, legitimate reason, less intrusive means, consequences and safeguards; covert monitoring only for specific serious suspicion, briefly.
  • Works councils in Germany and the Netherlands must agree to monitoring systems, and Italy requires union or inspectorate approval, so rollouts take longer and are narrower.
  • Workplace emotion recognition has been banned since 2 February 2025; AI Act high-risk employment rules now apply from 2 December 2027; the Platform Work Directive must be transposed by 2 December 2026.
  • Fines include H&M €35.3 million, Amazon France Logistique €32 million cut to €15 million on appeal in December 2025, Foodinho €5 million, and a stream of smaller CNIL sanctions for screenshots and over-monitoring.

Frequently asked questions

Is employee monitoring legal in the EU?

Yes, if it complies with the GDPR and national labor law: a lawful basis other than consent, a specific and proportionate purpose, prior notice to employees, minimization, short retention, usually a data protection impact assessment, and in many countries consultation with or approval by a works council or union.

Can employees consent to monitoring under GDPR?

Generally not validly. Because of the imbalance of power, EU regulators say consent in employment is rarely freely given. Employers rely on legitimate interests, with a documented balancing test, or on a legal obligation such as working-time recording.

Do German employers need works council approval for monitoring software?

Yes. Under section 87(1) no. 6 of the Works Constitution Act, the works council has co-determination rights over any technical device objectively capable of monitoring employees' behavior or performance, whether or not the employer intends to monitor. Approval is usually documented in a works agreement.

Is keystroke logging legal in France?

Generally no. The CNIL considers keyloggers unlawful except for exceptional, documented security needs, and regards systematic screenshots and continuous webcam monitoring as disproportionate. It fined a company €40,000 in December 2024 for screenshot-based monitoring.

Does the EU AI Act ban employee monitoring?

Not generally. It bans AI that infers employees' emotions at work, except for medical or safety reasons, from 2 February 2025. AI used to monitor and evaluate workers is classified as high-risk, with obligations applying from 2 December 2027 after the 2026 Digital Omnibus delay.

What was the Amazon France fine for?

The CNIL fined Amazon France Logistique €32 million in December 2023 over warehouse scanner monitoring, including idle-time and speed indicators kept for 31 days. In December 2025 the Conseil d'État reduced it to €15 million, annulling the findings on three indicators but upholding the retention and other breaches.

Can EU employers monitor employees covertly?

Only exceptionally. Under López Ribalda v. Spain, covert monitoring may be justified where there is reasonable suspicion of serious misconduct, the monitoring is limited in time and area, and less intrusive means would not work. National law may add conditions.

What is the Platform Work Directive?

An EU directive adopted in 2024 that member states must implement by 2 December 2026. It gives people working through digital platforms rights over algorithmic management, including limits on processing data such as emotional state and private conversations, human oversight of automated decisions and a right to review.
