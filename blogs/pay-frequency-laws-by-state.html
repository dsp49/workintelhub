Pay Frequency and Payday Laws by State (2026)

P Pavan
November 4, 2026 · 12 min read

Federal law does not say how often employees must be paid. The Fair Labor Standards Act requires that minimum wage and overtime be paid on the regular payday for the period in which they were earned, and leaves the rest to the states. The states have answered in every possible way: Connecticut, Massachusetts, New Hampshire, Rhode Island and Vermont require weekly or biweekly pay; most require at least semimonthly; Colorado, Delaware, Idaho, Kansas, North Dakota, Oregon, South Dakota, Washington and Wisconsin allow monthly; Alabama, Florida, Montana, Nebraska, North Carolina, Pennsylvania and South Carolina set no frequency at all. New York requires manual workers to be paid every week and has generated hundreds of lawsuits over it.

Frequency is only half the rule. Many states also cap the lag between the end of a pay period and the payday, from California's 7 days for weekly and biweekly schedules to Michigan's 14 and Illinois' 13. This guide gives both for all 50 states and the District of Columbia, the exemptions that let salaried staff be paid less often, the states that changed something recently, and how to choose a schedule that complies everywhere you have employees. The federal position

The Department of Labor's guidance states the rule in one sentence: "Wages required by the FLSA are due on the regular payday for the pay period covered." The Act requires that a payday exist and that minimum wage and overtime be paid on it, with overtime payable no later than the payday for the period in which the workweek ends (29 CFR 778.106), but it does not say the payday must come every week or every month. It also does not provide any mechanism for collecting promised wages above the minimum; those are "for agreement between the employer and the employees" and are enforced under state wage payment laws. That is why the Department of Labor publishes a table of state payday requirements rather than a federal one, and why an employer's obligations depend entirely on where each employee works.

Pay frequency by state

"Minimum frequency" is the least often an employer may pay most hourly or non-exempt employees. Where a state allows a less frequent schedule for certain employees, the exemption is noted. The table reflects state statutes as of November 2026; the Department of Labor's own table was last revised in January 2023 and is out of date for Connecticut.

Maximum lag between period end and payday

A semimonthly schedule that pays on the 15th for work through the 15th is impossible to run, so every employer builds in a lag, and about half the states cap it. These are the ones most often breached, usually when an employer moves paydays later to accommodate time-sheet approval or an outsourced payroll bureau.

Exemptions for salaried and exempt employees

Most frequency statutes were written for wage earners, and about a dozen states let employers pay salaried or exempt staff less often. The test is usually the Fair Labor Standards Act's executive, administrative and professional exemption (see the 2027 exempt salary threshold), so an employee's classification determines their minimum pay frequency as well as their overtime status.

  • Monthly permitted for exempt employees: California (by the 26th of the month worked), District of Columbia, Illinois, Maryland, Nevada, New Jersey (executive, supervisory and special classifications), New Mexico, Oklahoma, Texas (any FLSA-overtime-exempt employee), Utah (yearly-salaried employees).
  • Semimonthly permitted where hourly staff must be paid weekly or biweekly: Massachusetts (salaried employees semimonthly, or monthly at the employee's election), Virginia (salaried employees semimonthly; employees above 150 percent of the state average weekly wage may agree to monthly).
  • Employee election: Alaska and Hawaii let employees choose monthly pay; Massachusetts lets salaried employees choose it.
  • Agency permission: Connecticut, New Hampshire, New York (manual workers at large employers) and Rhode Island allow less frequent pay only with regulator approval, and in New Hampshire's case not less often than monthly.
  • Arizona's out-of-state rule: employers whose principal place of business and payroll system are both outside Arizona may pay exempt and supervisory employees monthly.

Misclassifying an employee as exempt therefore produces two violations in these states: unpaid overtime and, if the employee was paid monthly, a frequency violation for every pay period.

New York's weekly pay rule and the 2025 damages change

Labor Law 191(1)(a) requires that a "manual worker" be paid weekly and not later than seven days after the end of the week in which wages were earned. The Department of Labor reads "manual worker" broadly, to include anyone who spends more than 25 percent of working time on physical labor, which sweeps in retail clerks, restaurant staff, warehouse workers, hairdressers and many others whose employers assumed they were "clerical and other workers" who may be paid semimonthly. Large employers (the Department's threshold is 1,000 employees) may apply for authorization to pay manual workers semimonthly if they show a reliable payroll history and other conditions, and union consent where workers are represented.

For decades the only consequence of paying a manual worker biweekly was a Department of Labor order. In 2019 the First Department held in Vega v. CM & Associates that an employee paid late could sue privately for liquidated damages of 100 percent of the late-paid wages, even though the wages had in fact been paid, on the theory that a late payment is an underpayment until it arrives. Hundreds of class actions followed, with exposure equal to half of every biweekly payroll for six years. The Second Department disagreed in Grant v. Global Aircraft Dispatch in 2024, and the split was unresolved when the legislature acted.

The state budget signed May 9, 2025 (Chapter 56, Part U) amended Labor Law 198(1-a). For a first violation by an employer that paid the employee on a regular payday at least semimonthly, damages are now capped at "no more than one hundred percent of the lost interest found to be due for the delayed payment of wages," calculated daily at the rate set under Banking Law 14-a, which is 16 percent a year. Full liquidated damages apply only where the employer has previously been found, by a final order of the Commissioner or a court, to have violated the weekly-pay rule for employees doing the same work. The amendment applies to pending cases as well as new ones, and its retroactive application was being challenged in federal court as of late 2025. The practical effect is that a first-time biweekly payer owes interest on a week's wages for a week, a few dollars per paycheck, rather than half the paycheck. The obligation to pay weekly is unchanged.

Recent changes

  • New York (May 2025): damages for first-time weekly-pay violations limited to lost interest, as above.
  • Michigan (2018): monthly pay periods permitted for any employee if all wages are paid within 15 days of the period's end (2018 PA 170), a change that still surprises employers who learned the semimonthly rule.
  • Connecticut (2016): biweekly pay permitted without Labor Commissioner approval (P.A. 16-169). The federal Department of Labor's table still shows Connecticut as weekly only.
  • Pending, not enacted: Rhode Island bills to loosen the weekly-pay rule (most recently H 5939 in 2025) have not passed; Vermont's 2025 Act 91 amended parts of the wage payment chapter effective April 2026, with no confirmed change to the weekly default; New York bills to narrow "manual worker" have not advanced.

No state has changed its basic frequency requirement since Michigan's 2018 amendment. The movement has been in remedies and in the definition of who counts as a manual or hourly worker.

Choosing a schedule that complies in every state

An employer with staff in several states has three options.

  1. Biweekly everywhere, with weekly where required. Biweekly satisfies every state except New York (manual workers), Rhode Island and, for hourly employees, Vermont unless notice is given. It fits within every lag rule if payday is within six days of period end. This is the most common choice: 43 percent of US private employers paid biweekly in the Bureau of Labor Statistics' most recent survey, against 27 percent weekly, 19 percent semimonthly and 11 percent monthly. The hours per period are constant, which makes overtime simple. The cost is the 27-paycheck year, which 2027 is for Friday schedules beginning January 1; see pay periods in a year.
  2. Semimonthly for salaried staff, biweekly or weekly for hourly staff. Semimonthly is permitted for exempt employees everywhere and matches monthly accounting. Running two schedules costs more in payroll administration and causes confusion when an employee moves between classifications.
  3. Weekly everywhere. Lawful in every state, preferred by hourly workers, and increasingly offered as a recruiting feature; the cost is 52 payroll runs and the fees that come with them.

Whatever the schedule, the lag rules decide the payday. A biweekly period ending Saturday with a Friday payday has a six-day lag and complies everywhere; the same period with a payday the following Wednesday breaches Massachusetts and Vermont. What late pay costs

Frequency and lag violations are enforced by state labor departments, with penalties that range from administrative fines (Arizona treats a violation as a petty offense; California's Labor Code 210 imposes $100 for a first violation and $200 plus 25 percent of the amount for later or willful ones, per employee per pay period) to private damages (New York, as above; Massachusetts treble damages under its Wage Act for any late payment, with no good-faith defense). The larger exposure is usually indirect: a late payday that is also a final paycheck triggers the much harsher final paycheck penalties, and a pattern of late pay is evidence in wage theft claims. For employees, the state labor department takes frequency complaints at no cost in every state that has a rule, and the notice-of-pay documents most states require at hire are the evidence of what was promised.

Key takeaways

  • Federal law sets no pay frequency; the FLSA requires only that minimum wage and overtime be paid on the regular payday for the period, so frequency is entirely a matter of state law.
  • Weekly or biweekly pay is required for hourly employees in Connecticut, Massachusetts, New Hampshire, Rhode Island, Vermont and Virginia, and weekly for manual workers in New York. Most other states require at least semimonthly; nine allow monthly; seven set no frequency.
  • About half the states cap the lag between period end and payday: 6 days in Massachusetts and Vermont, 7 in California (weekly and biweekly) and New York (manual workers), 8 in Connecticut, 13 in Illinois, 14 in Michigan.
  • About a dozen states let exempt executive, administrative and professional employees be paid monthly, including California, Illinois, Nevada, New Jersey, New Mexico and Texas; misclassification creates a frequency violation on top of unpaid overtime.
  • New York's May 2025 budget capped damages for a first weekly-pay violation at lost interest (16 percent a year) where the employer paid at least semimonthly; full liquidated damages now require a prior final finding. The weekly obligation itself is unchanged.
  • Michigan has permitted monthly schedules since 2018 and Connecticut biweekly without approval since 2016; the federal Department of Labor's state table, last revised January 2023, does not reflect the Connecticut change.
  • Biweekly pay with a payday within six days of period end complies everywhere except for New York manual workers, Rhode Island and Vermont without notice; 43 percent of private employers use it.

Frequently asked questions

How often does an employer legally have to pay employees?

It depends on the state. Federal law sets no frequency. Connecticut, Massachusetts, New Hampshire, Rhode Island and Vermont require weekly or biweekly pay for most employees, and New York requires weekly pay for manual workers. Most states require at least semimonthly; Colorado, Delaware, Idaho, Kansas, North Dakota, Oregon, South Dakota, Washington and Wisconsin allow monthly; Alabama, Florida, Montana, Nebraska, North Carolina, Pennsylvania and South Carolina set no frequency.

Is it legal to pay employees once a month?

In nine states for all employees (Colorado, Delaware, Idaho, Kansas, North Dakota, Oregon, South Dakota, Washington, Wisconsin), in the seven states with no frequency rule, and for exempt executive, administrative and professional employees in about a dozen more, including California, Illinois, Nevada, New Jersey, New Mexico, Texas and the District of Columbia. Elsewhere, monthly pay for hourly employees violates state law.

Can my employer pay me late?

Most states set a maximum number of days between the end of the pay period and the payday, from 6 days in Massachusetts and Vermont to 31 in Wisconsin, and all states with a frequency rule require a regular payday designated in advance. A payment after the designated payday or outside the lag window is a violation reportable to the state labor department, and in Massachusetts and New York can support a private claim.

What is New York's weekly pay law for manual workers?

Labor Law 191(1)(a) requires manual workers, defined by the state to include anyone spending more than 25 percent of their time on physical labor, to be paid weekly and within seven days of the end of the week. Employers with at least 1,000 employees may apply for authorization to pay semimonthly. Since May 2025, a first violation by an employer that paid at least semimonthly carries damages limited to lost interest rather than 100 percent liquidated damages.

Can an employer change pay frequency?

Yes, if the new schedule meets the state minimum, employees receive advance notice (Vermont requires written notice to move from weekly; Massachusetts requires 90 days' notice for employers moving off weekly pay; most states require updating the hire-time pay notice), and no wages are delayed beyond the lag limit during the transition. Moving from biweekly to semimonthly changes hours per period and should be timed to a period boundary.

Is semimonthly pay legal for hourly employees?

In most states, yes. It is not sufficient for hourly employees in Connecticut, Massachusetts, New Hampshire, Rhode Island, Vermont (without notice) and Virginia, or for manual workers in New York. Semimonthly also requires care with overtime, since workweeks straddle pay periods; overtime must still be calculated on the workweek and paid by the next regular payday.

What is the penalty for paying employees late?

It varies: California charges $100 per employee for a first violation and $200 plus 25 percent of the late amount for subsequent or willful ones; Massachusetts awards mandatory treble damages for any late wages; New York's weekly-pay damages are now interest for first offenders and 100 percent liquidated damages for repeat offenders; Arizona treats violations as petty offenses. Late final paychecks carry separate, usually larger, penalties.

Does the FLSA require biweekly pay?

No. The Fair Labor Standards Act requires that wages due under it be paid on the regular payday for the pay period and that overtime be paid no later than the payday for the period in which the workweek ends, but it does not specify how often paydays occur. State law does.
