How to Set Up Employee Monitoring in a Team Under 50

P Pavan
November 26, 2026 · 7 min read

Setting up employee monitoring in a team under 50 takes about a quarter and six steps: write down the one question the data must answer, choose the smallest set of features that answers it, write a one-page policy, give written notice before anything is installed, run a visible pilot with managers included, and review the whole thing at ninety days with stopping as a real option. The software takes an afternoon. The order is what decides whether the team accepts it.

Small teams have one advantage and one risk. The advantage is that you can talk to everyone in person. The risk is that everyone knows who is being watched, so a careless rollout damages the whole company rather than one department. If you have not yet decided whether you need monitoring at all, start with our seven signs a small business needs employee monitoring.

The short answer

Decide what you will collect before you see what you could collect. Write the purpose and the policy first, then choose the tool.

Announce, then install, with a visible gap. At least a week between the notice and the first installation. A quiet install cannot be repaired afterward.

Start minimal. Time, applications and websites. Screenshots only for a billing reason, recording only for a documented concern on specific machines.

The timeline at a glance

Faster is possible, but the time comes out of the pilot, which is the step that catches the expensive mistakes. Do not start a step until the previous one exists in writing.

The six steps

  1. Decide the purpose in one sentence. Name the decision the data will change. Good: are we quoting projects accurately, is the support rotation sized correctly, is anyone carrying far more work than the rest. Bad: understand productivity, get visibility, see who is working. If your sentence contains the word visibility, rewrite it until it contains a verb someone can act on. That sentence settles every later argument about scope.
  2. Pick the minimum features. Choose the thinnest data that answers your sentence and nothing bundled alongside. For most small teams that is hours, applications and websites, and activity, viewed at team level. The table below sets out when each heavier feature earns its place. Limit monitoring to company devices.
  3. Write a one-page policy. Name the fields captured, who can see each one, how long records are kept as a number of days, what the data will never be used for, and how someone sees their own record. The section that does the most for acceptance is what is not collected: personal accounts, private messages, camera and microphone, activity outside working hours. Our employee monitoring policy template has the clauses, and how to write a policy employees will sign covers the eight sections.
  4. Give notice and collect acknowledgments, then announce. Issue the written notice your state requires, collect a signed acknowledgment, post it where people work, and hold a live meeting at least a week before the first installation. Cover four things in order: why, what, who sees it, and what it will never be used for.
  5. Run a visible pilot. One team of five to twelve people with a mix of work types, every manager in scope, for three or four weeks. Each person sees their own full record from day one. Your job is to learn whether the categories make sense and which fields nobody opens, not to analyze people.
  6. Review at ninety days. Put the date in the calendar at the start. Bring the list of decisions the data produced, delete any field that fed none, ask the team anonymously whether the arrangement feels fair, and publish the outcome: keep, change or stop.

Step 4 in detail: notice, acknowledgment and the law

Being small does not exempt you. Four states have dedicated monitoring notice laws, and notice duties generally follow where the employee works, so one remote hire can bring a state's rule with them.

ConnecticutPrior written notice of the types of monitoring, plus a conspicuously posted notice$500 first, $1,000 second, $3,000 third and each after
DelawareA daily electronic notice, or a one-time notice the employee acknowledges$100 per violation
New YorkWritten notice on hiring, acknowledged by the employee, plus a posted notice$500 first, $1,000 second, $3,000 third and each after
MaineNotice to applicants during hiring, plus written notice to affected employees at least once a year$100 to $500 per violation

Ask for acknowledgment, not consent. None of the state statutes requires consent, and an employee asked to sign on their first day cannot really refuse. What protects you is evidence, on a date, that the person was told before monitoring began. Our employee monitoring consent form has the wording, including the Delaware daily banner. Attach the policy as a file rather than a link, and re-issue the acknowledgment whenever what you record changes.

Never install first to see what the data looks like

Teams find out, and a rollout that started in secret cannot be repaired by a policy afterward. A form signed after the agent was installed documents the gap rather than closing it, and in a notice state a quiet install is also a violation. If you cannot announce it, that is your answer.

Step 5 in detail: running the pilot

In a team under 50, the pilot is usually one team, and in a business of a dozen it may be almost everyone. Choose a group with varied work rather than the most willing volunteers; a pilot with one skeptic finds problems while they are cheap to fix. Watch how often people correct their own entries, which categories collect a suspicious share of the week, how much time the system cannot explain, and which fields nobody opens. Those are your first deletions.

Publish aggregate findings to the pilot team before leadership sees them, and lead with whatever is uncomfortable for management. Meeting load is a common one: Microsoft's 2025 Work Trend Index found that 57% of meetings are ad hoc, called without a calendar invite. That is a management finding, and saying so proves the exercise is not aimed at individuals.

If someone refuses, meet privately and ask what specifically they object to. Many refusals point at a real flaw, such as wider access than stated. Fix what is fixable and publish the fix. The rollout guide on introducing tracking without losing trust covers the harder cases.

Step 6 in detail: the ninety-day review

A working program changes management decisions, not employee behavior. By day ninety you should be able to name what changed because of the data: a recurring meeting cut, a project restaffed, a quote corrected, a hire approved. If the only visible effect is people looking busier, switch it off. Watch for the failure signals too: activity that looks staged, thinner status updates, and people avoiding long stretches of deep work because idle time reads badly. Key takeaways

  • Write the one-sentence purpose and the policy before you look at any tool.
  • Start with time, applications and websites; add screenshots only for a billing reason and recording only for a documented concern.
  • Give written notice and collect acknowledgments at least a week before installing anything; Connecticut, Delaware, Maine and New York have notice laws.
  • Pilot with one mixed team of five to twelve, managers included, for three or four weeks, with everyone seeing their own record.
  • Review at ninety days with keep, change or stop as real options, and delete every field no decision used.

Frequently asked questions

How long does it take to set up employee monitoring in a small team?

About a quarter: a week to write the purpose and policy, a week of notice, three or four weeks of pilot, then a wider rollout and a formal review at ninety days. Installing the software itself takes an afternoon.

Do I need employee consent to monitor in a small business?

No state statute requires consent. Connecticut, Delaware, Maine and New York require notice, and Delaware and New York require the employee to acknowledge it. Collect a signed acknowledgment everywhere, because it turns a disputed conversation into a dated document.

Which monitoring features should a small team start with?

Hours, applications and websites, and activity viewed at team level. Add screenshots only if clients require proof of work, and keep recording and keystrokes for a specific documented concern on specific machines.

Should managers be monitored during the pilot?

Yes. Put every manager in scope alongside the pilot team. It shows the program is not aimed at individuals and surfaces findings, such as meeting load, that are management problems.

Can I monitor employees' personal devices?

Not without consent, and not beyond a work profile or work accounts even with it. For a small business the safer answer is company devices for anyone who will be monitored.
