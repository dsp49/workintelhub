Can You Monitor Independent Contractors? The Control Risk

P Pavan
November 20, 2026 · 10 min read

You can usually track an independent contractor's billable time, and contractors often agree to it to get paid, but the more you monitor how they work, the more you look like their employer. Every test for worker status asks about control: who decides how, when and where the work is done. Screenshots every few minutes, keystroke counts, mandatory GPS and real-time activity scores are tools for supervising the manner of work, and courts and agencies have treated that kind of oversight as evidence of employment. Misclassification exposes a business to back overtime, payroll taxes, benefits, penalties and, in California, liability under the ABC test.

This guide explains the tests (including the Department of Labor's final rule announced November 19, 2026), what the case law says about technology, how to track contractors' time without creating control, and a checklist. For the underlying distinction, see 1099 vs W-2.

The tests that decide worker status

LawTestWhere monitoring matters
Fair Labor Standards Act (DOL)Economic reality: is the worker economically dependent on the business or in business for themselves?The control factor, one of two core factors under the 2026 rule
Federal tax (IRS)Common-law control: behavioral control, financial control, relationship of the partiesBehavioral control: the right to direct how work is done, even if not exercised; systems that measure how the work is performed point to employment
California and some other states (ABC test)The worker is an employee unless (A) free from control and direction both under the contract and in fact, (B) doing work outside the usual course of the business, and (C) customarily engaged in an independent tradeProng A: monitoring how work is done can defeat contractor status on its own
National Labor Relations ActCommon-law agency test (Atlanta Opera, 2023), with entrepreneurial opportunity as one factorControl over the manner and means of work

Most states use one of these tests or a variant for wage law, unemployment insurance and workers' compensation, and the same worker can be a contractor under one and an employee under another.

The DOL's 2026 rule and technology

On November 19, 2026 the Department of Labor announced a final rule rescinding its 2024 independent contractor rule and returning to a streamlined economic-reality test like the 2021 version, with two core factors, the nature and degree of control over the work and the worker's opportunity for profit or loss, and additional factors including skill, permanence and whether the work is part of an integrated unit. The rule takes effect 60 days after publication in the Federal Register. Investigators had already stopped applying the 2024 standard under 2025 enforcement guidance.

The 2024 rule had listed "whether the potential employer uses technological means to supervise the performance of the work" as an example of control. The 2026 rule drops that wording but says technology does not change how the factors apply: where software does what a human supervisor used to do, such as setting schedules, assigning work, reviewing work, disciplining or terminating, those acts are just as relevant to control. It adds delivery-app examples of technology that does and does not indicate control, and says that requiring compliance with legal obligations, safety standards, or contractually agreed deadlines and quality standards is not control.

What courts have said about monitoring

  • O'Connor v. Uber (N.D. Cal. 2015): denying summary judgment, the court said Uber's ability to observe drivers "at essentially all times" through the app arguably gave it a tremendous amount of control over the manner and means of the work, and treated rating thresholds that could lead to deactivation as evidence of control.
  • Scantland v. Jeffry Knight (11th Cir. 2013): meaningful supervision and monitoring of cable technicians supported employee status despite contractor agreements.
  • Platform and gig cases since then have repeatedly treated algorithmic monitoring, ratings and automated discipline as evidence of control, while routine tracking for billing and payment has carried less weight. See algorithmic management.

No reported decision found has turned on a time tracker's screenshots alone, but practitioners consistently treat requiring keystroke, screenshot or GPS software on contractors as strong evidence of control, especially in California.

Tracking that is usually safe

  • Time tracking for billing, where the contractor bills by the hour and logs time to support invoices, especially with tools the contractor chooses.
  • Marketplace payment protection: freelance platforms such as Upwork make screenshot-based work diaries a condition of their hourly payment guarantee; the contractor opts in to secure payment, and the platform, not the client, runs the tracker.
  • Deliverable and milestone tracking: reviewing finished work against agreed specifications and deadlines.
  • Security controls on your systems: access controls, logging and data loss prevention on accounts and data you provide, applied to everyone with access.
  • Legal and safety compliance: requiring licenses, insurance and safety standards.

Monitoring that signals control

  • Requiring the contractor to install activity monitoring on their own computer, with screenshots, keystroke counts or idle-time scores.
  • Requiring set working hours or minimum online time, enforced by monitoring.
  • Continuous GPS tracking of contractors' locations and routes.
  • Productivity scores, rankings or automated warnings that lead to discipline or termination.
  • Managers reviewing how the contractor works in real time rather than what they deliver.
  • Applying the employee monitoring policy to contractors as if they were staff.

Monitoring a contractor's personal device also raises privacy questions under state law, and in the EU and UK requires a lawful basis and notice; see EU and UK rules.

Contract terms and practices

  1. Define the work as deliverables, milestones and acceptance criteria, not hours or methods.
  2. Where you pay by the hour, state that the contractor records time for billing using a tool of their choice, or a platform's tracker that they elect for payment protection.
  3. State that the contractor controls the manner, means, hours and location of the work, and live up to it.
  4. Limit monitoring to security of your systems and data, described in the contract and applied consistently.
  5. Do not include contractors in employee performance management, productivity dashboards or discipline.
  6. Review the relationship periodically; contractors who work only for you, full time, on core work, under close supervision are at the highest risk of reclassification under any test.

A checklist

  • Which test applies in the contractor's state, and for which purposes?
  • Is the tracking for billing, security or supervision? Only the first two are low-risk.
  • Who chose and runs the tool?
  • Does any monitoring data feed decisions about how the contractor works, or about ending the contract?
  • Does the contract describe outcomes rather than methods?
  • Would the contractor, viewed as a whole, look like an employee to the IRS, the DOL or a California court?

Key takeaways

  • Tracking a contractor's billable time is usually acceptable, but monitoring how they work (screenshots, keystrokes, GPS, activity scores) is evidence of control that can make them an employee.
  • Every worker-status test turns on control: the DOL economic-reality test, the IRS behavioral control test, the NLRB's common-law test and California's ABC test, where prong A can be failed on control alone.
  • The DOL's final rule announced November 19, 2026 restores a 2021-style test with control and opportunity for profit or loss as core factors; it says technology that performs supervisory functions is just as relevant as human supervision.
  • Courts have treated constant app-based observation and rating-based deactivation as evidence of control (O'Connor v. Uber, 2015) and meaningful monitoring as supporting employee status (Scantland, 2013).
  • Safer practices: deliverable-based contracts, contractor-chosen or platform-run trackers for billing, security controls on your own systems, and no inclusion in employee performance processes.

Frequently asked questions

Can you monitor independent contractors?

You can track the time a contractor bills and secure your own systems and data, but monitoring how they do the work, through screenshots, keystroke tracking, required hours or GPS, is evidence of control that can lead to the contractor being classified as an employee under federal and state tests.

Can I require a contractor to use time tracking software?

For billing, often yes, especially if the contractor chooses the tool or opts into a platform's tracker for payment protection. Requiring invasive activity monitoring, such as frequent screenshots or keystroke counts, on the contractor's own computer looks like supervising the manner of work and increases misclassification risk.

Does monitoring make a contractor an employee?

Not by itself, but it is evidence of control, the central question in every worker-status test. Combined with set hours, full-time dependence on one client and core work, monitoring can tip the analysis, particularly under California's ABC test, where any control over how the work is done can be decisive.

What did the DOL's 2026 independent contractor rule change?

Announced November 19, 2026, it rescinds the 2024 rule and returns to a streamlined economic-reality test focused on two core factors, control and opportunity for profit or loss. It drops the 2024 rule's specific reference to technological supervision but says software that performs supervisory functions counts like human supervision.

Is Upwork time tracking a sign of control?

Generally less so, because the freelancer opts into Upwork's work diary to qualify for its hourly payment protection, and the platform runs it for billing verification. A client that uses the diary to direct how the freelancer works, or adds its own monitoring, moves toward control.

How do I track contractor work without misclassification risk?

Contract for deliverables and milestones, let the contractor control hours and methods, accept time records for billing, review results rather than process, apply only security controls to your systems, and keep contractors out of employee performance management and productivity monitoring.
