9/80 Work Schedule: How It Works, FLSA Rules and a Template

P Pavan
November 12, 2026 · 12 min read

A 9/80 schedule fits 80 hours of work into nine days over two weeks instead of ten: eight nine-hour days, one eight-hour day, and every other Friday off. Employees get 26 three-day weekends a year without losing pay; employers keep the same hours of coverage and often extend the working day at both ends. It is the most common compressed workweek in aerospace, defense, engineering, utilities and local government, and the one that most often goes wrong on paper, because it only works under the Fair Labor Standards Act if the workweek is defined to split the eight-hour day down the middle.

This guide sets out the exact pattern, why the midday split matters and how to document it, what happens to holidays, PTO and sick days on a nine-hour day, how state daily-overtime laws (California above all) change the rules, the evidence on compressed weeks from the few studies that exist, and a template schedule and policy you can adopt. For the broader family of patterns, see hybrid work schedules and rotating shift schedules.

The 9/80 pattern

The schedule repeats every two weeks. In week A the employee works Monday to Thursday for nine hours each and Friday for eight: 44 hours. In week B the employee works Monday to Thursday for nine hours and has Friday off: 36 hours. Total 80 hours, nine days, one three-day weekend per fortnight. A typical day is 7:00 to 4:30 with a 30-minute lunch, or 8:00 to 6:00 with an hour.

Counted by calendar weeks, week A has 44 hours and week B has 36, and a non-exempt employee would be owed four hours of overtime in week A under federal law. The schedule avoids that through the definition of the workweek, which is the subject of the next section. For exempt salaried staff the overtime question does not arise, which is why many employers offer 9/80 to exempt staff first.

Teams usually split into two groups, one with Friday A off and one with Friday B off, so the office is staffed every Friday at half strength. Some employers run the off-day on Monday instead, or let employees choose any fixed day; the arithmetic is the same as long as the day is fixed in advance.

Why the workweek must split the 8-hour day

The FLSA requires overtime for hours over 40 in a workweek, and 29 CFR 778.105 defines a workweek as a fixed and regularly recurring period of 168 hours, seven consecutive 24-hour periods, which "need not coincide with the calendar week but may begin on any day and at any hour of the day." Once established, it stays fixed; it may be changed only if the change is intended to be permanent and not designed to evade overtime.

The 9/80 solution is to start the workweek in the middle of the eight-hour day. If the eight-hour Friday runs 7:00 to 4:00 with lunch at noon, the workweek is defined as beginning at noon on Friday and ending at 11:59 a.m. the following Friday. Each workweek then contains:

  • Friday afternoon of week A: 4 hours
  • Monday to Thursday: 36 hours
  • Friday morning of the next week: 4 hours in the week with the eight-hour Friday, or 0 hours in the week with the Friday off

Week A workweek: 4 + 36 + 0 = 40 (the off Friday contributes nothing). Week B workweek: 0 + 36 + 4 = 40. Every workweek is exactly 40 hours and no overtime is owed for the schedule itself. No regulation or opinion letter describes the 9/80 split by name; it rests on the plain text of 778.105, and public employers that run 9/80 for overtime-eligible staff write it into policy in exactly these terms (Sacramento County, for example, requires that the workweek "commence in the middle of the eight-hour work shift" and marks timesheets with the two four-hour halves). It is lawful as long as the workweek is genuinely fixed, applied consistently and documented before the schedule starts.

Four conditions keep it lawful:

  1. Document the workweek in writing before the schedule starts, in the policy and in the timekeeping system's workweek setting. A payroll system still set to Sunday-to-Saturday will compute 44 and 36 and either overpay or, if someone "fixes" it by hand, underpay.
  2. The split must be at the midpoint of the eight-hour day. If the eight-hour day is 7:00 to 4:00 with an hour's lunch, the split is at noon; with a 30-minute lunch and a 3:30 finish, it is 11:30. An employee who leaves early on the eight-hour day shifts hours between workweeks and can create overtime in one of them.
  3. The off day must be the same day of the week as the eight-hour day. A Friday-off group must have its eight-hour day on Friday. Moving the off day to Monday for one cycle breaks the 40-40 split.
  4. Do not flip-flop. Changing an employee's workweek definition when they switch groups is permitted if the change is permanent; changing it back and forth to avoid overtime in particular weeks is not, and 29 CFR 778.302 requires overtime to be computed on both the old and new workweeks for the overlap period, paying the higher.

Any actual extra work is still overtime. If an employee on 9/80 stays two hours late on a Tuesday, the workweek containing that Tuesday has 42 hours and two hours are owed at time and a half, measured against the defined workweek, not the calendar week. Holidays, PTO and sick days

The schedule's awkward moments are days when the employee does not work a scheduled nine hours. Three questions need a policy answer.

A holiday falls on a nine-hour day. If holiday pay is eight hours (the usual company standard), the employee is one hour short for the workweek. Options: pay nine hours of holiday pay for 9/80 employees (simplest and most common in public agencies), require the employee to work an extra hour elsewhere in the workweek, or charge one hour of PTO. The policy must choose one; leaving it to managers produces inconsistent pay for identical situations.

A holiday falls on the employee's off Friday. The employee receives no benefit from the holiday unless the policy provides one. Most 9/80 policies give an alternative day off in the same pay period or eight hours of floating holiday. Public agencies often shift the holiday observance to the Thursday for that group.

A holiday falls on the eight-hour Friday. Eight hours of holiday pay covers it, but the split workweek means four hours of holiday pay fall in each of two workweeks. Payroll systems that apply holiday hours to the calendar day handle this automatically if the workweek setting is correct.

PTO and sick leave are charged in the hours actually missed: nine hours for a nine-hour day, eight for the eight-hour day. Employees on 9/80 use leave 12.5% faster per day off than colleagues on 5/8, which is the price of the extra Fridays and should be explained at enrollment. Some employers convert leave to hours rather than days for exactly this reason. For FMLA, intermittent leave is tracked in hours against the employee's actual schedule, so a 9/80 employee's 12 weeks equal 480 hours just like anyone else's.

State daily overtime: California and others

States with daily overtime laws make 9/80 harder because a nine-hour day exceeds the eight-hour threshold regardless of how the workweek is defined.

  • California requires overtime after eight hours in a day under Labor Code 510. A 9/80 for non-exempt employees is lawful only as an alternative workweek schedule (AWS) adopted under Labor Code 511 and the applicable wage order: the employer proposes a written schedule, holds a secret-ballot election in which at least two-thirds of the affected work unit vote in favor, and files the results with the Division of Labor Statistics and Research within 30 days. An AWS may allow up to 10 hours a day without daily overtime, but hours over the scheduled day and over 40 in the week are overtime, and the eight-hour day in a 9/80 is itself a complication because the wage orders contemplate regularly scheduled days of no more than 10 hours and a repeating schedule; counsel usually structure California 9/80s so that the "workweek" and the AWS both align. Exempt employees are not covered by daily overtime and can go on 9/80 without an election.
  • Alaska requires overtime over eight hours a day, with a flexible-work-plan exception that must be approved by the Department of Labor and Workforce Development.
  • Nevada requires daily overtime over eight hours only for employees earning less than 1.5 times the minimum wage, and allows a 4/10 by mutual agreement; a 9/80 for low-wage non-exempt staff is a problem.
  • Colorado requires overtime over 12 hours a day, so a nine-hour day is fine.
  • Public sector: federal agencies run compressed schedules under 5 U.S.C. 6121 to 6133, where 9/80 is called a "5/4-9" and the biweekly 80-hour basis is written into the statute, so the workweek split is unnecessary. State and local governments follow their own civil-service rules and the FLSA's section 7(k) exemption for police and fire.

A 4/10 is simpler legally (every calendar week is 40 hours) and gives a day off every week, but ten-hour days are harder on parents, commuters and anyone with evening commitments, and daily-overtime states still require an election or agreement. The 9/80 is the compromise: nine-hour days are tolerable, Fridays remain staffed, and the three-day weekend arrives every other week.

What the evidence says

Compressed workweeks are among the most common flexible arrangements offered by US employers; the SHRM Employee Benefits Survey found 27% of employers offering compressed workweeks in 2023, rising to 32% in health care and social assistance in the 2025 survey. The research on outcomes is thinner than for remote work and almost all of it studies 4/10 schedules rather than 9/80 specifically:

  • A meta-analysis by Baltes and colleagues (Journal of Applied Psychology, 1999) covering flexible and compressed schedules found compressed workweeks improved supervisor-rated performance and job satisfaction and had no significant effect on productivity or absenteeism. Satisfaction with the schedule itself was the strongest effect.
  • Fatigue research on extended shifts (notably Dembe and colleagues, Occupational and Environmental Medicine, 2005) finds injury risk rising with hours beyond eight a day and 40 a week, with the steepest increase past 12 hours; nine-hour days sit in the band where the effect is small, ten to twelve-hour days where it becomes material in physical jobs.
  • Public-agency reports (Los Angeles County, several California cities and federal agencies' alternative work schedule evaluations) generally report reduced commute trips, reduced sick-leave use on the compressed days, and no measurable change in service output; most also report that scheduling meetings across groups with different Fridays off is the main operational cost.
  • The large 4 Day Week Global trials of 2022 to 2024 measured a 32-hour week, not a compressed 40, so their results on burnout and retention do not transfer directly; what does transfer is the finding that coordination problems, not output, are the first thing to manage.

In short: employees like it, retention and recruiting benefit, output is neutral, and the risks are coordination and the slightly longer day rather than fatigue.

Advantages and drawbacks

For employees: 26 three-day weekends a year with no loss of pay; one fewer commute per fortnight (a 5% cut in commuting costs and time); appointments can be scheduled on the off Friday; nine-hour days are a smaller change than ten. The costs: leave is consumed faster per day off; childcare and school pickup can be harder with a 5:30 or 6:00 finish; the eight-hour Friday feels like a short day but is still a full commute.

For employers: longer daily coverage (a 7:00 to 6:00 window with staggered groups); a recruiting and retention benefit that costs nothing in payroll; some agencies report lower Friday sick-leave use. The costs: Friday staffing at half strength; the discipline of a split workweek in payroll and timekeeping; meetings have to avoid both Fridays or alternate; managers must enforce the fixed off day to keep the FLSA structure intact; and employees who work through their off Friday "to catch up" generate overtime for non-exempt staff and resentment for exempt staff.

Template schedule and policy language

The following can be adapted. Replace the hours with yours and keep the workweek definition tied to the midpoint of your eight-hour day.

ElementTemplate text
ScheduleParticipating employees work eight 9-hour days and one 8-hour day in each two-week period, with one regular day off (RDO) every other week. Standard hours are 7:00 a.m. to 4:30 p.m. with a 30-minute unpaid meal period on 9-hour days and 7:00 a.m. to 3:30 p.m. on the 8-hour day.
GroupsEmployees are assigned to Group A (RDO on the first Friday of the pay period) or Group B (RDO on the second Friday). Assignments are fixed and may be changed only with the manager's written approval, effective at the start of a pay period, and no more than twice a year.
Workweek (non-exempt employees)For overtime purposes, the workweek for employees on the 9/80 schedule begins at 11:30 a.m. on Friday and ends at 11:29 a.m. the following Friday. This workweek is fixed and recorded in the timekeeping system. Hours worked over 40 in the defined workweek are paid at one and one-half times the regular rate.
Eight-hour dayThe 8-hour day must be worked on the same day of the week as the RDO (Friday). Employees may not shorten or shift the 8-hour day without approval, because doing so moves hours between workweeks.
HolidaysEmployees on the 9/80 schedule receive 9 hours of holiday pay when a holiday falls on a scheduled 9-hour day and 8 hours when it falls on the 8-hour day. When a holiday falls on an employee's RDO, the employee receives 8 hours of floating holiday to be used within the same pay period or the next, with manager approval.
LeavePaid time off and sick leave are charged in the number of hours the employee was scheduled to work on the day of absence (9 or 8).
Eligibility and reviewParticipation is voluntary and subject to operational needs. The department head may suspend the schedule for an employee or group with two weeks' notice. Participation will be reviewed after six months.
California addendumFor non-exempt employees in California, this schedule is adopted as an alternative workweek schedule under Labor Code 511 following a secret-ballot election approved by at least two-thirds of the affected work unit on [date], reported to the Division of Labor Statistics and Research on [date]. Hours over the scheduled day or over 40 in a workweek are paid as overtime.

Pair the policy with a published schedule showing each group's Fridays for the year, and with a timekeeping configuration that uses the defined workweek. The time card calculator can check a fortnight's hours against the split.

Rolling it out

  1. Decide who is eligible. Start with roles that do not need daily single-person coverage. Customer-facing teams can join if two groups alternate Fridays.
  2. Fix the hours and the workweek definition and have counsel review it for every state where non-exempt participants work. California requires the election; Alaska requires a plan approval.
  3. Configure timekeeping and payroll for the split workweek before the first fortnight, and run a parallel test with the old configuration to see the 44/36 versus 40/40 difference disappear.
  4. Write the holiday and leave rules into the policy before anyone asks about the first holiday.
  5. Set meeting norms: no all-hands on Fridays, or alternate Fridays by group; publish the Friday roster.
  6. Pilot for six months with a before-and-after measure of output, overtime hours, sick leave on Fridays and Mondays, and a short satisfaction survey. The what-to-measure guide has output measures that do not depend on hours.
  7. Enforce the off day. Non-exempt employees who work on their RDO create overtime in that workweek; make clear that RDO work needs advance approval and will be paid, and that quietly logging in is not a favor to the team.

Key takeaways

  • A 9/80 schedule is eight 9-hour days, one 8-hour day and one day off in every two weeks: 80 hours in nine days and 26 three-day weekends a year at the same pay.
  • For non-exempt employees it avoids overtime only if the FLSA workweek is defined to start at the midpoint of the 8-hour day (for example noon Friday to noon Friday), so each workweek contains exactly 40 hours; the definition must be fixed, written and configured in the timekeeping system.
  • The 8-hour day must fall on the same weekday as the off day, employees may not shift hours on the 8-hour day, and the workweek may not be flipped back and forth; actual extra hours are still overtime measured against the defined workweek.
  • Holidays need a rule: pay 9 hours on a 9-hour day, give a floating holiday when the holiday lands on the off Friday, and charge PTO and sick leave in scheduled hours (9 or 8), which consumes leave faster per day off.
  • California non-exempt employees need an alternative workweek election (two-thirds secret ballot, filed with DLSR) because of daily overtime after 8 hours; Alaska requires a flexible plan approval; Nevada has limits for low-wage staff; exempt employees can start without an election anywhere.
  • Evidence: compressed weeks raise satisfaction and help retention with neutral effects on output; coordination across groups with different Fridays off is the main operating cost, and 9-hour days carry little of the fatigue risk seen at 12 hours.
  • A 4/10 is legally simpler (every calendar week is 40 hours) but 10-hour days are harder on families; the 9/80 keeps Fridays staffed at half strength and is the usual compromise.

Frequently asked questions

What is a 9/80 work schedule?

A compressed two-week schedule in which an employee works 80 hours over nine days: eight 9-hour days and one 8-hour day, with the tenth day off. In practice that is Monday to Thursday 9 hours every week, an 8-hour Friday one week and no work the next Friday, giving a three-day weekend every other week at full pay.

Does a 9/80 schedule create overtime?

Not if the FLSA workweek is defined to begin in the middle of the 8-hour day, so that each seven-day workweek contains exactly 40 hours (4 + 36 or 36 + 4). If the payroll system uses an ordinary Sunday-to-Saturday week, one week shows 44 hours and 4 hours of overtime are owed to non-exempt employees. The workweek definition must be fixed in writing before the schedule starts.

How do you define the workweek for a 9/80?

Start it at the midpoint of the 8-hour day. If the 8-hour Friday runs 7:00 to 3:30 with a 30-minute lunch, the workweek runs from 11:30 a.m. Friday to 11:29 a.m. the following Friday. The four hours after the split belong to the new workweek, and the off Friday contributes zero, so every workweek totals 40.

What happens to holidays on a 9/80 schedule?

Policy decides. Most employers pay 9 hours of holiday pay when a holiday falls on a 9-hour day, 8 hours when it falls on the 8-hour day, and give a floating day or an alternative day off when the holiday falls on the employee's regular day off. Without a rule, an 8-hour holiday on a 9-hour day leaves the employee an hour short.

Is a 9/80 schedule legal in California?

For exempt employees, yes, without formalities. For non-exempt employees it must be adopted as an alternative workweek schedule under Labor Code 511: a written proposal, a secret-ballot election approved by two-thirds of the affected work unit, and a report to the Division of Labor Statistics and Research within 30 days. Otherwise every 9-hour day incurs one hour of daily overtime.

How is PTO charged on a 9/80 schedule?

In scheduled hours: a day off on a 9-hour day uses 9 hours of PTO, on the 8-hour day 8 hours. Employees on 9/80 therefore use leave faster per day than colleagues on 5/8, which should be explained before they opt in. FMLA entitlement is tracked in hours against the actual schedule.

What is the difference between a 9/80 and a 4/10 schedule?

A 4/10 is four 10-hour days every week with the fifth day off weekly; every calendar week is 40 hours so no special workweek definition is needed. A 9/80 has shorter days (9 hours) and a day off every other week, keeping Fridays staffed at half strength. Both need an alternative workweek election for non-exempt employees in California.

Can an employee on 9/80 work on their day off?

Yes, but for a non-exempt employee the hours count in the workweek containing that day and will usually be overtime, since the workweek already holds 40 hours. Most policies require advance approval for work on the regular day off and prohibit swapping the off day without changing groups permanently.
