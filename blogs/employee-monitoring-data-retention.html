How Long to Keep Employee Monitoring Data: Retention Rules

P Pavan
November 20, 2026 · 9 min read

Monitoring tools collect a lot of data, and most of it is useful for a short time. Screenshots, activity logs, location trails and camera footage that are kept indefinitely become a liability: they enlarge the damage of a breach, must be produced in lawsuits and access requests, and in the UK and EU breach the rule that personal data be kept no longer than necessary. Some time and pay data, on the other hand, must be kept for years. A retention schedule that separates the two is the cheapest privacy control an employer can adopt.

This guide sets out the legal rules, a model retention schedule by data type, vendor defaults to check, and how litigation holds interact with deletion. For payroll and time records specifically, see payroll record retention by state.

What the law requires

  • UK and EU GDPR: Article 5(1)(e) requires personal data to be kept no longer than necessary for its purpose. The ICO's 2023 monitoring guidance says employers must not keep monitoring data longer than necessary or "just in case," and need a retention schedule; the law does not set specific periods.
  • France (CNIL): workplace video footage should be kept no longer than one month, and a few days is usually enough; IT access logs are generally kept between six months and one year.
  • California (CCPA/CPRA): since January 1, 2023 the law covers employee data, and Civil Code 1798.100(a)(3) requires businesses to disclose how long they keep each category of personal information, or the criteria used, and not to keep it longer than reasonably necessary.
  • Illinois (BIPA): a written, public retention schedule for biometric identifiers, with destruction when the initial purpose is satisfied or within three years of the individual's last interaction, whichever comes first. The 2024 amendment limited damages but not the retention duty. See biometric privacy laws.
  • FLSA: payroll records for three years and time cards and wage computation records for two years (29 CFR 516.5 and 516.6). Time and attendance data captured by a monitoring tool may be the time record, which brings these minimums, and longer state periods, into play.
  • Notice laws: New York's Civil Rights Law 52-c, Connecticut and Delaware require notice of monitoring but set no retention period.
  • Sector rules: broker-dealers must keep business communications three years under SEC Rule 17a-4 (see off-channel communications); HIPAA requires six years for policies and certain documentation (see healthcare monitoring).

Vendor defaults to check

Monitoring vendors set retention by plan, and the defaults are often longer, or shorter, than you want. Examples from published plan details in November 2026: Hubstaff keeps screenshots from two weeks (free) to six months (Team), time logs for three years, with an add-on extending time logs to six years; Time Doctor keeps tracking data three months, six months or two years depending on plan; Monitask keeps screenshots one to two months; Apploye keeps data three to twelve months, with an add-on for longer; ActivTrak keeps data from 30 days on its free plan to one year or more on paid plans, with extra years sold as an add-on. Check whether the tool lets you set shorter periods than the plan maximum, whether deletion covers backups, and whether exports preserve the time records you are legally required to keep. Litigation holds and spoliation

Retention schedules stop when a dispute is reasonably anticipated. From that point, relevant monitoring data must be preserved, even if the schedule says delete. Under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 37(e), a court can order measures to cure prejudice from lost electronic information and, where a party acted with intent to deprive, instruct the jury to presume the data was unfavorable or dismiss the case. The rule cuts both ways: in Jones v. Riot Hospitality Group (9th Cir. 2024), an employee's discrimination case was dismissed because she deleted text messages and coordinated with witnesses to delete theirs. Build a hold procedure that can suspend automatic deletion for named employees or data types quickly, and document every hold and release.

How to build and run the schedule

  1. Inventory every monitoring data type and where it is stored, including vendor clouds and backups.
  2. Record the purpose of each and the shortest period that serves it.
  3. Overlay legal minimums (pay and time records, sector rules) and maximums (BIPA, GDPR necessity).
  4. Configure deletion in each tool and confirm it works.
  5. Publish the periods in the monitoring policy and, in California, the privacy notice; see the monitoring policy template.
  6. Set up a litigation hold process with legal and IT.
  7. Review annually and when tools or laws change.

Key takeaways

  • No US law sets a general retention period for monitoring data, but GDPR requires keeping it no longer than necessary, California requires disclosing retention periods, and Illinois caps biometric data at three years after last use.
  • Time and attendance data used for pay must be kept at least three years under the FLSA (two for time cards) and longer in many states.
  • A practical schedule: screenshots 30 days or less, activity logs 90 days to 6 months, CCTV 7 to 30 days, GPS 30 to 90 days, security logs 6 to 12 months, pay-related time records 3 to 6 years.
  • Vendor defaults vary by plan, from two weeks to several years; configure shorter periods where possible and confirm deletion reaches backups.
  • Litigation holds override the schedule once a dispute is anticipated; FRCP 37(e) allows severe sanctions for intentional deletion, as Jones v. Riot Hospitality (2024) shows.

Frequently asked questions

How long should employers keep employee monitoring data?

As short a time as its purpose allows. Common practice is 30 days or less for screenshots, 90 days to six months for activity logs, 7 to 30 days for CCTV, and 30 to 90 days for GPS, while time and attendance records used for pay must be kept at least three years under federal law and longer in some states.

Is there a legal limit on keeping monitoring data?

In the UK and EU, yes in principle: GDPR requires deleting personal data once no longer necessary, and regulators such as France's CNIL suggest specific limits such as one month for video. In the US, Illinois caps biometric data retention at three years after last use, and California requires disclosed, reasonable retention periods.

How long should CCTV footage be kept at work?

Typically 7 to 30 days unless footage is needed for an incident or investigation. France's CNIL says workplace video should not be kept more than a month. Footage relevant to a complaint or claim must be preserved under a litigation hold.

Do monitoring screenshots have to be kept for wage claims?

Not specifically, but if monitoring data is used as the record of hours worked, it becomes a time record that federal law requires keeping for two to three years and some states longer. Many employers keep time totals for the legal period and delete underlying screenshots much sooner.

What happens if an employer deletes monitoring data during a lawsuit?

If the data should have been preserved, a court can order measures to cure the prejudice and, where deletion was intended to deprive the other side, presume the data was unfavorable or dismiss claims or defenses under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 37(e). Automatic deletion must be suspended once litigation is reasonably anticipated.

Must employers tell employees how long monitoring data is kept?

In California, yes, under the CCPA notice requirements for employee data; in the UK and EU, privacy notices must state retention periods or criteria. Elsewhere it is good practice to include retention periods in the monitoring policy.
