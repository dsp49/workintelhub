Demand-Based Scheduling: Forecasting Staff From Volume

P Pavan
November 26, 2026 · 8 min read

Demand-based scheduling sets the number of people on each shift from forecast volume rather than habit: forecast how many customers, calls, orders or patients arrive in each hour, multiply by the time each one takes, and staff to that workload with a buffer for occupancy and absence. The schedule is then built to match the curve instead of a flat headcount.

The arithmetic is simple enough to do by hand, and the calculator on this page does it for one interval, including the Erlang C queueing model for work where customers wait in line. The harder parts are the forecast and the data behind it, which is where scheduling software earns its price; our review of workforce scheduling tools covers which products forecast and how well.

The short answer

Workload = volume x handle time. 60 calls an hour at 6 minutes each is 360 minutes of work, or 6 staff-hours, in that hour.

Divide by target occupancy so people are not busy every second: 6 / 0.85 rounds up to 8 on the floor.

Then add shrinkage for breaks, training and absence, and build shifts that cover each hour's number.

The method in five steps

  1. Pick the driver. The unit that creates work: calls or chats in a contact center, transactions or covers in retail and restaurants, admissions or census in care, orders picked in a warehouse. One driver per role.
  2. Forecast it by interval. Hourly is the usual grain; contact centers use 15 or 30 minutes. Start from the same weekday in recent weeks, then adjust for known events: promotions, paydays, holidays, weather, a new product.
  3. Convert volume to workload. Multiply by the average handle time, the full labor time per unit including after-work such as notes or bagging. The result is staff-hours of work per hour, which queueing texts call Erlangs.
  4. Apply a target. For work that can wait, cap occupancy, the share of paid time spent on the work. For work where customers queue, set a service level such as 80% answered within 20 seconds and let a queueing model find the staff. Use whichever gives the larger number.
  5. Add shrinkage and build shifts. Divide by (1 minus shrinkage) to get scheduled heads, then fit shift lengths and start times to the hourly curve. Auto-scheduling tools do this step; by hand, start with the peak and work outward.

Staffing calculator

Erlang C assumes random (Poisson) arrivals, exponentially distributed handle times, no abandonment, a steady rate within the hour and interchangeable staff. Real queues with abandonment usually need slightly fewer people than it says. One interval only; run it for each hour of the day.

Erlang C: what it does and what it assumes

Occupancy alone ignores waiting. If ten people are each busy 85% of the time, customers who arrive in a cluster still queue. Erlang C, the standard queueing model for contact-center staffing, estimates how long. With workload A (volume times handle time, in staff-hours per hour) and N staff, where N must exceed A:

  • Probability of waiting, Pw = [AN/N! x N/(N - A)] / [sum of Ak/k! for k = 0 to N - 1, plus AN/N! x N/(N - A)].
  • Service level, the share answered within T seconds = 1 - Pw x e-(N - A) x T / AHT.
  • Average speed of answer = Pw x AHT / (N - A).

The calculator computes Pw through the Erlang B recursion, which gives the same result without large factorials. We checked it against the direct formula: at 200 calls in an hour with a 180-second handle time (A = 10), 11 agents give a 68.2% chance of waiting and 39.0% answered in 20 seconds, 13 agents give 79.6%, and 14 agents are the first to clear an 80/20 target, at 88.8%.

Its assumptions are worth knowing before you trust the number:

  • Arrivals are random and independent within the interval. Bursty traffic, such as calls after an outage text, needs more staff than Erlang C says.
  • Nobody hangs up. Real callers abandon, which removes load, so Erlang C tends to overstate the staff needed when abandonment is material. Erlang A models abandonment.
  • The queue is unlimited and staff are interchangeable. Skill-based routing, where only some agents can take some work, breaks this.
  • The rate is steady across the interval. Use 15 or 30 minutes where volume swings within the hour.

For walk-in retail and restaurants, the queue is real but less measurable; most operations use the occupancy or labor-standard method and a sanity check against peak queues. The call center workforce management guide goes further into intervals, adherence and intraday re-forecasting.

A worked example: a store's Saturday

A store forecasts checkout transactions by hour from the last six Saturdays, adjusted for a promotion. Each transaction takes about 2.5 minutes of cashier time, and the manager caps cashier occupancy at 80%, accepting some queueing at peak.

A flat schedule of four cashiers all day would be overstaffed by two at 09:00 and short by two at 13:00, the hour that matters most for sales. The demand-based schedule uses staggered starts and a short midday shift to put six on at the peak. That is the whole idea: the same or fewer hours, placed where the work is. Where demand-based scheduling goes wrong

  • Forecasting from your own schedule. If you were understaffed, sales and answered calls were capped by staffing. Forecast demand, such as offered calls or footfall, not output.
  • Handle time that leaves out after-work. Notes, wrap-up, restocking and bagging are labor. Leaving them out understates workload.
  • Shrinkage guessed rather than measured. Pull a quarter of breaks, training, meetings, absence and leave from your own records. Industry rules of thumb are not your operation.
  • Ignoring schedule law. Fitting the curve tightly tempts managers to change shifts late. Where predictive scheduling laws apply, late changes carry premium pay, so the forecast has to be settled before the notice period.
  • Plugging gaps with on-call cover without pricing it. Standby rules can make on-call hours paid hours; see on-call scheduling and pay rules.
  • Never comparing forecast with actual. Track error by hour each week and adjust; a forecast nobody checks drifts.

Software that forecasts demand

You can run the method in a spreadsheet for one site. Software pays off when it reads the demand data automatically and builds the schedule from it. In the tools we ranked for the best workforce scheduling software, as reviewed in October 2026:

  • Deputy sets coverage from sales or foot-traffic forecasts and auto-schedules within break, overtime and predictive-scheduling rules; $5 to $9 per user with a $30 monthly minimum.
  • 7shifts drafts restaurant schedules against POS sales forecasts and a labor target; free for one location with 30 employees, paid plans per location.
  • Humanity, from TCP Software, schedules against patient or sales volume at enterprise scale; quote only.
  • When I Work Pro adds labor forecasting and custom rules at $5 per user, plus the clock add-on.

All of them depend on the feed: a forecast from a POS with clean transaction data works; one from a spreadsheet updated monthly does not. If you are still deciding whether you need forecasting at all, read workforce scheduling vs shift scheduling first.

Key takeaways

  • Demand-based scheduling sets staff per interval from forecast volume, not a flat headcount.
  • Workload equals volume times average handle time, expressed as staff-hours of work per hour.
  • Divide workload by a maximum occupancy for work that can wait; use Erlang C for a service-level target where customers queue, and staff to the larger number.
  • Erlang C assumes random arrivals, no abandonment and interchangeable staff; with real abandonment it tends to overstate staff slightly.
  • Add measured shrinkage last: scheduled heads equal staff needed divided by (1 minus shrinkage).
  • Forecast demand rather than past output, and compare forecast with actual every week.

Frequently asked questions

What is demand-based scheduling?

A scheduling method that sets how many people work in each interval from forecast demand, such as sales, calls, footfall or patient volume, multiplied by the labor time each unit takes, then builds shifts to match.

How do you calculate staff needed from volume?

Multiply volume per hour by average handle time in hours to get workload, divide by your maximum occupancy, and round up. For queued work, use Erlang C to find the staff that meets a service level, and take the larger figure. Then divide by (1 minus shrinkage) to get heads to schedule.

What is a good occupancy target?

It depends on the work and on how much queueing you accept. Very high occupancy leaves no slack for bursts and wears people out; lower occupancy costs more. Set it from your service goals and check it against measured queues, rather than copying a benchmark.

Is Erlang C accurate?

It is the standard starting point for queued work and accurate when its assumptions roughly hold: random arrivals, steady volume within the interval, no abandonment and interchangeable staff. With significant abandonment it tends to overstate staffing; with bursty arrivals or skill routing it can understate it.

What is shrinkage in scheduling?

The share of paid time when scheduled staff are not available for the core work: breaks, training, meetings, absence and leave. Measure it from your own records and add it after calculating staff needed.

Do I need software for demand-based scheduling?

Not for one site with stable data; a spreadsheet works. Software such as Deputy, 7shifts, Humanity or When I Work Pro pays off when it reads POS or volume data automatically and auto-builds schedules within labor rules.
