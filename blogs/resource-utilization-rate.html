Resource Utilization Rate: Formula, Benchmarks and Calculator

P Pavan
November 26, 2026 · 8 min read

Resource utilization rate is the share of a person's or team's available hours spent on billable or productive work: billable (or productive) hours divided by available hours, times 100. Someone with 36 available hours who spends 27 on client work has a utilization rate of 75 percent.

The formula is simple; the decisions around it are not. Which hours count as available, which count as productive, and what the target should be change the number more than anything the person does. This page gives the formula, a calculator that compares your rate with a target, a measured benchmark with its source, and a method for setting a target that holds up.

The resource utilization formula

Utilization rate (%) = billable or productive hours ÷ available hours × 100

Available hours = contracted hours in the period, minus leave, public holidays and sick time.

Billable or productive hours = hours spent on client work (billable utilization) or on planned project work (productive utilization).

Worked example. A consultant is contracted for 40 hours a week. Over a four-week month she takes two days of leave, so available hours are 160 minus 16, or 144. She records 101 billable hours. Her utilization is 101 ÷ 144 × 100 = 70.1 percent. The remaining 43 hours went to internal meetings, administration, training and business development, which is normal and should itself be tracked.

For a team, add the hours first and divide once. Averaging individual percentages gives part-time staff the same weight as full-time staff and distorts the result.

Available hours or hours worked: pick one and say so

Two denominators are in common use. Our billable hours glossary entry uses total hours available; our billable hours calculator uses hours actually worked. Both are legitimate, and they answer different questions.

DenominatorWhat it tells youWatch out for
Available hours (contracted less leave)How much of the capacity you are paying for goes to the work you planned. Best for capacity and resource planning.Overtime can push the rate above 100 percent; that is a warning, not an achievement.
Hours worked (recorded)How much of the time people actually put in was billable. Best for pricing and profitability.A person working 55 hours can show a healthy rate while burning out, because the long week is hidden in the denominator.

Whichever you use, write it on the report. A utilization figure without a stated denominator cannot be compared with anything, including last quarter's.

Resource utilization calculator

Available hours are contracted hours less leave, holidays and sickness for the same period as the billable hours. Use a week, a month or a quarter, but the same one for both.

Benchmarks: one measured figure, and planning ranges

Published utilization benchmarks are mostly targets firms set or surveys of what they say they achieve, and few name their method. The measured figure we can point to is from the legal sector: the Clio Legal Trends Report puts lawyers' utilization at 38 percent in 2025, with realization of 88 percent and collection of 93 percent, which works out to about 2.4 collected hours in an 8-hour day. It is a reminder of how far measured utilization can sit below the six billable hours a day many firms assume.

For planning, as distinct from benchmarking, our capacity planning templates and resource planning templates recommend 70 to 85 percent of available hours on committed work. That is a planning range, not an industry statistic: below it, demand estimates usually missed something; above it, there is no room for the incident, the urgent request or the person who leaves.

How to set a utilization target

  1. Start from real available hours. Subtract leave, holidays and expected sickness from contracted hours for the year. Our work year guide shows how quickly 52 weeks becomes about 46 or 47.
  2. Measure the non-billable load you cannot avoid. Meetings, support, administration, training and business development, from a few weeks of recorded time rather than from memory. Our resource planning templates find most people have 20 to 25 hours a week for planned project work after these; the team capacity planning guide covers how to measure the load.
  3. Set targets by role, not one number for everyone. A junior delivering client work, a manager running a team and a senior who also sells have different non-billable loads, so they need different targets.
  4. Check the target against revenue. Multiply available hours by the target, the billing rate and your realization. If it does not cover the fully loaded cost of the role, the problem is price or structure, not effort.
  5. Leave headroom. Plan inside the 70 to 85 percent range rather than at the top of it, and revisit after a quarter of actuals.

Billable utilization vs productive utilization

Professional services firms usually mean billable utilization: hours a client pays for. Internal teams, such as IT, product or operations, have no client invoice, so they measure productive utilization: hours on planned project work against available hours. The arithmetic is the same; what changes is the numerator. Define it once, in writing, before anyone starts tracking, so that time spent on a proposal or an internal tool is classified the same way by everyone.

If you bill, utilization is only the first of three ratios. Realization (the share of billable hours that survive write-downs) and collection (the share of invoices paid) multiply with it. The billable hours calculator runs all three and shows the effective hourly rate.

A team example, step by step

A five-person delivery team runs a four-week month. Four people are contracted for 40 hours a week and one for 24. Contracted hours are 4 × 160 plus 96, or 736. Leave and a public holiday take out 56 hours, so available hours are 680. The team records 486 hours on client projects.

StepCalculationResult
Contracted hours(4 × 40 + 24) × 4 weeks736
Less leave and holiday736 − 56680 available
Billable or productive hoursRecorded against client projects486
Team utilization486 ÷ 680 × 10071.5%
Hours at a 75% target680 × 0.75510, a gap of 24

A 24-hour gap across five people in a month is small. Before anyone is asked to bill more, check where it went: an onboarding week for a new client, a proposal, or project work recorded under an internal code. If the same gap appears three months running, it is spare capacity the team can sell or reassign; if the team is above target three months running, it is a hiring signal that the capacity plan should show.

Five mistakes that make utilization misleading

  • Not recording non-billable time. Without it there is no denominator and no evidence of where the rest of the week goes.
  • Planning at 100 percent. People planned at 100 percent run at 120, through evenings and weekends.
  • Publishing individual league tables. Ranking people by utilization pushes recorded hours up and realization down. Our guide to reporting team capacity to leadership keeps individual utilization out of planning documents for this reason.
  • Hiding long weeks. A healthy rate on a 55-hour week is a burnout signal, not a success. Track hours worked alongside the rate; see how to spot burnout in the data.
  • Averaging percentages. Sum the hours across the team, then divide.

Measuring utilization with software

A timesheet with a billable flag and a project code is enough to calculate utilization for a small team. As teams grow, the useful step is software that captures time automatically and shows capacity and utilization by team rather than by person. ActivTrak and Time Champ do this for desk-based teams; contact centers and shift operations use forecasting and scheduling engines instead. Our ranking of workforce optimization platforms covers both. For the bigger picture of where utilization fits, read what workforce optimization is and how WFO differs from WFM. Any tool that records activity needs a written monitoring policy before it goes live.

Key takeaways

  • Resource utilization rate = billable or productive hours divided by available hours, times 100.
  • Available hours are contracted hours less leave, holidays and sickness; say on the report whether you used available hours or hours worked.
  • For teams, sum the hours first and divide once; do not average individual percentages.
  • The Clio Legal Trends Report measured lawyers' utilization at 38 percent in 2025, well below what most firms assume.
  • Our planning templates recommend 70 to 85 percent of available hours on committed work, set by role and checked against revenue.
  • Report utilization with hours worked and, if you bill, with realization and collection.

Frequently asked questions

How do you calculate resource utilization rate?

Divide billable or productive hours by available hours for the same period and multiply by 100. A person with 144 available hours in a month who records 101 billable hours has a utilization rate of 70.1 percent.

What is a good resource utilization rate?

It depends on the role and the work. For planning committed project work, our capacity and resource planning templates recommend 70 to 85 percent of available hours. Measured figures can be much lower: the Clio Legal Trends Report put lawyers' utilization at 38 percent in 2025.

What is the difference between billable and productive utilization?

Billable utilization counts only hours a client pays for. Productive utilization counts hours on planned work, which is the measure internal teams use because they have no client invoice. The formula is the same; the numerator differs.

Can utilization be over 100 percent?

Yes, if more hours are recorded than were available, which means overtime or an available-hours figure set too low. Treat it as a warning about workload or data, not as high performance.

Should utilization be measured per person?

Per person is useful for the individual and their manager to plan workload. Publishing it as a ranking pushes recorded hours up and quality down, so team-level figures are better for planning and reporting.

What is the difference between utilization and capacity?

Capacity is the available hours a person or team has for planned work. Utilization is the share of those hours actually spent on billable or productive work. Capacity planning compares committed demand with capacity; utilization measures what happened.
