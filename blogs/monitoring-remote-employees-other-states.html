Monitoring Remote Employees in Other States and Countries

P Pavan
November 26, 2026 · 9 min read

When you monitor a remote employee, the rules that apply are generally those of the place where the employee works, not where your company is headquartered. A company in a state with no monitoring statute can still owe written notice to an employee working from Connecticut, Delaware, Maine or New York, and a US company monitoring someone who works in the European Union has to reckon with the GDPR.

This page sets out what changes when a remote team crosses state lines or borders: the four US notice laws, the extra limits Maine puts on monitoring in the home, audio recording consent, state privacy rights, and what the GDPR text itself says about employee monitoring. A short checker at the end lists the duties for the locations on your team. It is not legal advice; for any country outside the US, take local advice before you deploy.

The short answer

The employee's location decides the rule. Notice duties follow where the person works, so a distributed team inherits every state's rule at once.

Four US states have a monitoring notice statute: Connecticut, Delaware, Maine and New York. Everywhere else, wiretap, recording consent and privacy laws do the work.

Run one standard built to the strictest rule. One notice, acknowledged, with the most restrictive limits applied to everyone, is simpler than fifty versions.

The four US states with a monitoring notice law

The statutes below are the ones where electronic monitoring itself triggers a written notice duty. The details are on our employee monitoring laws by state page, which links each statute.

Two details matter for remote staff in particular. Connecticut's statute defines electronic monitoring as collecting information on the employer's premises, so how it reaches a home office is a question to put to counsel rather than assume either way. And the posting duties in Connecticut and New York were written for a workplace wall; for remote employees, the written, acknowledged notice is the part you can prove you gave.

Maine limits monitoring in the home

Maine's law, in force since July 14, 2026, is the one most directly aimed at remote work. It covers employer surveillance generally, including cameras, GPS, keystroke logging and biometric collection, and it restricts what may be monitored as well as requiring notice.

  • No audiovisual monitoring in the residence, personal vehicle or personal property unless the job genuinely requires it. A webcam check on a home worker falls squarely in that rule.
  • Employees may decline a request to install surveillance software on a personal device.
  • Notice repeats every year, so a notice given at hire in 2026 does not cover 2027.
  • The GPS exception is narrow. It covers employer-owned vehicles, not the employee's own car.

If you have one employee in Maine, these limits are the strictest rule on your team for anything involving cameras or personal devices, and the simplest course is to apply them to everyone.

What applies in the other states

Outside those four, monitoring a company device is broadly lawful, and the constraints come from laws not written with monitoring software in mind.

  • Audio recording consent. Roughly a dozen states require all parties to consent to recording a conversation. A tool that captures audio from a home office reaches family members and the person on the other end of a call, and the stricter state's rule applies to the recording.
  • Personal devices. Monitoring a personal laptop or phone needs consent and should be confined to a work profile or work accounts. Off-duty conduct and location limits in several states apply with more force once the device is the employee's own; our guide to whether an employer can track a laptop's location covers the device side.
  • State privacy rights. In California the CCPA gives employees the right to see what has been collected about them, and other states' privacy laws add access and deletion rights. Have an answer ready for the request.
  • Biometrics. Illinois's biometric statute carries a private right of action, which matters for any tool that uses facial recognition for remote clock-in. See our page on the Illinois workplace privacy rules.

The tools themselves differ on how well they support this: whether the employee can see their own record, whether screenshots can be blurred, whether the agent is visible. The remote team monitoring tools comparison sets those features out tool by tool.

Employees in the European Union: what the GDPR says

For staff outside the US, this page sticks to what can be read in the primary text. For the European Union that is the General Data Protection Regulation (EU) 2016/679. It is not an employment monitoring law as such, but monitoring is processing of personal data, and the regulation applies to it.

  • Reach (Article 3). The GDPR applies to processing "in the context of the activities of an establishment" in the EU, wherever the processing happens, and to a controller outside the EU whose processing relates to "the monitoring of their behaviour as far as their behaviour takes place within the Union". A US company monitoring someone working in the EU should assume it is covered and check which route applies.
  • National employment rules (Article 88). Member States may set more specific rules for employee data, and those rules must include safeguards with "particular regard to the transparency of processing" and "monitoring systems at the work place". The GDPR is the floor; each country can add to it, so a Germany-based employee and a Spain-based employee may come with different rules.
  • Data minimization (Article 5(1)(c)). Personal data must be "adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary" for the purpose. Collecting everything an agent can capture is hard to square with that.
  • A lawful basis (Article 6). One option is legitimate interests, which fails where they are "overridden by the interests or fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subject".
  • Information at collection (Article 13). The employee must be told, among other things, the purposes and legal basis of the processing when the data is collected.
  • Impact assessment (Article 35). Processing "likely to result in a high risk" to people's rights requires a data protection impact assessment before it starts.
  • Fines (Article 83(5)). Breaches of the basic principles, the lawful bases or data subjects' rights can carry fines up to EUR 20 million or 4% of worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher.

In practice, monitoring that a US employer treats as routine (continuous screenshots, keystroke logging, webcam checks) is the kind most likely to need an impact assessment and a strong justification in the EU. For other countries, including the UK, Canada, India and the Philippines, the rules differ by country and this page does not summarize them; get advice in the country where the person works before you switch anything on.

Running one standard across locations

  1. Know where everyone works. The remote work agreement fixes each person's primary location and requires approval before a move, which is what keeps this list accurate.
  2. Write one notice to the strictest rule. Say what is collected, what is not, who sees it and how long it is kept. Our monitoring consent form has wording that covers the acknowledgment states.
  3. Collect an acknowledgment from everyone. Delaware and New York require it; elsewhere it turns a disputed conversation into a dated record.
  4. Repeat it every year. Maine requires annual notice, and a yearly reissue keeps the notice in step with the tool's settings.
  5. Apply the strictest limits to everyone. No webcam monitoring in homes, no software on personal devices without a work profile, no audio without all-party consent.
  6. Treat contractors separately. Monitoring a contractor raises a classification question before a privacy one; see whether you can monitor independent contractors.

Location also changes pay rules for hourly remote staff, which is covered in remote employee overtime rules, and a team spread across zones needs a clear definition of the workday, covered in tracking hours across time zones. Key takeaways

  • Monitoring rules generally follow where the employee works, not where the company is based.
  • Connecticut, Delaware, Maine and New York have monitoring notice statutes; Delaware and New York require the employee's acknowledgment.
  • Maine bars audiovisual monitoring in an employee's home unless the job requires it, lets employees refuse software on personal devices, and requires notice every year.
  • Elsewhere, all-party audio consent in about a dozen states, personal device limits, the CCPA and Illinois biometric law do the work.
  • For staff in the EU, the GDPR applies, including data minimization, information at collection, impact assessments for high-risk processing, and national employment rules under Article 88.
  • Run one acknowledged notice built to the strictest rule on the team, reissued every year.

Frequently asked questions

Which state's monitoring law applies to a remote employee?

Generally the law of the state where the employee works. A company headquartered in a state with no monitoring statute can still owe notice to an employee working from Connecticut, Delaware, Maine or New York.

Do I need a separate monitoring notice for each state?

Not necessarily. One written notice that meets the strictest rule on your team, acknowledged by every employee and reissued each year, satisfies the acknowledgment requirements in Delaware and New York and the annual notice in Maine. Connecticut's posting rule still needs attention.

Can I use webcam monitoring on employees working from home?

In Maine, audiovisual monitoring in an employee's residence is barred unless the job genuinely requires it. Elsewhere it is not banned outright, but it reaches into the home, can capture other people, and can trigger audio consent rules. It needs a specific justification.

Does the GDPR apply to a US company monitoring an employee in Europe?

Article 3 applies the GDPR to processing in the context of an EU establishment and to controllers outside the EU that monitor the behavior of people in the EU. A US company monitoring someone working in the EU should assume it is covered, and check the national employment rules that Article 88 allows.

What are the GDPR fines for unlawful employee monitoring?

Breaches of the basic principles, the lawful bases or data subjects' rights can carry fines up to EUR 20 million or 4% of worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher, under Article 83(5).

Can I monitor an employee who works from another country outside the EU?

That depends on the country, and this page does not summarize non-EU rules. Get advice in the country where the person works, and in the meantime collect only what you could justify under the strictest rule you already follow.
