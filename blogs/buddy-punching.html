Buddy Punching: What It Is and How to Stop It

P Pavan
November 26, 2026 · 8 min read

Buddy punching is one employee clocking in or out on behalf of another who is not there: a coworker punches a late colleague in at the start of the shift, or punches someone out who left an hour early. It is the clearest form of time theft, because the record is false and both people know it. It is also one of the few forms that leaves checkable evidence.

It happens because most clocks identify a credential rather than a person. A PIN can be shared and a badge can be lent. The fix is a clock that checks who is punching, an edit trail managers actually read, and a schedule people can keep. This page covers how common it is according to the one survey with a stated method, which clock methods stop it, what each one costs you in legal obligations, and how to handle a case when you find one.

The short answer

It is real but badly measured. The best available figure is a July 2026 vendor survey in which one in four hourly workers admitted doing it in the past year. The widely repeated "75% of businesses" and "2.2% of payroll" figures have no traceable source.

PINs and badges cannot stop it. A photo at the kiosk, a face or fingerprint scan, or GPS on a phone punch can, in rising order of legal obligation.

Treat a proven case as falsification, not an attendance lapse. Investigate with HR, document it, and involve counsel before you act.

What counts as buddy punching

The core case is simple: a punch recorded under one employee's name by someone else, while the named employee was not working. It includes clocking in a colleague who is still in the car park, clocking out someone who has already gone home, and handing over a badge or PIN so a coworker can do either. Both people are involved. The absent employee gets paid for time not worked, and the colleague has falsified a pay record.

Some things look similar and are not. A supervisor correcting a missed punch with a note is an edit, not buddy punching, provided the edit is logged and the hours are true. An employee who forgot to clock out and fixes it the next day has made a timekeeping error. Our time theft guide sets out how to tell deliberate falsification from an honest mistake, and why most timesheet anomalies are the second.

How common it is: the one number with a method

Most buddy-punching statistics in circulation fail a source check. The claims that 75% of businesses are affected by time theft and that buddy punching costs 2.2% of payroll are attributed to an American Payroll Association survey that no page links and that we could not locate. Treat them as folklore.

One survey states its method. OnTheClock's employee admissions survey, run online by Centiment for OnTheClock among 831 US hourly workers from July 1 to 9, 2026, with a margin of error of about three points, found:

  • 25% had buddy punched in the past 12 months;
  • 41% of workers aged 18 to 29 had done so;
  • 31% thought it was no big deal or acceptable in some situations;
  • 71% said their employer already had a clear policy against it.

OnTheClock sells time clocks, so this is a vendor survey of a topic it has an interest in. It is still the best number available, and the last finding is the useful one: a written policy alone does not stop the behavior. The clock has to.

Why most clocks cannot prevent it

A clock that identifies a credential will accept that credential from anyone holding it. Paper cards, PIN pads, magnetic cards and RFID badges all work this way. Badges have a real advantage elsewhere: under Illinois law a badge is not a biometric identifier, so a badge system avoids the consent rules described below. Our page on RFID employee tracking covers that distinction. But against buddy punching, a badge is only as good as the habit of not lending it.

Phone apps without location checks have the same weakness in a different form: an employee who shares a login can be clocked in from anywhere. Adding a GPS stamp or a geofence closes most of that gap, because the punch now carries a place as well as a name.

Most employers do not need the top row of protection everywhere. A photo kiosk plus an edit trail stops most casual buddy punching on a single site. Biometric clocks earn their obligations where identity really matters: high turnover, large shifts and a history of problems. If you choose a biometric clock

A face or fingerprint clock collects a biometric identifier. The Illinois Biometric Information Privacy Act (740 ILCS 14) requires, before the first scan, written notice that the identifier is being collected, written notice of the purpose and how long it will be kept, a written release, and a published retention and destruction schedule. Damages are $1,000 per negligent and $5,000 per intentional or reckless violation, enforced by private lawsuit. Since August 2024, repeated scans of the same person by the same method count as one violation, which bounds the exposure but does not change the duty to get consent first.

Texas and Washington also require notice and consent, with enforcement by the state attorney general rather than private lawsuits. The practical rule is to meet the Illinois standard everywhere and to offer a non-biometric fallback, such as a badge or PIN with supervisor confirmation, for employees who cannot enroll or decline. Our biometric time clock entry covers the consent elements in full.

Six steps that prevent it without a new clock

  1. Turn on the edit trail and read it. Every change to a punch should record who made it and why. Most systems log this; few managers look.
  2. Review exceptions, not everything. Punches from an unusual device, location or time are the ones worth a glance.
  3. Put the policy in writing. Say plainly that punching for someone else is falsification of a pay record, for both people. The survey above shows a policy is necessary, not sufficient.
  4. Fix the schedule people are covering for. Repeated late arrivals covered by a friend often point to a start time someone cannot make. Flexibility removes the reason.
  5. Make missed punches easy to correct. If fixing a forgotten punch is painful, people ask a colleague instead. Most tools in our attendance software comparison let employees request a correction that a manager approves.
  6. Keep the rounding neutral. Clocks that round only in the employer's favor breed the sense that the system is unfair. The rule is covered in time clock rounding rules.

How to handle a case you find

Buddy punching is one of the few time-theft cases where intent is unambiguous, so it is one of the few where immediate termination can be proportionate. That makes the process matter more, not less.

  1. Secure the records. Punch logs, photos, location stamps, schedules and any door or login records that show where each person was.
  2. Investigate both employees. The one who was paid and the one who punched. Their accounts often differ.
  3. Involve HR and counsel before acting. Pay deductions and withheld final checks are limited by federal and state wage law, and the rules differ by state.
  4. Apply the policy consistently. The same finding should get the same outcome, whoever is involved. Document it with a written warning or termination record.
  5. Correct the payroll record. The FLSA requires accurate hours worked, so the false punch has to be fixed whatever the disciplinary outcome.

Key takeaways

  • Buddy punching is one employee clocking in or out for another who is not working. Both people have falsified a pay record.
  • The only figure with a stated method is OnTheClock's July 2026 survey of 831 hourly workers: 25% admitted doing it in the past year, 41% of those aged 18 to 29.
  • The 75% of businesses and 2.2% of payroll figures have no traceable primary source.
  • PINs and badges cannot prevent it. Photo kiosks deter it, GPS and geofenced phone punches mostly stop it, and biometric clocks stop it.
  • Biometric clocks carry written notice and consent duties in Illinois, with Texas and Washington rules too. Offer a non-biometric fallback.
  • Handle a proven case as falsification: secure the records, investigate both employees, and involve HR and counsel before acting.

Frequently asked questions

What is buddy punching?

One employee clocking in or out on behalf of another who is not there, for example punching in a late colleague or punching out someone who left early. It is deliberate falsification of a pay record by both people involved.

How common is buddy punching?

OnTheClock's survey of 831 US hourly workers, fielded July 1 to 9, 2026, found 25% admitted buddy punching in the past 12 months, rising to 41% of workers aged 18 to 29. It is a vendor survey, but it is the only figure we found with a stated method. The 75% and 2.2% of payroll figures on vendor pages have no traceable source.

Is buddy punching illegal?

There is no federal crime called buddy punching. It is falsification of time records, which employers can treat as grounds for discipline or termination, and deliberate falsification can carry further consequences depending on the facts and the state. Check with counsel before making pay deductions.

How do you prevent buddy punching?

Use a clock that verifies the person rather than a credential: a kiosk that takes a photo, a phone punch with GPS or a geofence, or a face or fingerprint clock. Then turn on the edit trail, review exceptions, put the rule in writing and fix schedules people are covering for.

Do biometric time clocks stop buddy punching?

Yes, because the credential is the person. They also bring written notice and consent duties under Illinois BIPA, and notice and consent rules in Texas and Washington, so get the release signed before enrollment and offer a fallback for employees who cannot or will not enroll.

Can you fire an employee for buddy punching?

Usually yes, where it is proven, because it is deliberate falsification. Investigate both employees, document the evidence, apply the policy consistently and involve HR and counsel first, particularly before deducting pay or withholding a final check.
