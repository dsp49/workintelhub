Wage Theft Statistics (2026): How Much Is Stolen and How Much Is Recovered

P Pavan
October 28, 2026 · 13 min read

Wage theft is an employer failing to pay what the law requires: minimum wage, overtime, pay for all hours worked, meal break premiums, tips, or treating an employee as a contractor. The best national estimate is that minimum wage violations alone take more than $15 billion a year from about 2.4 million workers in the ten largest states and more nationally, roughly $3,300 a year from each affected worker. Against that, the US Department of Labor recovered $259 million in back wages in fiscal 2025 for 176,957 workers, its best year since 2020, with 611 investigators, the fewest in 52 years.

This page gives every widely cited wage theft number with its source, sample and date, separates the figures that measure different things (minimum wage violations, all violations, recoveries, damages), lists the states that have made wage theft a crime, and explains the 2025 federal enforcement changes. It is about employers underpaying workers; employee time theft is a different subject with no comparable data, covered on our time theft statistics page.

How much is stolen

The Economic Policy Institute's 2017 study by David Cooper and Teresa Kroeger remains the most cited estimate and the most misquoted. Using Current Population Survey data for the ten most populous states, it found that 17% of eligible low-wage workers were paid less than the applicable minimum wage, 2.4 million people losing $8 billion a year, or $3,300 each for year-round workers. Because those states hold 53% of US employment, EPI extrapolated to more than $15 billion a year nationally. The figure covers only minimum wage violations; unpaid overtime, off-the-clock work, meal break violations, tip theft and misclassification are extra and were not estimated. The Rutgers and Northwestern Workplace Justice Lab's May 2025 brief reached a similar order of magnitude by a different route: at least 4 million workers paid below the minimum wage each year, losing about $3,000 each, more than $13 billion in total.

Older and narrower studies fill in the texture. The 2008 Broken Laws survey of 4,387 workers in low-wage industries in Chicago, Los Angeles and New York, published by the National Employment Law Project and partners in September 2009, found 68% had suffered at least one pay violation in the previous week, 26% had been paid below the minimum wage, 76% of those who worked over 40 hours had not received the legal overtime rate, and 70% of those who came early or stayed late were not paid for it; the average loss was $51 of $339 in weekly pay, about $2,634 of $17,616 a year. It is a three-city sample from before the last recession and should be cited as such. NELP's December 2021 estimate put wage theft from workers earning under $13 an hour who were covered by forced arbitration clauses at $9.27 billion in 2019, and EPI cites an estimate that 98% of such workers never file a claim. California's Labor Commissioner has estimated that misclassification alone costs the state more than $7 billion a year in lost revenue.

The comparison that circulates, that wage theft exceeds all robberies, burglaries and larcenies combined, rests on the FBI's property crime loss figure for 2015 of about $12.7 billion set against EPI's $15 billion. A 2026 American Journal of Public Health article by J. Paul Leigh repeats the comparison with updated figures. It is a fair illustration of scale, but the two numbers come from different years and methods and should not be presented as a measured ratio.

These are the Wage and Hour Division's "back wages" series. The department's press releases use a larger "back wages and damages" figure, $273 million for FY2024 and $274 million for FY2023, which adds liquidated damages, and secondary sites have inflated FY2025 to $295 million. Use one series and say which. The average recovery per worker in FY2025 was $1,465. Fair Labor Standards Act back wages specifically rose from just under $150 million in FY2024 to over $184 million in FY2025, while the number of compliance actions concluded fell slightly to just under 17,000.

Federal recoveries are a fraction of the total. EPI counted $3.24 billion recovered from 2017 to 2020 across the Labor Department ($1.156 billion for more than a million workers, averaging $1,101), 41 state agencies and the District of Columbia ($558.2 million, at least $126.1 million of it in 2020) and the ten largest private class action settlements each year ($1.5 billion). EPI itself warns that the recovery figure "is not representative of all wage theft", and dividing four years of recoveries into one year of estimated minimum wage violations produces a number that looks precise and is not. The defensible statement is that recoveries are a small fraction of the losses. The Duane Morris class action review puts the ten largest wage and hour settlements of 2025 alone at $430.6 million, and California's Labor and Workforce Development Agency received 8,846 Private Attorneys General Act notices in fiscal 2024-25.

Where it happens

In FY2025 the Wage and Hour Division resolved 4,088 violations in food services, recovering more than $42 million, and 2,370 in healthcare, recovering more than $53 million, the two largest sectors by dollars. Agriculture, construction, hotels, retail, car washes, garment manufacturing and home care round out the division's priority industries, which share tipped or piece-rate pay, subcontracting chains and workforces unlikely to complain. The violation types are stable across years: unpaid overtime through misclassifying hourly workers as salaried exempt, off-the-clock work before and after shifts, automatic meal deductions for breaks not taken, illegal tip pools and tip credits taken without notice, and independent contractor misclassification. Our meal and rest break laws and 1099 vs W-2 pages cover the two most expensive categories.

Enforcement capacity

The Wage and Hour Division had 1,232 investigators in 1978, 674 at the end of the Biden administration, and 611 on May 14, 2025 according to the Rutgers and Northwestern brief, with further departures through deferred resignations not disclosed. That is roughly one investigator for every 278,000 covered workers; the division's budget of about $315 million was about one fifteenth of Immigration and Customs Enforcement's enforcement and removal budget. The paradox of FY2025 is that recoveries rose to a five-year high while headcount fell, because the division closed fewer but larger cases and collected $58.7 million in civil money penalties, the most in the series. Claims that enforcement has collapsed need that caveat; claims that it is adequate need the headcount.

Prosecutions remain rare everywhere; the laws are used mostly as leverage in agency settlements and in a handful of high-profile cases a year. On June 25, 2025 the Labor Department published a plan on criminal regulatory offenses signaling a narrower federal use of criminal referrals.

2025 and 2026 enforcement changes

  • PAID relaunched (July 24, 2025). The Payroll Audit Independent Determination program lets employers self-audit, report violations and pay back wages within 15 days without liquidated damages or civil penalties, with a release of the federal claims paid. It is closed to employers under investigation or in litigation and to those found in violation in the prior three years, and it now covers Family and Medical Leave Act violations. Employees may decline the payment and sue, and state claims survive.
  • Liquidated damages in administrative cases. The division has indicated it will no longer seek liquidated damages in pre-litigation settlements, returning to the pre-2021 practice; it still seeks them in court.
  • Staffing at 611 investigators in May 2025 and falling; no 2026 count had been published by October 2026.
  • State activity continued to grow: California's Labor Commissioner issued more than 3,600 notices in 2025 and citations including $2.3 million against Los Angeles developers; New York's Attorney General recovered about $1.03 million for 250 cleaners at Rikers Island; Colorado's SB 26-121 raises penalties for agricultural wage theft from January 1, 2027.

Numbers that are quoted wrongly

  • "$3.3 billion in wage theft." There is no such EPI figure. $3,300 is the annual loss per worker; $8 billion is the ten-state total; $15 billion is the national extrapolation.
  • "$15 billion stolen from 2.4 million workers." Mixes the national dollar figure with the ten-state worker count.
  • "$15 billion in wage theft." It is minimum wage violations only; total wage theft is higher and unmeasured.
  • "$273 million recovered in FY2024" beside "$259 million in FY2025." Different series; the FY2024 back-wages-only figure is $202.7 million.
  • "$295 million recovered in FY2025." Not a Labor Department figure.
  • "68% of low-wage workers suffer wage theft." A 2008 three-city sample presented as current national data.
  • "Wage theft exceeds all property crime." Fair as an illustration, wrong as a measured comparison across years.
  • Time theft figures presented as wage theft, or the reverse. Different actors, different data.

What the data means for employers

Almost every category in the enforcement data is a recordkeeping failure before it is a theft: hours not captured, breaks deducted that were not taken, bonuses left out of the overtime rate, exempt classifications never reviewed. The fix is the record. Hours worked each day and week are required under 29 CFR 516.2 and are the only defense when a worker's estimate is the alternative evidence; where records are missing, courts accept the worker's reasonable estimate under Anderson v. Mt. Clemens Pottery (1946). Our time card calculator and overtime calculator cover the arithmetic, the payroll error statistics page covers the error rates, and the year-end payroll checklist includes the annual exempt review. A PAID self-audit is now available to employers who find a problem first.

Key takeaways

  • EPI's 2017 study found 2.4 million workers in the ten largest states paid below the minimum wage, losing $8 billion a year ($3,300 each), and extrapolated to more than $15 billion nationally for minimum wage violations alone. Rutgers and Northwestern (2025) estimate at least 4 million workers and more than $13 billion.
  • The Labor Department recovered $259.3 million in back wages for 176,957 workers in FY2025, the most since FY2020, up from $202.7 million in FY2024; its press figures add liquidated damages and are higher.
  • All channels combined, EPI counted $3.24 billion recovered over 2017 to 2020, a small fraction of estimated losses; the comparison should not be stated as a precise rate.
  • The Wage and Hour Division had 611 investigators in May 2025, a 52-year low, about one per 278,000 workers, yet FY2025 recoveries and penalties ($58.7 million) rose.
  • Food services (more than $42 million) and healthcare (more than $53 million) were the largest recovery sectors in FY2025.
  • Colorado, Minnesota, California, New York and Connecticut treat wage theft above a threshold as a felony; prosecutions are rare.
  • The PAID self-audit program returned on July 24, 2025, letting employers pay back wages without liquidated damages or penalties if they are not already under investigation.

Frequently asked questions

How much wage theft occurs in the US each year?

The Economic Policy Institute estimated in 2017 that minimum wage violations alone cost workers more than $15 billion a year, extrapolating from 2.4 million affected workers losing $8 billion in the ten most populous states. The Rutgers and Northwestern Workplace Justice Lab estimated in 2025 that at least 4 million workers lose more than $13 billion a year to minimum wage violations. Unpaid overtime, off-the-clock work and misclassification are additional and unmeasured nationally.

How much did the Department of Labor recover in 2025?

$259.3 million in back wages for 176,957 workers in fiscal 2025, according to Wage and Hour Division enforcement data, with $58.7 million in civil money penalties. That is up from $202.7 million in FY2024 and the highest since FY2020. The department's press releases add liquidated damages and show larger figures.

What is the most common form of wage theft?

In Labor Department enforcement data, unpaid overtime is the most common violation by dollars, usually through misclassifying hourly workers as exempt or failing to count all hours. Minimum wage violations, off-the-clock work, unlawful meal deductions, tip violations and independent contractor misclassification follow. The 2008 Broken Laws survey found 76% of low-wage workers who worked over 40 hours were not paid the overtime rate.

Which industries have the most wage theft?

In FY2025 the Wage and Hour Division recovered more than $53 million in healthcare and more than $42 million in food services, the two largest sectors. Agriculture, construction, hotels, retail, car washes, garment manufacturing and home care are the division's other priority industries.

How many investigators does the Wage and Hour Division have?

611 as of May 14, 2025, according to the Rutgers and Northwestern Workplace Justice Lab, the fewest since 1973 and down from 1,232 in 1978 and 674 in January 2025. That is about one investigator for every 278,000 covered workers. No 2026 count had been published by October 2026.

Is wage theft a crime?

In some states. Colorado (2019), Minnesota (2019), California (AB 1003, 2022), New York (2023) and Connecticut treat wage theft above a dollar threshold as a felony, and Illinois, New Jersey, Hawaii, Rhode Island and Virginia provide for imprisonment. Federally and in most states it is a civil matter, and criminal prosecutions are rare everywhere.

What is the PAID program?

The Payroll Audit Independent Determination program, relaunched by the Labor Department on July 24, 2025, lets employers self-audit, report wage violations and pay back wages within 15 days without liquidated damages or civil penalties, in exchange for a release of the federal claims paid. It is not available to employers under investigation, in litigation, or found in violation within the prior three years.

How much of stolen wages is recovered?

A small fraction. EPI counted $3.24 billion recovered across the Labor Department, state agencies and major class actions over 2017 to 2020, against an estimated $15 billion a year in minimum wage violations alone. The figures cover different years and scopes, so a precise percentage cannot be stated, and EPI cautions that recoveries are not representative of all wage theft.
