Employee Monitoring Laws in the UK: What Employers Can Do

P Pavan
November 12, 2026 · 14 min read

Monitoring employees is legal in the United Kingdom, but unlike in most of the United States it is regulated before it starts. Any monitoring that collects information about identifiable workers is processing of personal data under the UK General Data Protection Regulation and the Data Protection Act 2018, so the employer needs a lawful basis, must tell workers what is collected and why, must show the monitoring is necessary and proportionate, and must carry out a data protection impact assessment before anything intrusive is switched on. The Information Commissioner's Office published detailed guidance on monitoring workers in October 2023 and issued its first enforcement action against an employer's biometric attendance system in February 2024.

This guide, written for US readers with UK staff and for UK employees who want to know their rights, covers the legal framework (UK GDPR, the DPA 2018, the Human Rights Act, the interception rules), what the ICO guidance requires in practice, the 2025 and 2026 changes under the Data (Use and Access) Act, the rules for specific tools (CCTV, email and browsing, keystroke and screen capture, biometrics, vehicle tracking, personal devices), covert monitoring, worker rights and remedies, and how UK and US rules compare. For the US position, see employee monitoring laws by state.

The legal framework

Five sources of law govern workplace monitoring in the UK.

  • UK GDPR and the Data Protection Act 2018. Monitoring data is personal data. Article 5 requires processing to be lawful, fair and transparent, limited to specified purposes, minimized, accurate, time-limited and secure. Article 6 requires a lawful basis; the ICO says consent is unlikely to be valid in employment because of the imbalance of power, so employers rely on legitimate interests (with a documented balancing test), legal obligation or contract. Article 9 adds conditions for special category data (health, biometrics used for identification, trade union membership). Article 35 requires a DPIA for high-risk processing. Articles 13 and 14 require privacy information to be given to workers.
  • Human Rights Act 1998, Article 8. The right to respect for private life and correspondence applies directly to public-sector employers and informs how tribunals read the law for private ones. In Bărbulescu v Romania (Grand Chamber, September 5, 2017) the European Court of Human Rights held that an employer's monitoring of an employee's messaging account engaged Article 8 and set out the factors: prior notice, the extent and intrusiveness of monitoring, legitimate reasons, whether less intrusive means were available, the consequences for the employee, and safeguards.
  • Investigatory Powers Act 2016. Section 3 makes it an offense to intercept communications in the course of transmission without lawful authority. The Investigatory Powers (Interception by Businesses etc. for Monitoring and Record-keeping Purposes) Regulations 2018 (SI 2018/356, in force June 27, 2018, replacing the Lawful Business Practice Regulations 2000) authorize interception on a business's own systems for listed purposes: establishing facts, compliance, standards, national security, crime prevention, detecting unauthorized use and ensuring system security, provided the employer has made all reasonable efforts to inform users that interception may take place.
  • Employment law. The implied term of mutual trust and confidence, unfair dismissal law (evidence from unlawful monitoring can still be admissible but the manner of obtaining it goes to fairness), and the Employment Relations Act's protections for trade union activity.
  • Equality Act 2010. Monitoring and the decisions built on it must not discriminate; algorithmic productivity scoring that disadvantages disabled workers or those with caring responsibilities is the live issue.

What the ICO guidance requires

The ICO's guidance "Employment practices and data protection: monitoring workers," published October 3, 2023, replaced the monitoring chapter of the 2011 Employment Practices Code. Its core requirements:

  1. Define the purpose and be able to show the monitoring is necessary for it. "We might need it" is not a purpose.
  2. Identify a lawful basis and, for legitimate interests, document the three-part test: purpose, necessity, balancing against workers' rights and expectations.
  3. Be transparent. Workers must be told what is monitored, how, why, who sees the data and how long it is kept, in a way they will actually see, before monitoring starts. The ICO expects a specific monitoring notice, not a line in a 40-page handbook.
  4. Carry out a DPIA for monitoring that is likely to be high risk, which the ICO says includes most systematic monitoring, anything covert, biometrics and any use of the data for automated decisions or inferences about workers.
  5. Minimize. Collect the least data that achieves the purpose; monitor the system, not the person, where that works; avoid continuous capture when sampling would do.
  6. Respect private life. Workers have a reasonable expectation of privacy even at work, including in personal communications sent from work devices and in home environments during remote work. Monitoring must not capture private communications beyond what is unavoidable, and must stop when it becomes clear a communication is personal.
  7. Handle special category data (health inferred from browsing, biometrics, union membership) only with an Article 9 condition and an appropriate policy document.
  8. Automated decisions. Workers have rights where monitoring data feeds decisions with legal or similarly significant effects; a human must be able to review.
  9. Retention and security. Set and enforce deletion periods; restrict access to those who need it.
  10. Consult workers or their representatives before introducing monitoring. Not a strict legal requirement for private employers, but the ICO calls it good practice and the TUC's polling found 82% of workers back a legal duty to consult.

The guidance also covers specific scenarios: homeworking (the home is a private space and household members are not workers), vehicle tracking (do not track when a vehicle is available for private use), and the use of monitoring data for purposes other than the one notified, which is usually unlawful.

Data protection impact assessments

A DPIA describes the processing, assesses necessity and proportionality, identifies risks to individuals and sets out mitigations. For monitoring, the ICO expects it to be done before deployment, to involve the data protection officer where there is one, to consider consulting workers, and to be revisited when the tool or purpose changes. If the DPIA identifies a high residual risk that cannot be mitigated, the employer must consult the ICO before proceeding. In practice, a DPIA for productivity software should answer: what exactly is captured (screenshots, keystrokes, URLs, app titles, idle time, webcam), at what frequency, whether personal use is possible on the device, who can see raw data versus aggregates, what decisions are made from it, how long it is kept, whether workers can see their own data, and what the less intrusive alternatives were and why they were rejected. The monitoring policy template includes a DPIA skeleton.

Covert monitoring

Secret monitoring is lawful only in exceptional circumstances: where the employer has grounds to suspect criminal activity or equivalent malpractice, where telling the worker would prejudice its prevention or detection, and where the monitoring is authorized by senior management, targeted at the suspected individuals and activity, limited in time, and stopped when the investigation ends. It must never be used in toilets or changing areas, and information gathered incidentally about other matters or other people should be disregarded unless it reveals something no reasonable employer could ignore. A DPIA is still required, documented before the monitoring begins. Private employers do not need authorization under the Regulation of Investigatory Powers Act 2000, which governs public authorities, but the proportionality test is the same in substance. Evidence from unlawful covert monitoring may still be admitted by a tribunal, but the employer risks a damages claim and an ICO complaint, and dismissals built on it are more likely to be found unfair.

What the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 changed

The Data (Use and Access) Act received Royal Assent on June 19, 2025, with the main data protection amendments brought into force on February 5, 2026 and a complaints-handling duty from June 19, 2026. For monitoring, the relevant changes are:

  • Recognized legitimate interests: a new list of purposes (national security, emergencies, crime prevention, safeguarding) that need no balancing test. Workplace monitoring is not on it, so the balancing test still applies to employers.
  • Automated decision-making: the general prohibition on solely automated decisions with significant effects is replaced, for non-special-category data, by a requirement for safeguards (information, the right to make representations, human intervention and the right to contest). Employers using monitoring data in automated scheduling, scoring or discipline must build those safeguards in rather than avoid the decision altogether.
  • Subject access requests: the duty to search is now "reasonable and proportionate," and the clock can pause while identity or scope is clarified. Workers' requests for their monitoring data remain valid and are the most common way employees find out what was collected.
  • Complaints: from June 19, 2026 organizations must have a process for data protection complaints and respond within 30 days; workers should complain to the employer first and then to the ICO.
  • The regulator: the ICO is being reconstituted as the Information Commission, with the same powers.

The ICO's monitoring guidance has not been rewritten for the Act; its requirements stand, read alongside the new automated-decision rules.

Enforcement: Serco and the fine levels

On February 23, 2024 the ICO issued enforcement notices against Serco Leisure, Serco Jersey and seven community leisure trusts, ordering them to stop using facial recognition and fingerprint scanning to monitor the attendance of more than 2,000 employees at 38 leisure centers and to destroy the biometric data within three months. The ICO found the processing was neither necessary nor proportionate because less intrusive means, such as ID cards or fobs, were available, and that employees had not been offered an alternative or properly informed. No fine was imposed, but it was the first ICO action against an employer's monitoring of its own staff and it set the standard for biometrics at work. The ICO has since published guidance on biometric recognition and on employment records (final March 11, 2025), with recruitment guidance in draft.

The maximum fine under the UK GDPR is the higher of £17.5 million or 4% of global annual turnover for the most serious infringements, and £8.7 million or 2% for others. Fines against employers for monitoring have so far been rare; enforcement notices, reprimands and the reputational cost have done the work. Individuals can also claim compensation in the courts for material or non-material damage, including distress.

Prevalence figures explain the regulator's interest: a TUC poll of 2,209 workers found 60% had been subject to some form of surveillance in the previous year, up from 53% in 2020; the Chartered Management Institute's 2025 survey of 900 managers found a third of UK firms using monitoring software on home workers; and an ExpressVPN survey of 1,000 UK employers in January 2025 reported 85% monitoring online activity and 29% having disciplined or dismissed someone on the basis of recorded messages. Treat the vendor-commissioned figures with the usual caution.

Worker rights and remedies

  • To be informed: a privacy notice describing the monitoring, before it starts.
  • Access: a subject access request returns the monitoring data held about you (screenshots, logs, scores), free of charge, within one month (extendable), subject to the reasonable-and-proportionate search standard and third-party exemptions.
  • Objection: where the lawful basis is legitimate interests, you can object and the employer must stop unless it shows compelling grounds.
  • Automated decisions: the right to information, to make representations and to a human review.
  • Complaint: to the employer (30-day response from June 2026), then the ICO, which can investigate and order changes.
  • Compensation: in the county court or High Court for breaches causing damage or distress; Article 8 claims against public bodies.
  • Employment tribunal: unfair dismissal where monitoring evidence was gathered unlawfully or the process was unfair; discrimination where monitoring outcomes disadvantage protected groups; detriment for trade union activity.
  • Consultation: through a recognized union or information and consultation body, where one exists.

In practice, a worker who suspects undisclosed monitoring should ask HR in writing for the privacy notice and the DPIA for the monitoring, then make a subject access request if the answer is unsatisfactory. A compliance checklist for employers

  1. Write down the purpose of each monitoring tool and why it is necessary.
  2. Choose and document the lawful basis; run the legitimate interests balancing test.
  3. Complete a DPIA before deployment and review it annually.
  4. Publish a specific monitoring privacy notice and get evidence that workers saw it.
  5. Consult workers or their representatives.
  6. Configure for minimization: aggregates over raw data, sampling over continuous capture, no personal communications, no private areas, privacy switches on vehicles.
  7. Offer alternatives to biometrics and record why biometrics are needed if used.
  8. Set retention periods and automate deletion.
  9. Restrict access and log it.
  10. Build human review into any decision that uses monitoring data.
  11. Write a covert-monitoring procedure requiring senior authorization and a DPIA.
  12. Prepare for subject access requests and the 30-day complaints process.

Key takeaways

  • Monitoring is lawful in the UK but regulated in advance: it is processing of personal data under the UK GDPR and the Data Protection Act 2018, so the employer needs a lawful basis (usually legitimate interests, not consent), must inform workers before it starts, and must show necessity and proportionality.
  • The ICO's October 2023 guidance expects a specific monitoring notice, a data protection impact assessment for anything systematic, covert, biometric or used for automated decisions, minimization, respect for private communications, and consultation with workers.
  • Interception of communications on business systems is authorized by the 2018 Regulations for listed purposes if users have been told; Bărbulescu v Romania supplies the Article 8 factors tribunals apply.
  • Covert monitoring is allowed only in exceptional circumstances with grounds to suspect crime or serious malpractice, senior authorization, targeting and time limits, never in private areas.
  • The Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 (in force February 5, 2026) replaced the ban on significant automated decisions with safeguards, made subject access searches 'reasonable and proportionate', and added a 30-day complaints duty from June 19, 2026; monitoring remains outside the recognized legitimate interests list.
  • The ICO's February 2024 enforcement notices against Serco Leisure ended biometric attendance monitoring of 2,000-plus staff because cards or fobs would have done; maximum fines are £17.5 million or 4% of turnover.
  • Compared with the US, the UK requires notice everywhere, justification for every tool, access to one's own monitoring data, alternatives to biometrics and human review of decisions; US monitoring stacks usually need reconfiguring for UK staff.

Frequently asked questions

Is it legal for employers to monitor employees in the UK?

Yes, if the monitoring complies with the UK GDPR and Data Protection Act 2018: a lawful basis, prior notice to workers, a documented purpose, necessity and proportionality, a data protection impact assessment for intrusive monitoring, and respect for private communications. The ICO's 2023 guidance on monitoring workers sets out the detail.

Do UK employers have to tell employees they are being monitored?

Yes. Transparency is a core principle of the UK GDPR and the ICO expects a specific notice describing what is monitored, how, why, who sees the data and for how long, given before monitoring starts. The only exception is covert monitoring in exceptional circumstances, such as a specific suspicion of crime, with senior authorization and time limits.

Can my employer read my emails in the UK?

Business email on business systems can be monitored for the purposes listed in the 2018 interception regulations (compliance, standards, crime prevention, security) if staff have been told. Content review must stop when a message is clearly personal, and monitoring a personal webmail account is almost never justified. Bărbulescu v Romania requires notice, limited scope and consideration of less intrusive means.

Can UK employers use keystroke logging or screenshots?

Only with a strong, specific justification. The ICO treats keystroke logging and screen capture as highly intrusive, requiring a DPIA, a serious need such as a regulated trading environment or a fraud investigation, targeting, time limits and no capture of passwords or personal communications. General productivity tracking does not meet the bar.

Is biometric clocking-in legal in the UK?

Only where it is necessary and proportionate and workers are offered an alternative. Biometric data used for identification is special category data under Article 9. In February 2024 the ICO ordered Serco Leisure and seven trusts to stop facial recognition and fingerprint attendance monitoring of more than 2,000 staff because ID cards or fobs would have achieved the purpose.

Can my employer monitor me working from home?

Within limits. The home is a private space, so the ICO expects employers to use output-based measures, to avoid continuous screen capture and webcam monitoring, and never to capture household members. Monitoring must be proportionate to a documented purpose and disclosed in advance, the same as in the office, with the home setting weighing against intrusive tools.

How can I find out what monitoring data my employer holds about me?

Make a subject access request under the UK GDPR. The employer must respond within one month (extendable by two for complex requests) with a copy of your personal data, including monitoring logs, screenshots and scores, subject to a reasonable and proportionate search and exemptions for other people's data. Ask also for the privacy notice and the DPIA covering the monitoring.

What changed under the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025?

From February 5, 2026: a list of recognized legitimate interests that skip the balancing test (monitoring is not on it); automated decisions with significant effects are allowed for non-special-category data if safeguards exist, including human intervention and the right to contest; subject access searches need only be reasonable and proportionate. From June 19, 2026 organizations must handle data protection complaints within 30 days.
