Remote Employee Overtime: Who Owes What When Work Happens at Home

P Pavan
November 26, 2026 · 10 min read

A non-exempt remote employee is owed overtime on exactly the same terms as one in the office: time and a half the regular rate for every hour over 40 in the workweek, counting all work the employer knows or has reason to believe is happening, including the email answered at 9 p.m. On top of that federal floor, the overtime law of the state where the employee does the work applies, which is how a company with no daily overtime rule ends up paying California's.

This page covers what counts as hours worked at home under the Fair Labor Standards Act, what the Department of Labor's Field Assistance Bulletin 2020-5 on teleworkers asks employers to do, how state law follows the worker, and a calculator for what unlogged after-hours minutes add up to.

The short answer

Home hours are hours. Work an employer knows or has reason to believe is being done at home counts, whether or not it was requested.

A reporting procedure is the employer's main defense. Give employees a reasonable way to report unscheduled time, pay for all of it, and do not discourage its use.

The worker's state can add daily overtime. The FLSA lets stricter state law apply, so where the employee sits matters as much as where the company is.

What counts as hours worked at home

Three short regulations decide this, and they were written for exactly this situation long before remote work was common.

  • 29 CFR 785.11: "Work not requested but suffered or permitted is work time." The reason the employee kept working "is immaterial".
  • 29 CFR 785.12: the rule "is also applicable to work performed away from the premises or the job site, or even at home. If the employer knows or has reason to believe that the work is being performed, he must count the time as hours worked."
  • 29 CFR 785.13: it is management's duty to see that work it does not want is not performed. "The mere promulgation of a rule against such work is not enough."

For a remote team that means the late Slack reply, the weekend fix, the hour spent finishing a report after logging off all need an answer: either they are recorded and paid, or the employer takes steps to stop them. The ordinary break rules still apply at home. Rest breaks of 5 to 20 minutes count as hours worked under 29 CFR 785.18, while a bona fide meal period of 30 minutes or more, fully relieved of duty, does not under 29 CFR 785.19. The hours worked guide covers rounding and the rest of Part 785.

What DOL Field Assistance Bulletin 2020-5 says

The Department of Labor issued FAB 2020-5 on August 24, 2020 to address employers' obligation "to exercise reasonable diligence in tracking teleworking employees' hours of work". It was prompted by the shift to telework during COVID-19, but it says it "also applies to other telework or remote work arrangements". Its main points:

  1. Knowledge can be actual or constructive. An employer knows an employee's scheduled hours and any hours reported to it; it has constructive knowledge of unscheduled hours it "should have acquired knowledge of" through reasonable diligence.
  2. A reporting procedure is one way to be diligent. Providing "a reasonable reporting procedure for non-scheduled time and then compensating employees for all reported hours of work, even hours not requested by the employer" meets the duty.
  3. The employer need not dig through device records. If an employee does not report unscheduled hours through the procedure, the employer generally does not have to sift through records such as logins to work devices outside reported hours to find them.
  4. The procedure fails if it is discouraged. A reporting process is not reasonable diligence where the employer "either prevents or discourages an employee from accurately reporting the time he or she has worked", and an employee cannot waive the right to be paid.
  5. Training matters. The bulletin notes that if employees are not properly instructed on the reporting system, the employer may be liable for the hours.

Two cautions on reading it. The bulletin also says records outside the timekeeping system may be relevant where consulting them is practical, so it is not a license to ignore what a manager can plainly see. And it is guidance to investigators, not a regulation; the duty itself comes from the statute and Part 785.

Does monitoring data create knowledge?

This is the question remote employers ask most, because tracking software records activity outside scheduled hours. The bulletin's answer is cautious. Having access to non-payroll records does not by itself mean the employer must comb through them, but where checking them is practical, they can form the basis of constructive knowledge.

In practice, the safer reading is the simple one. If a manager receives an alert or a report showing a non-exempt employee working at 10 p.m., the employer now knows. Either pay for that time or tell the employee to stop and enforce it. A tracking tool that captures evening activity also makes it harder to argue that the employer had no reason to believe work was happening. That is one reason the remote employee time tracking tools worth shortlisting are the ones with overwork reporting, so the after-hours pattern surfaces and gets dealt with rather than sitting in a log.

State law follows the worker

The FLSA is a floor. Section 18, 29 U.S.C. 218(a), says no provision of the Act excuses noncompliance with a federal or state law setting a higher minimum wage or a shorter maximum workweek. Wage and hour law is generally applied where the work is performed, so a remote employee brings their state's overtime and minimum wage rules with them.

California is the case that matters most. Per the California Division of Labor Standards Enforcement, California pays time and a half after eight hours in a workday and double time after twelve, with special rules for the seventh consecutive day. In Sullivan v. Oracle Corp. (2011) the California Supreme Court held that the Labor Code's overtime rules applied to overtime work performed in California for a California-based employer by out-of-state residents who worked full days and weeks there; it did not decide the partial-day question. Alaska, Nevada and Colorado have their own daily thresholds with their own conditions.

  • Track where each person works. The remote work agreement should fix the primary location and require approval for moves.
  • Configure overtime by the employee's state, not the company's. A tool that only applies weekly overtime will understate a California employee's pay.
  • Ask before long stays elsewhere. Weeks of work from another state raise the question of whether that state's rules apply; settle it before the trip, not after.

Location also changes the monitoring notice an employee is owed; see monitoring remote employees in other states and countries.

What unlogged after-hours minutes cost

Small amounts add up. Enter a scheduled week and the after-hours work that is not being logged. The calculator applies the federal weekly rule only: 1.5 times the base rate for hours over 40. If the employee earns bonuses or differentials, the regular rate is higher; use the full overtime calculator for that.

Federal weekly overtime only, at 1.5 times the base rate, for 52 weeks. State daily overtime, bonuses and liquidated damages are not included. Nothing you enter leaves your browser.

With the defaults, twenty minutes a day on a 40-hour schedule is 1.67 overtime hours a week. Unpaid overtime claims under the FLSA reach back two years, three if the violation is willful, and add an equal amount in liquidated damages, as our 1099 vs W-2 guide sets out.

Exempt remote employees

None of this applies to an employee who is properly exempt, which takes all three tests: paid on a salary basis, at least $684 a week (the federal threshold after the Department rescinded the 2024 increase), and doing work within a recognized exemption. Working from home changes none of those tests. A salaried employee who fails the duties test is non-exempt, owed overtime, and needs hours recorded; see exempt vs non-exempt. Overtime must be paid in money; comp time instead of pay is limited to public agencies under the FLSA.

A remote overtime policy that holds up

  1. Require pre-approval for overtime, and pay it anyway. Section 785.13 says management "has the power to enforce the rule" against unwanted work, so treat breaches through your normal process, but pay for every hour that was worked.
  2. Give a simple way to report unscheduled time. One field in the timesheet for "after-hours work" is enough, with a line in the policy saying it will be paid.
  3. Train people on it and say it out loud. FAB 2020-5 notes the employer may be liable where employees are not properly instructed on the reporting system.
  4. Do not discourage reporting. A manager who frowns at overtime entries undoes the procedure. Track the pattern, then fix the workload.
  5. Keep the record. The recordkeeping rule at 29 CFR 516.2(a)(7) requires hours worked each workday and total hours each workweek for non-exempt employees.
  6. Set quiet hours for messages. Use scheduled send across zones; the guide to tracking hours across time zones covers workweek definitions for distributed teams.

If some of the people logging hours are contractors rather than employees, overtime is not owed, but controlling their hours creates a different risk; see whether you can monitor independent contractors. Key takeaways

  • Non-exempt remote employees are owed time and a half for hours over 40 in the workweek, counting work at home the employer knows or has reason to believe is done (29 CFR 785.11 to 785.13).
  • DOL Field Assistance Bulletin 2020-5 says a reasonable reporting procedure, with all reported hours paid, is one way to meet the duty of reasonable diligence.
  • The procedure fails if the employer prevents or discourages accurate reporting, or if employees are not properly instructed on it.
  • The FLSA lets stricter state law apply, so the worker's state can add daily overtime, as California does after eight hours.
  • Twenty unlogged minutes a day on a 40-hour week is 1.67 overtime hours a week, and FLSA claims reach back two to three years with liquidated damages.
  • A rule requiring approval for overtime is fine, but unapproved hours that were worked must still be paid.

Frequently asked questions

Do remote employees get overtime?

Yes, if they are non-exempt. The FLSA requires time and a half the regular rate for hours over 40 in a workweek wherever the work is done, and 29 CFR 785.12 says hours at home count when the employer knows or has reason to believe the work is being performed.

Is checking email after hours paid time?

For non-exempt employees it can be. Work not requested but suffered or permitted is work time under 29 CFR 785.11. If the employer knows or has reason to believe the employee is doing it, the time counts. Give employees a way to report it.

What is DOL Field Assistance Bulletin 2020-5?

A Department of Labor bulletin issued on August 24, 2020 on employers' obligation to exercise reasonable diligence in tracking teleworkers' hours. It says a reasonable procedure for reporting unscheduled time, with all reported hours paid, is one way to meet that duty, provided the employer does not discourage reporting.

Which state's overtime law applies to a remote employee?

Generally the law of the state where the work is performed, layered on top of the FLSA, which lets stricter state law apply. A remote employee working in California is subject to California's daily overtime rules even if the employer is based elsewhere.

Can I refuse to pay overtime that was not approved?

No. Under 29 CFR 785.11 to 785.13, work suffered or permitted is work time, and a rule against unapproved work is not enough on its own; the employer must enforce it. Treat a breach of the approval rule as a conduct issue, but pay for the hours actually worked.

Does monitoring software mean I know about after-hours work?

Not automatically. FAB 2020-5 says access to non-payroll records does not by itself require an employer to comb through them, but where consulting them is practical they can form the basis of constructive knowledge. An alert a manager actually reads is knowledge.
