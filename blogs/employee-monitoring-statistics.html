Employee Monitoring Statistics in 2026 US Managers Can Actually Trust

WH The WorkIntel Hub editorial team
July 30, 2026 · 9 min read

Employee monitoring statistics are survey and telemetry figures that describe how workers and the public respond to workplace tracking, and how tracked work actually behaves. Most managers meet them in a bad form: a percentage with no sample size, no fielding date, and no named author. Before anything else, we should say plainly where those numbers usually come from. A large share of the employee monitoring statistics circulating online originate on the blogs of companies that sell monitoring software, and those pages tend to cite each other in a loop that never reaches a primary study.

We went looking for the sources behind the most quoted figures and kept landing on dead links, undated PDFs, and surveys with no stated method. So this page uses a narrower rule: every number below traces to a named organization, with a stated sample and a fielding date, and we give you both so you can check us. That is a shorter list than the roundups you will find elsewhere. It is also a list you can put in front of a works council, a lawyer, or a skeptical team without it falling apart.

The point of the exercise is not trivia. If you are deciding what to switch on, these figures tell you roughly what each choice will cost you in consent, and where the public draws its lines. That is genuinely useful, and it is different from what a vendor deck will tell you.

Why most employee monitoring statistics fail a source check

A statistic is usable when four things are visible: who ran it, how many people were asked, when they were asked, and who was in the sample. Miss any one and the number cannot be interpreted, only repeated. Most of what ranks for this topic misses two or three.

The failure is rarely malicious. A vendor writes a blog post and cites an older post, that post cited a press release, the press release summarized a survey whose method page is gone. Four hops later the figure looks established because it appears in twenty places, when it appears in twenty places because of one broken link. Sample size drift is the common tell: the same percentage shows up attributed to "US employers", then "global businesses", then "companies worldwide", with no version naming a count.

We would rather hand you six numbers you can defend than sixty you cannot. If a figure you need is not here, the honest move is to write the sentence without a number and say what you observed on your own team instead. That principle runs through our ethical playbook for employee productivity monitoring, which deals with the decisions these figures inform.

What Americans oppose, in verified numbers

Public opposition to workplace tracking is high, and it varies sharply by what is being tracked. The Pew Research Center published survey results in April 2023, from American Trends Panel Wave 119, measuring how US adults feel about employers using AI to monitor and evaluate workers. Three findings should shape your shortlist.

61% oppose employers using AI to track workers' movements while they work. 56% oppose using it to keep track of when office workers are at their desks. 51% oppose using it to record what people do on their work computers. The ordering is the interesting part. Physical location draws the strongest objection, presence in a seat draws slightly less, and computer activity draws the least. Every option still faces majority or near-majority opposition.

The 81% who expect workers to feel inappropriately watched

The single most useful figure in the Pew data is not about approval, it is about feeling. 81% of US adults said workers would feel inappropriately watched if employers used AI to collect and analyze information about how workers do their jobs. 52% said definitely, 29% said probably. Only a small remainder expected people to feel otherwise.

Read what that does to a rollout. It means the reaction you are managing is not a minority problem to be handled with an FAQ. It is the default expectation your announcement lands in. People arrive already braced, and the burden of proof sits with you from the first sentence. Nothing about the tool changes that. What changes it is the order of operations: state the question you are trying to answer, name the data you will collect, name the data you refuse to collect, and say who can see it.

This is why the sequencing advice in our guide to rolling out time tracking without losing trust matters more than any feature comparison. An 81% expectation of feeling watched is a starting position you have to work back from, not an obstacle you can route around.

The honest limitation of these figures

Pew's April 2023 questions asked specifically about employers using AI to monitor and evaluate workers, and the base was US adults generally, not only employed people and not your industry. So these are a reliable read on American public sentiment toward automated monitoring, and a rough guide to your own workforce. They are not a forecast of how your team will react, and anyone presenting them as one is overselling. If you want your team's number, ask your team.

Age changes the answer more than anything else

The generational gap in the Pew data is wide enough to change a decision. 64% of adults aged 18 to 29 oppose tracking what people do on work computers, against 38% of adults 65 and over. Pew also found adults under 65 were consistently more likely than those 65 and over to oppose each of the six uses tested.

That has a practical consequence most monitoring plans miss. Headline percentages get quoted in leadership meetings, but the headline is an average across an age range your team probably does not match. A team of engineers with a median age of 31 is closer to the 64% figure than the 51% one. A senior leadership group is closer to the other end. Plan against the population you actually employ.

The common misreading is to treat younger opposition as unfamiliarity that will fade. The data does not support that story and neither does the direction of travel. It is more useful to assume the group most opposed to computer activity tracking is also the group whose work is hardest to evaluate by activity counts in the first place, which is a good reason to look at how to measure employee productivity when you cannot see the work before you decide that activity data is the answer.

The remote work numbers that frame the decision

Monitoring decisions in 2026 are mostly decisions about distributed teams, and the distribution is settled. Pew Research Center, in figures published in January 2025 and fielded October 7 to 13, 2024 among 2,315 US workers whose job can be done from home, found 75% work remotely at least some of the time. The base matters: those are remote-capable workers, not all US workers.

Two more figures from the same survey set the stakes. 46% said they would be unlikely to stay in their job if they could no longer work from home, with 26% saying very unlikely. And 75% were required to be in the office certain days, up from 63% in early 2023. Among hybrid workers, 72% preferred hybrid. The pattern is a workforce with a strong stated preference and a credible willingness to move on it.

Put those next to the 81% who expect monitoring to feel intrusive and you get the real calculation. Monitoring introduced badly does not just annoy people, it interacts with a population that has already shown it will change jobs over working conditions. That is the cost side of the ledger, and it belongs in the same document as the benefit you are chasing. Pew's remote work data is the cleanest US source we found on this.

What computer activity data actually captures

Activity data captures interruption at least as much as it captures effort. Microsoft's Work Trend Index, published in June 2025 and combining anonymized Microsoft 365 telemetry with a survey of 31,000 knowledge workers across 31 markets fielded February 6 to March 24, 2025, found knowledge workers are interrupted roughly every two minutes during work hours, about 275 times a day.

The same research found 48% of employees and 52% of leaders describe their work as chaotic and fragmented, that 57% of meetings are ad hoc with no calendar invite, and that one in three say the pace makes it impossible to keep up. Those conditions are what your activity graph is drawing. A busy line means a fragmented day. A quiet line might mean the one thing you actually want, which is two uninterrupted hours on a hard problem.

That inversion is the strongest practical argument against reading activity as output, and it is worth understanding properly before you buy anything, which is the subject of why productivity metrics fail knowledge teams. The measurement problem is not solved by measuring harder.

What US law requires regardless of the numbers

Public opinion is not the binding constraint, state law is. New York Civil Rights Law section 52-c requires employers who monitor telephone conversations, email, or internet usage by an electronic device to give prior written notice upon hiring to all employees subject to monitoring. The notice must be in writing or electronic form and acknowledged by the employee, and the employer must also post it conspicuously where affected employees can see it.

Penalties run up to $500 for a first offense, $1,000 for a second, and $3,000 for a third and each subsequent offense. There is an exemption for processes that manage the type or volume of email, voicemail, or internet usage performed solely for computer system maintenance or protection. We are describing New York's statute because it is specific and checkable, not because it is universal. Monitoring law varies by state, and you should confirm your obligations with counsel in every state where you employ people. If you employ staff in the EU, GDPR adds requirements beyond anything discussed here.

Notice is the low bar, not the goal. The practical version of compliance is a written policy people have read and signed, and our walkthrough of how to write an employee monitoring policy covers what that document needs to contain.

Turning employee monitoring statistics into a decision

Use the figures to rank options by the consent they cost, then pick the cheapest one that answers a question you have actually written down. On the Pew ordering, movement tracking is the most expensive choice at 61% opposition, desk presence next at 56%, and computer activity the least expensive at 51%. Least expensive is not the same as free.

Here is the sequence we would defend. Write the question first, in one sentence, before any tool is discussed. Ask what the smallest data set is that answers it, and whether an existing report already does. Decide what you will never collect and put that in writing. Announce before you switch anything on, not after. Then check whether the answer changed a decision within a quarter, and turn off anything that did not.

The advice does not apply everywhere. Regulated environments with recording obligations, security incident response, and safety-critical operations run on different rules, and general productivity monitoring reasoning should not be stretched over them. Equally, if your real problem is that you cannot tell what your team is working on, employee monitoring statistics will not fix that. Better reporting will, and our complete guide to employee productivity is the wider frame that sits around every number on this page.

Key takeaways

  • Most figures on this topic trace back to vendor blogs citing each other. Demand an organization, a sample, and a fielding date before you quote anything.
  • Pew found in April 2023 that 61% of US adults oppose AI tracking of worker movements, 56% oppose desk presence tracking, and 51% oppose recording work computer activity.
  • 81% expect workers would feel inappropriately watched. That is your starting position, not an edge case.
  • Opposition is much higher among younger workers: 64% of adults 18 to 29 versus 38% of those 65 and over on work computer tracking.
  • Microsoft's 2025 data shows interruption every two minutes, so activity graphs largely measure fragmentation, not effort.
  • State law binds regardless of opinion. New York requires prior written notice, acknowledged and posted, with penalties up to $3,000 per offense.

Frequently asked questions

Why are most employee monitoring statistics unreliable?

Because the figures that dominate search results are published by companies that sell monitoring software, and they tend to cite each other rather than a primary study. When you follow the chain back you often reach a dead link, an undated page, or a survey with no stated sample. A usable statistic names the organization that fielded it, the number of people asked, the dates it was collected, and who exactly was in the sample.

What share of Americans oppose employers tracking worker movements?

61 percent. In a survey published in April 2023, the Pew Research Center found that 61 percent of US adults oppose employers using AI to track workers' movements while they work. That was the most opposed use of the six Pew tested.

How many Americans oppose recording what people do on work computers?

51 percent, according to Pew Research Center data published in April 2023. That is the narrowest margin of the tracking uses Pew tested, which tells you activity monitoring is the most defensible of the common options, not that it is uncontroversial. Roughly half the public still objects.

Do workers expect monitoring to feel intrusive?

Yes, overwhelmingly. Pew found in April 2023 that 81 percent of US adults think workers would feel inappropriately watched if employers used AI to collect and analyze information about how workers do their jobs. 52 percent said definitely and 29 percent said probably. This is an expectation about how it would feel, not a measurement of harm, but it is the reaction your rollout has to survive.

Does age change how people feel about workplace monitoring?

Substantially. Pew reported in April 2023 that 64 percent of adults aged 18 to 29 oppose tracking what people do on work computers, compared with 38 percent of those 65 and over. Adults under 65 were consistently more likely than those 65 and over to oppose each of the six uses Pew tested. If your team skews young, expect resistance well above the headline average.

What does the law require before monitoring employees in the US?

It depends on the state. New York Civil Rights Law section 52-c requires employers who monitor telephone conversations, email, or internet usage by electronic device to give prior written notice upon hiring to every employee subject to monitoring, in writing or electronic form and acknowledged by the employee, and to post that notice conspicuously. Penalties run up to 500 dollars for a first offense, 1,000 for a second, and 3,000 for a third and each one after. There is an exemption for processes performed solely for computer system maintenance or protection. Monitoring law varies by state, so confirm your obligations with counsel.

How many people work remotely at least some of the time?

75 percent of US workers whose job can be done from home, according to Pew Research Center data published in January 2025 and fielded October 7 to 13, 2024 among 2,315 such workers. The base matters. That figure describes people whose work is remote-capable, not the whole US workforce.

Is presence data a good measure of productivity?

No. Microsoft's 2025 Work Trend Index found that knowledge workers are interrupted about every two minutes during work hours, roughly 275 times a day, and that 48 percent of employees and 52 percent of leaders describe their work as chaotic and fragmented. Presence and activity counts under those conditions largely record how interrupted someone is. They do not tell you whether useful work happened.

What is the limitation of the Pew monitoring numbers?

Two things. The questions asked specifically about employers using AI to monitor and evaluate workers, so they measure attitudes toward automated monitoring rather than every form of oversight. And the base is US adults generally, not only employed people or only your industry. Treat the figures as a reliable read on public sentiment and a rough guide to your own workforce, not a forecast of your team's response.

What is the safest way to use these statistics in a decision?

Use them to rank options by cost of consent, not to justify a plan you already made. Movement tracking carries the highest public objection, desk presence next, computer activity lowest of the three. Pick the least intrusive collection that answers your actual question, write down the question first, tell people in advance, and drop anything you cannot explain in a sentence to the people it applies to.
