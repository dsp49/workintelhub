Promotion Announcement

WH The WorkIntel Hub editorial team
October 5, 2026 · 7 min read

A promotion announcement tells the organisation that someone has moved up, and why. The why is the part that gets left out. "Please join me in congratulating Dana on her promotion to Operations Manager" is a title change; "Dana led the warehouse migration, cut order errors by a third and trained the three coordinators who joined this spring" is a promotion, and it tells everyone else what the organisation rewards. The announcement is a recognition event for the person and a signal to the rest of the team, and both jobs need the specifics.

The generator writes five versions from the same details: email, a spoken all-hands script, a chat post, a LinkedIn post and the formal promotion letter to the employee. The rest of the page covers the order of events, what goes in each version, and the handful of mistakes that turn good news sour.

The order of events

  1. Tell the employee first, in private, with the title, date, pay and scope agreed. The promotion letter in the generator is that conversation in writing; it goes in the personnel file and is the document the pay change is processed from.
  2. Tell the people most affected next: anyone who will now report to the promoted employee, anyone who applied for the same role, and the peers whose work changes. A direct conversation before the broadcast, not after it.
  3. Announce to the team or department by email, with the specifics.
  4. Say it out loud at the next all-hands or team meeting; the spoken version is where the applause happens, and it is the one the person remembers.
  5. External posts last, once the internal audience knows and the employee has agreed to be named publicly.

Steps one and two are the ones that go wrong. An employee who learns about a colleague's promotion from the all-company email, having applied for the role, is a stay interview waiting to happen.

Pay is in the letter and nowhere else. Results in the public versions are stated in proportion ("cut order errors by a third") rather than in figures the company would not publish. The letter is the one document that must exist; the performance review that preceded the promotion should tell the same story, which is what makes the decision defensible if another employee asks why not them.

Mistakes that sour the news

  • No specifics. An announcement without results reads, to everyone who was not promoted, as favouritism.
  • Announcing before the letter is agreed. A public promotion with the pay still under negotiation is leverage the company has given away, and a title the employee may now hold without the salary.
  • Forgetting the people who applied. Tell them first, one to one, and tell them what would get them there.
  • Announcing a promotion that is really a workload increase. A new title with no pay change and more responsibility is noticed by the whole team; the salary and exemption rules also change when a promotion moves someone into a managerial role.
  • Making it about the manager. "I am proud to have mentored" belongs in the private conversation. The announcement is about the person promoted.
  • Posting externally without asking. Some people do not want a LinkedIn post, and recruiters read them.

Key takeaways

  • A promotion announcement needs the why: two or three specific results. Without them it reads as a title change or favouritism.
  • Order: the employee in private, then the people most affected (reports, other applicants, peers), then the team email, then the spoken announcement, then external posts.
  • Pay goes in the promotion letter only; public versions state results in proportion, not internal figures.
  • The promotion letter and the preceding performance review should tell the same story.
  • Ask before posting externally.

Frequently asked questions

How do you write a promotion announcement?

Name the person, the new title and the effective date; give two or three specific results that earned it; say what they will now own; and invite congratulations. Keep it to 100 to 150 words from the manager or department head. The generator on this page writes the email, a spoken script, a chat post, a LinkedIn post and the formal letter.

Who should be told about a promotion first?

The employee, in private, with pay and scope agreed. Then the people most affected: anyone who will now report to them, anyone who applied for the same role, and peers whose work changes. The broad announcement comes after those conversations, never before.

What is the difference between a promotion announcement and a promotion letter?

The letter is the formal document to the employee: title, effective date, reporting line, new pay, changes to bonus or benefits, a statement that other terms are unchanged, and an acknowledgement line. It goes in the personnel file. The announcement is the message to colleagues and contains no pay information.

Should a promotion be announced on LinkedIn?

Only after the internal announcement and only with the employee's agreement. Keep results general, use the first name and the new title, and leave out internal detail. Some employees prefer not to be posted about, and that preference should be asked for rather than assumed.

How long should a promotion announcement be?

Email: 100 to 150 words. Spoken at an all-hands: about 60 seconds with three numbered points. Chat: two sentences. The specifics matter more than the length; an announcement with three concrete results in 80 words beats one with none in 300.
