Activity Level Percentage: What It Measures and What It Misses

P Pavan
November 26, 2026 · 8 min read

An activity level percentage is the share of seconds in a tracked period in which you pressed a key or moved the mouse. If you had input in 300 of the 600 seconds in a ten-minute block, your activity level for that block is 50%.

It does not record which keys you pressed, and it does not judge whether the work was useful. Ten minutes of typing scores high; ten minutes on a phone call, reading a contract or thinking scores near zero. This page explains how Hubstaff and Time Doctor use the measure, what a manager sees, why the number is low for good work, and how to read it without turning it into a grade.

The short answer

It counts seconds with input. Any key press or mouse movement in a second makes that second active.

It measures input, not work. Hubstaff's own support guidance says it is a poor measure for people on calls and suggests turning activity recording off for those projects.

There is no correct target. A normal knowledge-work day with calls and reading produces a modest number, and chasing a higher one is how mouse jigglers start.

How the activity level is calculated

The agent divides tracked time into seconds and asks one question of each: was there any keyboard or mouse input? The activity level is active seconds divided by tracked seconds.

  1. The timer runs. In Hubstaff, activity is measured only while the timer is on; when you are off the clock, input is not counted.
  2. Each second is checked. A key press or a mouse movement marks it active. Which key, and what you typed, is not recorded.
  3. Blocks are summed. Hubstaff shows the activity percentage for each time block on the dashboard, beside that block's screenshots if screenshots are on.
  4. Periods roll up. Blocks are averaged into daily and weekly activity levels for the person and the team.

Because one movement makes a whole second active, steady typing can approach a high percentage while careful reading, where you scroll every so often, sits far lower. The measure is about how often your hands touch the machine, nothing else.

With the defaults, a day that includes two hours of calls and two of reading lands at about 35%, although every minute of it was work. Excluding calls, as Hubstaff suggests for call projects, lifts the figure to about 45%. The number tracks the mix of tasks, which is a property of the role, not of the person's effort.

What the activity level misses

  • Calls, meetings and presentations. Listening produces almost no input. Reviews of Hubstaff describe agents marking people idle during presentations and video calls.
  • Reading and thinking. Reviewing a contract or a design scores near zero, and so does working out the answer before you type it.
  • The value of what was typed. A thousand keystrokes of a draft you will delete count the same as the ten lines that fixed the bug.
  • Work off the device. Paper, whiteboards and phone calls are invisible, as with any agent. The idle time tracking guide explains how those stretches are flagged.
  • What app you were in. Activity counts input anywhere. Whether that app counts as productive is a separate question, covered in how productive and unproductive apps are classified.

Our Hubstaff alternatives page calls the activity level a keyboard and mouse ratio, not a measure of work, and notes that the score is one of the main reasons teams leave: managers read the number as a grade and employees know it.

Activity level, idle time and productivity score are different numbers

Dashboards often show these three side by side, and they are easy to confuse. They share an input but answer different questions.

A person can have a high activity level and a low productivity score (typing all day in an app labeled neutral) or the reverse (a few focused hours in a core tool between long calls). Each needs reading on its own terms, and none of them measures output.

Jigglers, detection and the cost of chasing the number

A measure based on input invites fake input. Some people respond to a low activity level with a mouse jiggler, and it usually ends badly. Time Doctor's Premium plan includes mouse-jiggler and irregular-keyboard detection and an unusual-activity report built to catch exactly that. Wells Fargo dismissed more than a dozen employees in 2024 for simulated keyboard activity, and employers generally treat simulated activity as falsifying time records.

For employees: if your manager reads a low percentage as low effort, point to the vendor's own guidance on calls and ask for activity recording to be turned off for meeting-heavy projects. A dishonesty finding is far worse than a low score.

How managers should read it

  1. Turn it off where it cannot work. Disable activity recording for call, meeting and review projects, as Hubstaff recommends.
  2. Compare people with themselves. A sudden fall against someone's own baseline can signal a problem worth a conversation. A difference between two roles signals nothing.
  3. Never set a target. A target turns the measure into the job, and the job becomes moving the mouse.
  4. Pair it with outcomes. Hours and activity are evidence of time spent, useful for billing and proof of work. Output is a separate measure.
  5. Consider a different method. If you need productivity insight rather than proof of work, a classification or utilization tool answers a better question. The employee productivity score page shows how those are built.

Key takeaways

  • The activity level percentage is the share of tracked seconds with any keyboard or mouse input.
  • Hubstaff measures it only while the timer runs and shows it for each time block on the dashboard; it does not record which keys were pressed.
  • Time Doctor measures activity from keyboard and mouse input and, on Premium, detects mouse jigglers and irregular keyboard patterns.
  • Calls, meetings, reading and thinking score near zero, so the number reflects the mix of tasks in a role, not effort.
  • Hubstaff's own guidance says the measure suits calls poorly and suggests disabling activity recording for those projects.
  • Use it as a personal trend and evidence of time, never as a target or a grade.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good activity level percentage?

There is no good number in general. A role made of typing scores high and a role with many calls and documents scores low, even when both are fully working. Compare a person with their own baseline, and turn activity recording off for call-heavy work.

How does Hubstaff calculate activity level?

As the percentage of seconds in a tracked period in which a key was pressed or the mouse moved. It does not record which keys, and it measures only while the timer runs.

Does Time Doctor track activity level?

Yes. Time Doctor measures activity from keyboard and mouse input during tracked time and marks stretches without input as idle. Its Premium plan adds mouse-jiggler and irregular-keyboard detection.

Does activity level record what I type?

No. Hubstaff counts whether input happened in each second, not which keys. Recording what you type is not listed among Time Doctor's published features.

Why is my activity level low during meetings?

Because listening on a call produces almost no keyboard or mouse input. Hubstaff's support guidance acknowledges this and suggests disabling activity recording for call projects.

Is activity level the same as a productivity score?

No. Activity level counts input in any app. A productivity score depends on which apps you used and how your employer has labeled them productive, neutral or unproductive.
