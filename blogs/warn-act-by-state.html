WARN Act Notice Requirements by State: Mini-WARN Laws Table

P Pavan
November 12, 2026 · 14 min read

The federal Worker Adjustment and Retraining Notification Act requires employers with 100 or more employees to give 60 days' written notice before a plant closing or mass layoff. It covers fewer situations than most managers assume (a layoff of 40 people at a 500-person site is not a federal WARN event) and it is now the minimum rather than the rule in a growing list of states. New Jersey and New York require 90 days and count smaller layoffs; New Jersey and Maine require severance pay; California, Illinois and Maryland cover employers with 50 or 75 employees; Washington's law took effect in July 2025 and Ohio's in September 2025, bringing the number of states with mandatory mini-WARN statutes to about fifteen.

This guide sets out the federal rules with the definitions that matter (single site, employment loss, the 90-day aggregation window), the exceptions and how courts have read them, each state statute with thresholds, notice periods, severance and penalties, the new disclosure requirements for layoffs involving artificial intelligence in New York and Connecticut, the sequence for a compliant notice, and what a laid-off employee can claim when notice was not given. For the broader process, see the furlough vs layoff and severance pay guides.

The federal WARN Act

The Worker Adjustment and Retraining Notification Act (29 U.S.C. 2101 to 2109, regulations at 20 CFR 639) applies to employers with 100 or more employees, excluding part-time employees, or 100 or more employees including part-timers who together work at least 4,000 hours a week excluding overtime. A covered employer must give at least 60 calendar days' written notice to affected employees (or their union), the state dislocated-worker unit and the chief elected official of the local government before:

  • a plant closing: the permanent or temporary shutdown of a single site, or of one or more facilities or operating units within it, that results in an employment loss for 50 or more employees (excluding part-timers) during any 30-day period; or
  • a mass layoff: a reduction in force that is not a plant closing and results in an employment loss at a single site of either 500 or more employees, or 50 to 499 employees if they make up at least 33% of the active workforce at that site, again excluding part-timers.

Notice must be specific: whether the action is permanent or temporary, the expected date of the first separation and the schedule, the job titles and number of affected employees in each, whether bumping rights exist, and a contact. A sale of the business shifts the obligation from seller to buyer at the closing date. Federal WARN is enforced only through private suits in federal court; the Department of Labor has no enforcement role beyond issuing regulations and guidance.

Definitions that decide coverage

  • Employment loss: a termination other than for cause, voluntary departure or retirement; a layoff exceeding six months; or a reduction in hours of more than 50% in each month of any six-month period. A furlough announced as temporary becomes an employment loss, and triggers retroactive liability, when it passes six months without notice.
  • Single site of employment: usually one building or a campus; separate buildings in the same area that share staff and equipment may be one site; and under 20 CFR 639.3(i)(6), workers who travel or work from home are assigned to the site they report to or from which their work is assigned. The 50 and 33% tests are applied site by site, so a company-wide layoff of 300 people spread across ten sites of 100 may trigger no federal notice at all.
  • The 90-day window: separate layoffs within any 90-day period are aggregated unless the employer shows they resulted from separate and distinct causes. Rolling layoffs of 30 and then 30 at the same site, six weeks apart, are a 60-person event.
  • Part-time employees (under 20 hours a week on average, or employed fewer than 6 of the last 12 months) are excluded from the counts but are entitled to notice if the thresholds are met by others.
  • Who counts as the employer: parent and subsidiary can be a single employer under a five-factor test (common ownership, common directors and officers, de facto control, unity of personnel policies, dependency of operations), which matters for private-equity portfolio companies and staffing arrangements.

Exceptions and how courts read them

Three exceptions allow shorter notice, never no notice: the employer must still give as much notice as practicable with a brief statement of why the period was shortened.

  • Faltering company (plant closings only): the employer was actively seeking capital or business that would have avoided or postponed the shutdown and reasonably believed notice would have precluded obtaining it. Courts read this narrowly and require documentation of the financing efforts.
  • Unforeseeable business circumstances: a sudden, dramatic and unexpected event outside the employer's control, such as the loss of a major contract, a client's bankruptcy or a government order. The pandemic produced a wave of litigation on this exception; most courts accepted it for closures in March and April 2020 but required employers to give notice as soon as the circumstances became probable.
  • Natural disaster: floods, earthquakes, storms; the closing must be a direct result.

Strikes and lockouts are excluded, as are temporary facilities and projects where employees were hired with the understanding that employment was limited. California's law has no faltering-company exception and a narrower unforeseeable-circumstances rule, which is why California employers often default to 60 days even when federal law would excuse them.

Federal penalties

An employer that fails to give required notice is liable to each affected employee for back pay and benefits for each day of violation, up to 60 days, less wages paid during the period and any voluntary and unconditional payments not required by law. The civil penalty for failing to notify the local government is up to $500 a day, waived if the employer pays the back pay within three weeks of the closing. Courts may award attorney's fees. Liability is often settled by paying the 60 days as severance, which is the arithmetic behind the common "60 days pay in lieu of notice" practice; pay in lieu is a damages measure, not compliance, and does not satisfy state laws that require actual notice to the state.

State mini-WARN laws

About fifteen states have mandatory laws that lower the thresholds, lengthen the notice period or add severance; others require notice to the state agency only or encourage voluntary notice. The ones that change what an employer must do:

  • New Jersey (Millville Dallas Airmotive Plant Job Loss Notification Act, amended effective April 10, 2023): employers with 100 or more employees anywhere; a mass layoff is 50 or more terminations within 30 days aggregated across all New Jersey establishments, part-timers counted; 90 days' notice; mandatory severance of one week's pay per full year of service, plus four additional weeks if notice is short; severance may not be waived without state or court approval. The most demanding law in the country.
  • New York (Labor Law 860, regulations amended June 21, 2023): employers with 50 or more full-time employees in the state; plant closing affecting 25 or more; mass layoff of 25 or more if 33% of the site or 250 or more; relocation; 90 days' notice; remote employees based at a New York site are counted. Exceptions require a determination by the Commissioner. A 2026 amendment requires employers to state whether artificial intelligence was a factor in the layoff decision (see below).
  • California (Labor Code 1400 to 1408): employers with 75 or more employees in the preceding 12 months; 50 or more employment losses in 30 days, a relocation of 100 miles or more, or a termination of operations; 60 days; back pay up to 60 days and $500 a day; no faltering-company exception. California's definition of employment loss is broader and includes layoffs with no six-month test.
  • Illinois (820 ILCS 65): employers with 75 or more full-time employees; mass layoff of 25 or more full-time employees if 33% of the site or 250 or more; plant closing affecting 50 or more; 60 days; back pay up to 60 days and $500 a day.
  • Maryland (Economic Stabilization Act, mandatory since October 1, 2020): employers with 50 or more employees; a reduction in operations that cuts a workplace's workforce by 25% or 15 employees, whichever is greater, over three months; 60 days; civil penalties up to $10,000 a day. Exemptions for labor disputes, seasonal work, bankruptcy and temporary sites.
  • Washington (RCW 50.55, Securing Timely Notification and Benefits for Laid-Off Employees Act, effective July 27, 2025): employers with 50 or more full-time employees in the state; a mass layoff or business closing affecting 50 or more employees within 30 days, not limited to a single site; 60 days' notice including names and job titles to the Employment Security Department, employees and unions; back pay and benefits up to 60 days and a $500-a-day civil penalty.
  • Ohio (R.C. 4113.31, enacted in the 2025 budget bill, effective September 29, 2025): employers with 100 or more employees working at least 4,000 hours a week; mirrors federal definitions but omits the 33% qualifier for layoffs of 50 or more (a drafting point in dispute); 60 days' notice to employees or unions, local officials and the Department of Job and Family Services with expanded content; federal remedies.
  • Maine (26 MRSA 625-B): establishments with 100 or more employees in the prior 12 months; 90 days' notice of a closing or relocation of 100 miles or more to the Director, employees and municipality; notice of a mass layoff (33% and 50, or 500, lasting 6 months or more) to the Director within 7 days; severance of one week's pay per year of service for employees with three or more years; penalties of $500 a day for notice failures and up to $1,000 per employee for severance failures.
  • Delaware (19 Del. C. chapter 19): employers with 100 or more employees (or 100 working 2,000 hours a week); mass layoff of 50 and 33% or 500; plant closing of 50; relocation of 50 miles or more; 60 days; penalty of $1,000 a day or $100 a day per worker, plus back pay up to 60 days.
  • Hawaii (HRS 394B): employers with 50 or more employees; closing, divestiture or relocation; 60 days' notice; a dislocated-worker allowance equal to four weeks' wages less unemployment benefits.
  • Iowa (Code chapter 84C): employers with 25 or more employees; 25 or more employment losses; 30 days' notice.
  • Wisconsin (109.07): employers with 50 or more employees; 25 or more employment losses and 25% of the site, or 500; 60 days.
  • Tennessee (50-1-601 to 604): employers with 50 to 99 employees; 50 or more employment losses in three months; notice to the state as required, closing the gap below the federal 100-employee line.
  • Vermont (21 V.S.A. 411 to 418): employers with 50 or more employees; 50 or more employment losses in 90 days; 45 days' notice to the state and 30 to employees.
  • Connecticut (31-51n and 51o): no separate notice period, but employers with 100 or more employees that close a plant must continue group health insurance for 120 days; Public Act 26-15 (signed May 27, 2026) requires disclosure of the role of artificial intelligence in WARN filings from October 1, 2026.

Kansas (public utilities and listed industries only), Massachusetts (voluntary standards plus health-insurance continuation for 90 days under 151A, 71A), Michigan and Minnesota (voluntary or advisory), Oregon (notice to the state concurrent with federal WARN under ORS 285A.516) and Georgia (notice to the Department of Labor) have lesser requirements. New Hampshire enacted a mini-WARN in 2010 and repealed it in 2012; some trackers still list it. Colorado has no mini-WARN law.

AI disclosure in layoff notices

Two states now require employers to say whether automation played a role. New York's Department of Labor added a checkbox to its WARN form in 2025 asking whether the layoff was due to technological innovation or automation and, if so, whether that meant artificial intelligence, and a 2026 amendment put the requirement into the statute. Connecticut's Public Act 26-15, signed May 27, 2026, requires WARN filings from October 1, 2026 to disclose the role of artificial intelligence in the decision. Challenger, Gray and Christmas counted artificial intelligence as the stated reason for about 121,000 announced job cuts in the first ten months of 2026, roughly a fifth of the total, so the question will be answered "yes" in a meaningful share of notices. Employers should decide the answer with counsel before the notice is drafted, because it is discoverable and because a "no" contradicted by internal documents is worse than a "yes." The layoff statistics page tracks the AI-attributed figures.

Remote employees and which site they belong to

Under the federal regulations, employees who work from home are assigned to the single site from which their work is assigned or to which they report, so a remote team managed from a Dallas office counts toward the Dallas site's 50 and 33% thresholds even if no one lives in Texas. New York's 2023 regulations say the same for state coverage: remote employees "based at" a New York site count toward New York's 50-employee and 25-person thresholds. New Jersey's statewide aggregation and Washington's "not limited to a single site" language mean remote employees residing in those states count regardless of where their manager sits. Employers with distributed workforces should map every employee to a reporting site and a state of residence before planning a reduction, because the same 60-person layoff can be a non-event federally, a New Jersey event with 90 days and severance, and a Washington event with 60 days, all at once.

A compliant notice, step by step

  1. Count employees by site and by state, excluding part-timers where the law allows, and including remote staff at their assigned site; look back 90 days for prior losses to aggregate.
  2. Test each jurisdiction's thresholds: federal, then every state where employees sit. The longest notice period and any severance mandate govern.
  3. Decide dates: the first separation date drives the notice date; work back 60 or 90 days and add a few days for mailing.
  4. Draft the three notices (employees or union, state dislocated-worker unit, local chief elected official), with every element the regulations require, in plain language. State forms (New York, California, New Jersey, Washington) have their own fields.
  5. Decide the AI question for New York and Connecticut filings.
  6. Deliver by a method that proves receipt: hand delivery with signature or mail with tracking; email alone is risky for employees.
  7. Coordinate severance, benefits continuation (COBRA and state continuation such as Connecticut's 120 days), final pay deadlines (see final paycheck laws by state), accrued leave payout and the offboarding checklist.
  8. Document any exception relied on, contemporaneously, with the facts that made the circumstances unforeseeable or the financing efforts active.
  9. Keep the record: notices, delivery proofs, counts and the decision memo, for the limitations period (federal WARN borrows the most analogous state period, often 2 to 6 years).

If you were laid off without notice

Ask three questions. How many people lost their jobs at your site (or in your state, for New Jersey and Washington) within 30 days, and within 90? How many employees did the company have? Which state were you based in? If the federal thresholds were met and you received less than 60 days' notice, you can claim up to 60 days of back pay and benefits in federal court, less anything paid in lieu; WARN suits are usually class actions and lawyers take them on contingency. In New Jersey you are owed severance of a week per year regardless of notice, and four extra weeks if notice was short. In New York the notice period is 90 days and a complaint can be filed with the Department of Labor. Severance agreements routinely include a WARN release; the consideration must exceed what the law already owes you, so compare the offer against 60 or 90 days of pay before signing. State WARN notices are public records, and most states post them online, which is how to check what the employer told the state and when.

Key takeaways

  • Federal WARN covers employers with 100 or more employees and requires 60 days' notice before a plant closing (50 or more losses at one site in 30 days) or a mass layoff (500 or more, or 50 to 499 and 33% of the site); part-timers are excluded from the counts and separate layoffs within 90 days are aggregated.
  • Remedies are back pay and benefits up to 60 days plus $500 a day to the local government; the three exceptions (faltering company, unforeseeable business circumstances, natural disaster) shorten notice but never eliminate it.
  • New Jersey is the strictest state: 90 days, 50 terminations aggregated statewide, part-timers counted, and mandatory severance of a week per year of service plus four weeks if notice is short.
  • New York requires 90 days for 25 or more losses at employers with 50 or more employees; California covers 75-employee employers and has no faltering-company exception; Illinois and Maryland use 75 and 50-employee thresholds; Maine mandates severance after three years of service.
  • Washington (July 27, 2025) and Ohio (September 29, 2025) are the newest mini-WARN states, bringing the count of mandatory state laws to about fifteen; Colorado has none and New Hampshire repealed its law in 2012.
  • New York and Connecticut (from October 1, 2026) require employers to disclose whether artificial intelligence played a role in the layoff; AI was the stated reason for about a fifth of announced US job cuts in 2026.
  • Remote employees count toward the site they report to under federal and New York rules, and toward statewide totals in New Jersey and Washington; map every employee to a site and a state before planning a reduction.

Frequently asked questions

What is the WARN Act?

The Worker Adjustment and Retraining Notification Act, a 1988 federal law requiring employers with 100 or more employees to give 60 calendar days' written notice to employees, the state and local government before a plant closing or mass layoff. Employers that fail to give notice owe affected employees up to 60 days of back pay and benefits.

How many employees trigger the WARN Act?

The employer must have 100 or more employees (excluding part-timers) or 100 or more including part-timers who work 4,000 hours a week. The event must be a plant closing with 50 or more employment losses at a single site in 30 days, or a mass layoff of 500 or more, or 50 to 499 if they are at least 33% of the site's workforce. Separate layoffs within 90 days are added together.

Which states have mini-WARN laws?

States with mandatory laws that go beyond federal WARN include California, Delaware, Hawaii, Illinois, Iowa, Maine, Maryland, New Jersey, New York, Ohio (2025), Tennessee, Vermont, Washington (2025) and Wisconsin, plus Connecticut's health-insurance continuation and AI disclosure. Massachusetts, Michigan, Minnesota, Oregon, Georgia and Kansas have voluntary, advisory or limited requirements. Colorado has none.

Does the WARN Act require severance pay?

No. Federal WARN requires notice, and the remedy for failing to give it is up to 60 days of back pay, which is why employers often pay 60 days in lieu. New Jersey requires severance of one week per year of service for covered layoffs regardless of notice, plus four weeks if notice is short, and Maine requires a week per year for employees with three or more years of service when a plant closes.

What is the penalty for violating the WARN Act?

Back pay and benefits to each affected employee for each day of the violation, up to 60 days, less wages and voluntary payments made, plus attorney's fees, and a civil penalty of up to $500 a day for failing to notify local government, waived if back pay is paid within three weeks. State penalties add to this: $500 a day in California, Illinois and Washington, up to $10,000 a day in Maryland.

Does the WARN Act apply to remote employees?

Yes. Remote employees are assigned to the single site from which their work is assigned or to which they report, and count toward that site's thresholds. New York counts remote employees based at a New York site, and New Jersey and Washington count employees statewide rather than by site, so remote workers residing in those states count toward their laws.

Can an employer give pay instead of WARN notice?

Paying 60 days of wages and benefits satisfies the federal damages an employee could recover, and many employers do it, but it is not technically compliance: the notice to the state and local government is still required, and state laws such as New York's 90 days, New Jersey's severance and Washington's notice to the Employment Security Department are not satisfied by pay in lieu.

What are the exceptions to the WARN Act?

Three: a faltering company actively seeking financing that notice would have jeopardized (plant closings only), unforeseeable business circumstances such as the sudden loss of a major contract, and natural disasters. Each allows shorter notice, not none, and the employer must still give as much notice as practicable with an explanation. California does not recognize the faltering-company exception.
