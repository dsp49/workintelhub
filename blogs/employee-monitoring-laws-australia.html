Employee Monitoring Laws in Australia: NSW, ACT, Victoria

P Pavan
November 20, 2026 · 11 min read

Australia regulates workplace surveillance mostly at state and territory level, and unevenly. New South Wales and the Australian Capital Territory have dedicated workplace surveillance statutes that require written notice before camera, computer or tracking surveillance begins and a court authority for covert surveillance. Victoria bans surveillance in workplace toilets, change rooms and lactation rooms and is considering a broader law after a 2025 parliamentary inquiry. Every state and territory has a surveillance devices act that controls recording of private conversations. The federal Privacy Act exempts most handling of private-sector employee records, though a new statutory tort for serious invasions of privacy has applied since June 2025.

This guide covers each regime, the Fair Work Commission's approach to monitoring evidence, what is changing, and a checklist for employers. For the US, UK and EU, see laws by state, UK and EU.

New South Wales: Workplace Surveillance Act 2005

The Act covers three kinds of surveillance of employees at work: camera, computer (software or equipment that monitors computer use, including email and internet access) and tracking (devices such as GPS that monitor location or movement).

  • Notice (section 10): written notice at least 14 days before surveillance starts, unless the employee agrees to a shorter period; email counts as written notice; new employees must be notified before they start. The notice must state the kind of surveillance, how it will be carried out, when it will start, and whether it is continuous or intermittent, and for how long.
  • Camera surveillance: cameras must be clearly visible and signs must be displayed at workplace entrances.
  • Tracking surveillance: a clearly visible notice on the tracked vehicle or item.
  • Computer surveillance: must follow a policy the employee has been notified of in advance.
  • Prohibitions: no surveillance in change rooms, toilets, showers or bathing facilities (section 15); no surveillance of employees when they are not at work, except computer surveillance of employer-provided equipment (section 16); no blocking of email or internet access except under the notified policy, and no blocking because a message relates to industrial matters (section 17).
  • Covert surveillance: only with a covert surveillance authority issued by a magistrate, solely to establish whether an employee is involved in unlawful activity at work, for up to 30 days, with a report within 30 days of expiry.
  • Penalties: up to 50 penalty units for most offenses, including covert surveillance without an authority, and 20 penalty units for misusing surveillance records.

Australian Capital Territory

The Workplace Privacy Act 2011 covers optical, tracking and data surveillance (not listening devices, which fall under other law). Employers must give notice before surveillance starts, consult employees in good faith about its introduction, and obtain a Magistrates Court authority for covert surveillance. Surveillance is prohibited in toilets, change rooms, nursing rooms, first-aid rooms and prayer rooms. The ACT model is often described as the most protective because of its consultation duty.

Victoria and the coming reforms

Victoria's Surveillance Devices Act 1999 (Part 2A) makes it an offense for employers to use optical or listening devices to observe or listen to workers in toilets, washrooms, change rooms or lactation rooms. Other workplace surveillance is governed by the general device rules, which allow optical surveillance of activities in a workplace that are not private.

A Legislative Assembly committee inquiry into workplace surveillance reported on May 13, 2025 with 18 recommendations, centered on a technology-neutral law requiring surveillance to be reasonable, necessary and proportionate. The government supported 15 in principle and in July 2026 pledged, if re-elected at the November 28, 2026 state election, to legislate what it called the toughest workplace surveillance laws in the country, including limits on AI and biometric monitoring, notice and consultation duties and a ban on selling workplace surveillance data. No bill had been introduced by the time this page was written.

Listening devices in other states

Every jurisdiction regulates recording of private conversations. Queensland, Victoria and the Northern Territory allow a party to a conversation to record it without others' consent ("participant monitoring"). New South Wales, Western Australia, South Australia, Tasmania and the ACT generally require the consent of the principal parties, with limited exceptions, such as South Australia's for recordings reasonably necessary to protect a lawful interest. Employers that record calls or meetings should obtain consent through notices and announcements; employees who secretly record meetings may commit an offense outside the participant-monitoring states. Check the current act in each state before recording.

The federal Privacy Act and the new privacy tort

  • Employee records exemption: the Privacy Act 1988 does not apply to a private-sector employer's handling of an employee record where the act is directly related to the current or former employment relationship. It does apply to job applicants, contractors and to information that is not part of an employee record. The Privacy and Other Legislation Amendment Act 2024 left the exemption in place, although reform proposals to narrow it remain under discussion.
  • Statutory tort: since June 10, 2025, individuals can sue for serious invasions of privacy, including intrusion upon seclusion such as unlawful surveillance, where the invasion was intentional or reckless and the public interest in privacy outweighs countervailing interests. Commentators consider that the employee records exemption does not extend to the tort, which would make egregious workplace surveillance actionable; this has not yet been tested.

Monitoring evidence in unfair dismissal cases

The Fair Work Commission accepts lawful monitoring data as evidence. In Cheikho v Insurance Australia Group Services ([2023] FWC 1792) it upheld the dismissal of a work-from-home consultant whose laptop activity data showed very low keystroke activity over several months and who had missed deadlines and meetings. The decision turned on the combination of activity data and performance evidence, not keystrokes alone, and on the employer's notified policies. Surveillance that breaches the NSW or ACT Acts risks being excluded and exposes the employer to prosecution.

Australian workers are wary of monitoring: a 2026 University of Technology Sydney report citing Minderoo polling found 75% believed monitoring should be used only if clearly communicated and strictly necessary, and around 70% were concerned about audio surveillance, live-screen monitoring and emotion recognition.

A compliance checklist

  1. Identify where each employee works; NSW and ACT rules apply to work in those jurisdictions.
  2. Give written notice at least 14 days before any camera, computer or tracking surveillance in NSW, with the required details, and before work starts for new hires; consult employees in the ACT.
  3. Publish a computer surveillance policy and follow it; do not block messages about industrial matters.
  4. Never use surveillance in toilets, change rooms, showers, lactation or prayer rooms.
  5. Stop computer and tracking surveillance when employees are off work, except on employer-provided equipment in NSW, and disable tracking on personal time.
  6. Seek a magistrate's covert surveillance authority before any covert monitoring in NSW or the ACT.
  7. Obtain consent before recording conversations, especially in states without participant monitoring.
  8. Keep monitoring proportionate in anticipation of Victorian reform and the privacy tort.

Key takeaways

  • NSW's Workplace Surveillance Act 2005 requires written notice at least 14 days before camera, computer or tracking surveillance, visible cameras and signs, a notified computer policy, and a magistrate's authority for covert surveillance.
  • NSW bans surveillance in toilets and change rooms and of employees outside work (except computer surveillance of employer equipment), with penalties up to 50 penalty units.
  • The ACT's Workplace Privacy Act 2011 adds a duty to consult employees; Victoria bans surveillance in toilets, change rooms and lactation rooms and has promised broader laws after a 2025 inquiry.
  • Listening device laws differ: Queensland, Victoria and the NT allow participant recording; other states generally require consent of the parties.
  • The Privacy Act's employee records exemption survived the 2024 reforms, but a statutory tort for serious invasions of privacy has applied since June 10, 2025.
  • The Fair Work Commission accepts lawful monitoring evidence alongside performance evidence, as in Cheikho v IAG (2023).

Frequently asked questions

Is employee monitoring legal in Australia?

Yes, subject to state and territory law. In NSW and the ACT, employers must give written notice before surveillance begins and need a court authority for covert surveillance; Victoria bans surveillance in toilets and change rooms; and every state controls recording of conversations. The federal Privacy Act largely exempts employee records.

How much notice must NSW employers give before monitoring?

At least 14 days' written notice before camera, computer or tracking surveillance starts, unless the employee agrees to a shorter period, and notice before a new employee starts work. The notice must state the type of surveillance, how and when it will happen, and whether it is continuous or intermittent.

Can my employer monitor my work laptop at home in Australia?

Generally yes, if it is employer-provided equipment and, in NSW, you received notice under a computer surveillance policy. NSW prohibits surveillance of employees when they are not at work, except computer surveillance of equipment provided by the employer.

Is covert workplace surveillance legal in Australia?

In NSW and the ACT only with an authority from a magistrate, granted solely to establish whether an employee is involved in unlawful activity at work, and for a limited period. Covert surveillance without an authority is an offense.

Does the Privacy Act apply to employee monitoring?

Mostly not for current and former employees of private-sector employers, because of the employee records exemption, which the 2024 reforms left in place. It does apply to job applicants and contractors. The separate statutory tort for serious invasions of privacy, in force since June 2025, may reach egregious surveillance.

Can I secretly record a meeting with my boss in Australia?

It depends on the state. In Queensland, Victoria and the Northern Territory a party to a conversation may generally record it. In NSW, Western Australia, South Australia, Tasmania and the ACT, recording a private conversation without the other parties' consent is generally an offense, with limited exceptions.
