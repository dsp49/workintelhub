Employee Monitoring Laws in Canada: PIPEDA, Ontario, Quebec

P Pavan
November 20, 2026 · 11 min read

Canada has no single employee monitoring law. Which rules apply depends on where the employee works and what kind of business employs them. Federally regulated employers, such as banks, airlines, railways and telecom companies, are covered by the federal private-sector privacy law, PIPEDA. Private employers in Alberta, British Columbia and Quebec are covered by provincial privacy statutes that apply to employee information. Ontario requires employers with 25 or more employees to publish a written electronic monitoring policy. Private employers in the remaining provinces have no general privacy statute covering their employees, though courts, arbitrators and human rights law still impose limits. Canada's privacy commissioners said jointly in 2023 that workplace privacy laws are outdated or absent and called for the gaps to be closed.

This guide covers each regime, the court and arbitration tests that apply everywhere, the federal reform bill tabled in June 2026, and a compliance checklist for employers with Canadian staff. For the US position see laws by state; for the UK and EU, UK and EU.

PIPEDA and federally regulated employers

PIPEDA applies to employee personal information only where the employer operates a federal work, undertaking or business. Such employers may collect, use and disclose employee information only for purposes a reasonable person would consider appropriate in the circumstances. In Eastmond v. Canadian Pacific Railway (2004 FC 852), the Federal Court held that cameras at a rail yard were reasonable because they addressed real security problems, were not watched live and were used only to investigate incidents. The reasoning is usually summarized as four questions: is there a demonstrated need, is the monitoring likely to be effective, is the loss of privacy proportionate to the benefit, and is there a less invasive alternative.

The Office of the Privacy Commissioner's 2023 "Privacy in the workplace" guidance adds that monitoring should be specific, targeted and appropriate rather than continuous and general, that employers should be transparent about it, and that employees should be able to see and correct information about them. Employees can complain to the OPC, which investigates and reports but cannot fine under PIPEDA as it stands.

Alberta, British Columbia and Quebec

  • Alberta: PIPA lets employers collect "personal employee information" without consent where it is reasonable for recruiting, managing or ending employment, but current employees must be given reasonable notice first. The Alberta privacy commissioner has treated vehicle GPS data as personal employee information. PIPA was under legislative review in 2024 and 2025.
  • British Columbia: PIPA allows collection of information reasonably required to establish, manage or end employment, with notice instead of consent. The BC commissioner's guidance covers video, audio, software and GPS monitoring and recommends a privacy impact assessment before monitoring begins.
  • Quebec: Law 25 reshaped the private-sector act in stages. Since September 22, 2023, anyone using technology that can identify, locate or profile a person, including analysis of work performance, must inform them of it and of how to activate those functions. Privacy impact assessments are required for projects to acquire, develop or overhaul information systems involving personal information, which in practice covers new monitoring tools. Employees must be told when a decision about them is made solely by automated processing. Section 5 of the Quebec Charter protects respect for private life, and the Civil Code (articles 35 and 36) treats intentional surveillance of private life as an invasion of privacy.

Manitoba passed a private-sector privacy act in 2013 that was never brought into force, so its private employers, like those in the other provinces and territories without a statute, rely on common law and arbitral limits.

Ontario's electronic monitoring policy

Since the Working for Workers Act, 2022 (Bill 88) added Part XI.1 to the Employment Standards Act, any employer with 25 or more employees in Ontario on January 1 of a year must have a written policy on electronic monitoring for that year. The count includes all Ontario locations, and an employer that falls below 25 later in the year remains covered.

  • Deadline: the first policies were due October 11, 2022; in later years the policy must be in place by March 1.
  • Content: whether the employer electronically monitors employees; if so, how and in what circumstances; the purposes for which information obtained may be used; the date the policy was prepared; and the date of any changes.
  • Copies: to existing employees within 30 days of the date the policy must be in place or is changed; to new employees within 30 days of starting; to temporary help agency assignment employees within 24 hours of the assignment starting.
  • Retention: keep every version for three years after it stops being in effect.
  • Limits: the rule requires disclosure only. It does not create a right not to be monitored, and employment standards officers can investigate only failures to provide the policy.

From January 1, 2026, Ontario employers with 25 or more employees must also say in publicly advertised job postings whether artificial intelligence is used to screen, assess or select applicants. The monitoring policy template can be adapted to Ontario's required content.

Courts, arbitrators and the reasonableness test

  • R. v. Cole (2012 SCC 53): a teacher had a reasonable, though diminished, expectation of privacy in personal information on a work-issued laptop; workplace policies are one factor in the totality of the circumstances but do not decide the question.
  • Jones v. Tsige (2012 ONCA 32): the Ontario Court of Appeal recognized the tort of intrusion upon seclusion, after a bank employee repeatedly looked up a colleague's partner's bank records, with damages of up to $20,000 without proof of financial loss (the plaintiff received $10,000). Unjustified intrusive monitoring of private matters can found such a claim.
  • Arbitration: in unionized workplaces, employer rules, including monitoring policies, must meet the KVP test (consistent with the collective agreement, reasonable, clear, brought to employees' attention and consistently enforced), which the Supreme Court endorsed in Irving Pulp and Paper (2013 SCC 34). Arbitrators balance the employer's interests against employee privacy, and covert video is usually admissible only where there were reasonable grounds and no less intrusive alternative.
  • Human rights law applies to monitoring that targets or disproportionately affects protected groups, including disability-related productivity tracking.

Federal reform: Bill C-36

Bill C-27, which would have replaced PIPEDA's private-sector rules, died when Parliament was prorogued on January 6, 2025. On June 15, 2026 the government tabled Bill C-36, which would enact a Protecting Privacy and Consumer Data Act, repeal Part 1 of PIPEDA, and create a Digital Safety and Data Protection Commission with penalties of up to $10 million or 3% of gross global revenue. Its treatment of employee data and its progress through Parliament should be checked before relying on it; for now PIPEDA governs federally regulated employers.

A compliance checklist

  1. Map every Canadian employee to the applicable regime: federal, Alberta, British Columbia, Quebec or none.
  2. Document the purpose of each monitoring tool and why it is necessary, effective and proportionate, with less intrusive alternatives considered.
  3. Give notice before monitoring starts; in Alberta and British Columbia notice replaces consent, and in Quebec notice of identifying, locating or profiling functions is mandatory.
  4. In Ontario, publish the electronic monitoring policy by March 1 each year, distribute it on the statutory timelines and keep versions for three years.
  5. In Quebec, complete a privacy impact assessment before deploying a new monitoring system and disclose solely automated decisions.
  6. Configure for proportionality: avoid continuous keystroke logging, screenshots and webcam monitoring unless there is a specific need.
  7. In unionized workplaces, test policies against the collective agreement and the KVP criteria, and consult.
  8. Allow employees to access their monitoring data, and route decisions through a person.

Key takeaways

  • Canada has no single employee monitoring law: PIPEDA covers federally regulated employers, Alberta and BC PIPA and Quebec's private-sector act cover private employers in those provinces, and other provinces have no general statute for employees.
  • Under PIPEDA monitoring must serve purposes a reasonable person would consider appropriate; Eastmond v. Canadian Pacific Railway (2004) frames the test as need, effectiveness, proportionality and no less invasive alternative.
  • Alberta and BC allow collection without consent for reasonable employment purposes with notice; Quebec's Law 25 requires notice of identifying, locating or profiling technology and impact assessments for new systems.
  • Ontario employers with 25 or more employees must publish a written electronic monitoring policy by March 1 each year, give copies within 30 days and keep versions for three years; from 2026 job postings must disclose AI screening.
  • R. v. Cole (2012), Jones v. Tsige (2012) and the KVP arbitration test limit monitoring even where no statute applies.
  • Bill C-27 died in January 2025; Bill C-36, tabled June 15, 2026, would replace PIPEDA's private-sector rules if passed.

Frequently asked questions

Is employee monitoring legal in Canada?

Yes, within limits. Federally regulated employers must meet PIPEDA's reasonable-purpose test; private employers in Alberta, British Columbia and Quebec must follow provincial privacy laws that require notice and proportionality; Ontario employers with 25 or more employees must publish a monitoring policy; and courts and arbitrators everywhere weigh monitoring against reasonable expectations of privacy.

Do Ontario employers have to tell employees they are monitored?

Employers with 25 or more employees in Ontario on January 1 must have a written electronic monitoring policy saying whether and how they monitor and for what purposes, and give it to employees within 30 days. The rule requires disclosure but does not limit what can be monitored.

Does PIPEDA apply to my employer?

Only if your employer is federally regulated, such as a bank, airline, railway, interprovincial trucking company or telecommunications provider. Other private employers are governed by provincial law in Alberta, British Columbia and Quebec, or by no general privacy statute elsewhere.

Do Canadian employers need consent to monitor employees?

Usually not. Alberta and BC allow collection of employee information without consent for reasonable employment purposes if employees are notified, and PIPEDA uses a reasonable-purpose test for federal employers. Notice and proportionality are the main requirements; Quebec adds mandatory notice of tracking and profiling technology.

Can my employer read my personal files on a work laptop in Canada?

Not without limits. In R. v. Cole the Supreme Court held that employees can have a reasonable, though diminished, expectation of privacy in personal information on work computers, and Ontario recognizes a tort of intrusion upon seclusion. Employer policies matter, but they do not erase all privacy.

What does Quebec's Law 25 require for monitoring?

Since September 2023, organizations using technology that can identify, locate or profile people, including analysis of work performance, must inform them and explain how to activate those functions. Privacy impact assessments are required for new information systems, and employees must be told of decisions made solely by automated processing.
