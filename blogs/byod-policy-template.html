BYOD Policy Template: Security, Privacy and Reimbursement

P Pavan
November 13, 2026 · 11 min read

Most employees already use their own devices for work, whether or not there is a policy. Ivanti's 2024 research found 81% of office workers used a personal device for some work, and that 78% of organizations that banned it said employees did it anyway. A bring-your-own-device policy turns that reality into something the company can secure and defend: which devices and apps are allowed, what the employer installs and can see, what happens if the phone is lost, who pays for it, and what happens to company data when the employee leaves.

This template covers phones, tablets and personal laptops. It follows the NIST mobile security guidance (SP 800-124 Revision 2 and the SP 1800-22 BYOD practice guide), the expense reimbursement laws that require employers to pay part of a personal phone bill in several states, wage and hour rules for after-hours use, privacy notice laws, and the NLRA limits on device rules. For the narrower question of phones on the work floor, see the cell phone policy at work.

Why a BYOD policy matters

  • Use is already widespread. In Ivanti's research, half of office workers logged into work systems on personal devices and 32 to 40% of them said their employer did not know.
  • Mobile is the attack surface. Verizon's 2025 Mobile Security Index found 85% of organizations reported rising mobile attacks and 80% had faced phishing or smishing; only 4% had implemented all eight recommended mobile protections. Lookout reported in early 2026 that about 65% of mobile phishing arrives outside email, through texts and messaging apps, where click rates run several times higher.
  • Credentials live on phones. SlashNext's 2023 survey found 71% of employees stored sensitive work passwords on personal phones.
  • Legal exposure runs both ways: data breaches and lost devices on one side, and on the other, reimbursement claims, overtime claims for after-hours work, and privacy claims when an employer sees or wipes personal data.

Three approaches: MDM, work profile, app-only

ApproachWhat is installedEmployer controlEmployee privacyBest for
Full mobile device management (MDM)A management profile over the whole deviceHigh: passcode, encryption, app inventory, full wipeLow; rarely appropriate for personal devicesCompany-owned devices
Work profile or user enrollmentA separate managed container (Android work profile, iOS and macOS user enrollment)Controls work apps and data only; can wipe the containerHigh: personal apps, photos, messages and usage stay privateMost BYOD programs
App-level management (MAM)Policies on specific apps (email, Teams, Office) with no device enrollmentControls data inside managed apps: copy-paste, save-as, PIN, selective wipeHighestLight use: email and chat on a personal phone

NIST SP 800-124 Revision 2 (May 2023) and the National Cybersecurity Center of Excellence's SP 1800-22 BYOD practice guide both recommend separating work and personal data rather than managing the whole personal device. For personal laptops, the equivalent is a virtual desktop or browser-based access with conditional access checks, rather than installing management software on a personal computer.

Google's documentation for Android work profiles says personal apps, data and usage remain private and that work apps cannot read personal SMS on Android 11 and later; Apple's user enrollment similarly separates managed data and does not give the organization the device's serial number or personal content. The policy template

1. Purpose and scopeThis policy sets the conditions under which employees may use personally owned phones, tablets and computers ("personal devices") to access Company email, applications or data. It applies to all employees, contractors and temporary staff. Participation is [voluntary / required for roles listed in Appendix A].
2. Eligible devicesPersonal devices must run a supported operating system version receiving security updates, must not be jailbroken or rooted, and must be enrolled through [work profile / user enrollment / app protection] before accessing Company data.

Security requirementsA screen lock with a PIN of at least six digits or biometric unlock; automatic lock after [5] minutes; device encryption; operating system and app updates installed within [14] days of release; Company apps installed only from the official app stores through the Company portal.
4. What the Company can see[Insert the table above.] The Company will not access personal content and does not track device location.
5. Acceptable useCompany data must stay in Company-managed apps. Do not copy Company data to personal apps, personal cloud storage or personal email, and do not use unapproved AI tools with Company data. Do not allow others to use the work profile.
6. Lost or stolen devicesReport a lost or stolen device to [IT contact] within [24 hours]. The Company will remove Company data from the device; it will not erase personal data except as described in section 7.
7. Remote wipeThe Company may remove the work profile and Company data remotely when a device is lost, when employment ends, or when the device no longer meets security requirements. [If full enrollment is used:] A full device wipe will be performed only with the employee's consent or when the device is reported lost, and employees are responsible for backing up personal data.
8. ReimbursementEmployees in roles that require use of a personal device receive [a stipend of $__ a month / reimbursement of __% of their monthly plan], paid [monthly] [as a taxable stipend / under an accountable plan]. In states that require reimbursement, the Company will reimburse at least a reasonable share of the cost.
9. Working timeNon-exempt employees should not read or respond to work messages outside scheduled hours unless asked to do so. All work time on a personal device, including outside scheduled hours, must be recorded and will be paid.
10. SupportIT supports Company apps and the work profile only. Personal device repairs and carrier issues are the employee's responsibility.
11. Leaving the CompanyOn the last day, the Company will remove the work profile and Company apps. Employees must not retain Company data on personal devices or accounts.
12. Legal rightsNothing in this policy restricts employees' rights under Section 7 of the National Labor Relations Act to communicate about wages, hours and working conditions, including on personal devices.
13. AcknowledgmentI have read this policy, understand what the Company can and cannot see on my device, and agree to its terms. [Signature, date.]

Reimbursement laws

Federal law requires reimbursement only where unreimbursed business costs push a non-exempt employee below minimum wage. Several states go further:

  • California: Labor Code 2802 requires reimbursement of necessary business expenses, and Cochran v. Schwan's Home Service (Cal. Ct. App. 2014) held that when employees are required to use personal cell phones for work, the employer must reimburse "a reasonable percentage" of the bill, even on unlimited plans and even if someone else pays it.
  • Illinois: 820 ILCS 115/9.5 requires reimbursement of necessary expenditures, including phones, within 30 days of submission; a written policy may set a reasonable cap but not zero.
  • Iowa, Montana, New Hampshire, North Dakota and South Dakota have general necessary-expense reimbursement statutes; Massachusetts and Minnesota cover certain expenses and equipment; New York and the District of Columbia have narrower rules.

Common practice is a flat monthly stipend (often in the $25 to $75 range) or a percentage of the plan. Stipends paid without substantiation are taxable wages; reimbursements of substantiated business use under an accountable plan are not. The expense reimbursement policy covers the mechanics, and the IRS treats employer-provided phones used primarily for business as a tax-free working-condition fringe.

After-hours use and overtime

For non-exempt employees, time spent reading and answering work email and messages is hours worked under the FLSA if the employer suffers or permits it (29 CFR 785.11 to 785.13), and a personal phone makes after-hours work easy to do and hard to record. Claims for unpaid after-hours phone time are a recurring wage and hour theme. Three safeguards: tell non-exempt staff not to work outside scheduled hours unless asked; give them a way to record any time they do work; and consider app settings that mute work notifications outside shifts. Several jurisdictions have right-to-disconnect rules; see right to disconnect laws and monitoring after hours.

Privacy, remote wipe and labor law

  • Notice: Connecticut, Delaware and New York require written notice of electronic monitoring of employees' email, phone or internet use, which covers BYOD monitoring of work accounts; see employee monitoring laws by state.
  • Remote wipe: courts have been reluctant to treat wiping an employee's personal phone as a federal computer crime, but a wipe that destroys personal data without clear consent invites state-law claims for conversion, privacy invasion or trespass to chattels. Use selective wipe of the work profile, and get specific written consent if full wipe is ever possible.
  • Personal data access: accessing personal accounts on a device can violate the Stored Communications Act, as an Ohio federal court held in Lazette v. Kulmatycki (2013).
  • NLRA: device rules that a reasonable employee would read as restricting protected communication, such as a ban on discussing pay on personal phones or a broad prohibition on photographing the workplace, are judged under the NLRB's Stericycle standard (2023), which remained in force in late 2026 though the General Counsel had asked the Board to revisit it. Include the savings clause in section 12.
  • UK and EU staff: BYOD monitoring is personal-data processing requiring a lawful basis, a data protection impact assessment and a specific notice; see employee monitoring laws in the UK.

Rolling it out

  1. Decide which roles need device access and whether participation is voluntary.
  2. Choose the approach (work profile for most; app protection for light use) and configure it in the device management platform.
  3. Publish the policy with the "what we can and cannot see" table; employees enroll more readily when they know.
  4. Set the stipend or reimbursement and check the state rules for every location.
  5. Brief managers on after-hours messaging for non-exempt staff.
  6. Add device steps to the onboarding and offboarding checklists.
  7. Review the policy annually and when operating systems change their management features.

Key takeaways

  • Personal-device use is near universal: 81% of office workers use a personal device for some work (Ivanti), so a BYOD policy is about securing existing practice, not permitting something new.
  • Use a work profile or user enrollment for most personal devices, and app-level management for light use; reserve full device management for company-owned devices, as NIST SP 800-124 Rev. 2 and SP 1800-22 recommend.
  • Tell employees exactly what the employer can see (device compliance, work apps and data) and cannot see (personal apps, photos, messages, location), and limit remote wipe to the work profile.
  • California (Cochran v. Schwan's, 2014) requires reimbursing a reasonable share of a personal phone bill when phone use is required; Illinois and several other states have reimbursement statutes.
  • After-hours messages on personal phones are compensable time for non-exempt employees; tell them not to work off schedule, give them a way to record time, and consider muting notifications.
  • Notice laws in Connecticut, Delaware and New York, the Stored Communications Act, state privacy torts, the NLRA's Stericycle standard and UK GDPR all shape what a BYOD policy may do.

Frequently asked questions

What is a BYOD policy?

A bring-your-own-device policy sets the rules for employees using personally owned phones, tablets or computers for work: which devices are allowed, required security settings, what the employer installs and can see, what happens if a device is lost, reimbursement, after-hours work, and how company data is removed when the employee leaves.

Can my employer see my personal data on a BYOD phone?

Not with a properly configured work profile or app-level management. The employer can see device compliance information and the contents of managed work apps; it cannot see personal photos, messages, personal email, call history, browsing in personal apps or location. Full device enrollment gives more access and is rarely appropriate for personal phones.

Can my employer wipe my personal phone?

With a work profile or app management, the employer can remove only the work profile and company data. A full device wipe is possible only if the device was fully enrolled, and should require clear prior consent; wiping personal data without consent can lead to state-law claims. Back up personal data regardless.

Do employers have to pay for employees' personal phones?

Under federal law, only if unreimbursed costs would push a non-exempt employee's pay below minimum wage. California requires employers to reimburse a reasonable percentage of the phone bill when personal phone use is required, and Illinois, Iowa, Montana, New Hampshire, North Dakota and South Dakota have expense reimbursement statutes. Many employers pay a monthly stipend.

Is a BYOD stipend taxable?

A flat stipend paid without substantiation of business use is taxable wages. Reimbursement of substantiated business costs under an accountable plan is not taxable. The IRS treats employer-provided phones used primarily for business as a tax-free working-condition fringe benefit.

Do non-exempt employees get paid for checking work email on their phone?

Yes, if the employer knows or should know the work is happening. Under the FLSA, time spent reading and responding to work messages is hours worked. BYOD policies should tell non-exempt employees not to work outside scheduled hours unless asked, and provide a way to record any time worked.

What should a BYOD policy include?

Scope and eligibility, device and security requirements, the enrollment method, a clear statement of what the employer can and cannot see, acceptable use of company data, lost-device reporting, remote wipe limits, reimbursement, after-hours work rules, support boundaries, offboarding steps, an NLRA savings clause and an acknowledgment.

Can an employer require employees to use personal phones for work?

In most states yes, but requiring it triggers reimbursement obligations in California and other states with expense laws, and makes after-hours use compensable for non-exempt staff. Many employers offer a company phone as an alternative for employees who do not want work apps on their own device.
