Revenue per Employee: Formula and Benchmarks

P Pavan
November 26, 2026 · 7 min read

Revenue per employee is total revenue for a period divided by the average number of full-time equivalent employees over the same period. A company with $14.2 million in revenue and 96 average FTE has revenue per employee of about $147,900.

The formula is simple; the benchmark is not. Revenue per employee varies so much by industry and business model that a single "good" number would be meaningless, and many figures circulating online do not say how they were calculated. Rather than repeat them, this page shows how to calculate the figure consistently, gives a calculator with year-on-year change, and explains how to build your own benchmark from peers' annual reports filed with the SEC and from Bureau of Labor Statistics productivity data.

The short answer

Revenue per employee = revenue / average FTE. Use the same period for both, and average headcount, not the year-end figure.

Your own trend is the first benchmark. Three to five years of the same calculation tells you more than any industry table.

For an external benchmark, compute it yourself from three to five peers' 10-K filings, using the same method you use for your own figure.

The formula, and the four choices inside it

Revenue per employee = total revenue for the period / average FTE headcount for the period

  • Revenue. Net revenue from the income statement, for a full year or the trailing twelve months. Do not mix a quarter's revenue with an annual headcount.
  • Average, not year-end, headcount. (Start + end) / 2 at minimum, or the average of twelve monthly counts. A growing company that divides by year-end headcount understates the figure; a shrinking one overstates it.
  • FTE, not heads. Convert part-time staff to FTE by dividing their scheduled hours by full-time hours. Ten people at 20 hours a week in a 40-hour company are five FTE.
  • Contractors. Decide whether to include them and say which. If you outsource work that employees used to do, revenue per employee rises with no change in productivity, so report a second figure that includes contractor FTE.

The figure is one of three standard productivity formulas, alongside output per labor hour and utilization; the employee productivity formula guide works through all three.

Revenue per employee calculator

Average FTE is (start + end) / 2. For a more precise figure, average twelve monthly counts and enter that number as both start and end. Revenue is nominal; adjust for price changes before comparing across years with high inflation. Nothing typed here leaves your browser.

Worked example

Revenue of $14.2 million on average FTE of 96 gives $147,900 per employee. The prior year was $12.8 million on 91 FTE, or $140,700, so the figure rose 5.1 percent. Revenue grew 10.9 percent while FTE grew 5.5 percent: the company added revenue faster than people.

Now add six contractor FTE. Including them, the figure is about $139,200. If the prior year had no contractors, the like-for-like comparison is $139,200 against $140,700, a slight fall. Part of the apparent gain came from moving work off the payroll rather than from doing it more productively. That is the most common way revenue per employee misleads, and it is why the contractor line belongs in the report.

Why this page has no industry benchmark table

Revenue per employee differs between industries by multiples, not percentages, and within an industry by business model. A distributor that resells goods books the full sale price as revenue; a services firm earns a fee; a company that outsources manufacturing counts none of the factory workers. Two firms with identical productivity can differ several-fold on this measure. Published tables also mix year-end and average headcount, global and domestic staff, and fiscal years, usually without saying so.

We could not find an original public source that publishes revenue per employee by industry with a stated method, so we do not print one. The same problem affects national productivity tables, where sources disagree by more than the gaps between neighboring countries; our page on productivity statistics by country shows how much the dataset and price base change the answer.

How to build a benchmark you can defend

  1. Choose three to five peers with your business model, not just your industry code: similar revenue type (product, service, resale), similar degree of outsourcing, similar size if possible.
  2. Pull their annual reports from SEC EDGAR. Public US companies file a 10-K each year, searchable on EDGAR full-text search. Revenue is on the income statement. Headcount is in Item 1, Business: Regulation S-K Item 101(c)(2)(ii) requires a description of human capital resources, including the number of persons employed.
  3. Note what the headcount means. The 10-K figure is usually a point-in-time count, often at fiscal year-end, and may include part-time and international staff. Average the current and prior year's counts to approximate average headcount, and record any footnote about part-time or contract workers.
  4. Calculate every peer the same way, and your own company the same way, for the same three to five years.
  5. Compare trends before levels. If your figure grows in line with peers, the level gap is probably business model. If it diverges, that is worth investigating.

Private peers do not file 10-Ks. For them, the honest benchmark is your own trend, plus the direction of productivity in your industry from the BLS.

Using BLS productivity data for context

The BLS productivity program publishes labor productivity, defined as output per hour worked, for the nonfarm business sector and for detailed industries in manufacturing, mining, trade and selected services, including a downloadable table of detailed-industry labor productivity. In its release of November 5, 2026, BLS reported that nonfarm business labor productivity increased 3.6 percent at an annualized rate in the third quarter of 2026, and 2.1 percent from the same quarter a year earlier (preliminary figures).

These figures are a context check, not a direct comparison. BLS measures real output per hour, adjusted for price changes; revenue per employee is nominal revenue per head. To compare your trend with your industry's, deflate your revenue growth by your own price increases and use hours rather than heads if hours changed. If your industry's output per hour is rising and your price-adjusted revenue per FTE is flat, that is a signal worth looking into.

What to do with the number

  • Calculate it quarterly or annually, on trailing twelve months. Shorter windows are dominated by seasonality and hiring timing.
  • Read it with revenue per labor dollar. Revenue per employee can rise because you replaced senior people with junior ones; revenue divided by loaded labor cost, from the employee cost calculator, shows whether the change paid.
  • Do not push it down to teams. Most teams do not generate revenue directly, and allocating it creates arguments, not insight. Use team-level measures instead; our list of workforce productivity metrics gives twelve with their formulas and blind spots.
  • Look for the cause elsewhere. The figure shows direction, not cause. Utilization, capacity and meeting-load data explain why it moved; the workforce productivity platforms we reviewed report those by team, and our workforce productivity software guide sorts them by which questions they answer.

Key takeaways

  • Revenue per employee is revenue for the period divided by average FTE headcount for the same period.
  • Use average FTE, convert part-time staff, and report a second figure including contractor FTE.
  • Outsourcing raises revenue per employee without any change in productivity; the contractor line exposes it.
  • There is no meaningful cross-industry benchmark; business model drives the level more than productivity does.
  • Build an external benchmark from three to five peers' 10-K filings, calculated the same way, and compare trends first.
  • BLS output-per-hour data gives industry direction; it is real and per hour, so adjust your figure before comparing.

Frequently asked questions

How do you calculate revenue per employee?

Divide total revenue for a period by the average number of full-time equivalent employees in the same period. Average FTE is at minimum the start and end headcount divided by two. A company with $14.2 million revenue and 96 average FTE has revenue per employee of about $147,900.

What is a good revenue per employee?

There is no cross-industry figure worth quoting, because business model drives the level: resellers book full sale prices, service firms earn fees, and outsourcers exclude production staff. The useful comparisons are your own trend and peers with your business model, calculated the same way.

Where can I find revenue per employee for public companies?

In their annual 10-K filings on SEC EDGAR. Revenue is on the income statement, and Regulation S-K requires the business section to state the number of persons employed. Divide one by the other, noting that the headcount is usually a point-in-time figure.

Should contractors be included in revenue per employee?

Report it both ways. Excluding contractors is the standard definition, but if work moves from employees to contractors the figure rises with no productivity change. A second figure including contractor FTE shows whether the gain is real.

Is revenue per employee a good productivity measure?

It is the hardest productivity figure to manipulate from inside a team and the least actionable. It shows whether the organization is adding revenue faster than people, not why. Pair it with revenue per labor dollar and team-level measures.

How is revenue per employee different from labor productivity?

Labor productivity, as BLS measures it, is real output per hour worked, adjusted for price changes. Revenue per employee is nominal revenue per FTE. Price increases raise revenue per employee without any change in real productivity.
