How to Make a Shift Schedule, Step by Step

P Pavan
November 26, 2026 · 8 min read

To make a shift schedule, work out how many people you need at each hour you are open, divide those hours into shifts, collect everyone's availability, fill the shifts against it, check each person's weekly hours and rest gaps, and publish the schedule at least two weeks ahead. The order matters: a schedule built from people first and demand second is the one that gets rewritten in swap requests by Wednesday.

This guide takes the seven steps in turn. The calculator in step three turns your opening hours, staff per shift and shift length into the number of shifts and the headcount the week needs, which is the number most managers guess at. The work schedule template is where you fill the grid once you have it.

The short answer

Start from demand. The number of people needed at each hour decides everything else; availability and preferences come after.

Headcount is hours divided by hours per person, plus a buffer. A shop open 12 hours a day, 7 days a week, with 2 people on at all times needs about 168 staffed hours a week before anyone is off sick.

Check before you publish. Weekly overtime over 40 hours, rest gaps between close and open, and any local fair workweek notice rule.

Step 1: Map demand by hour

Write down your opening hours for each day and how many people each hour needs. Use what you already have: sales or transactions by hour from the till, call volumes, patient census, or simply the times the floor feels short. Most businesses have three or four levels a day: a quiet opening, a peak, a shoulder and a close.

Include the work that happens while the doors are shut. Opening and closing tasks, deliveries and cash-up are staffed hours even though no customer sees them, and leaving them out is the most common reason a schedule looks covered on paper and is short in practice.

Step 2: Choose shift lengths and a pattern

Shift length is a trade-off between coverage and people. Eight-hour shifts fit a 16-hour day neatly in two; 12-hour shifts cover 24 hours with two shifts but put four hours of daily overtime on every shift in states with a daily threshold, such as California. Shorter shifts of four to six hours suit a lunch or evening peak and the part-time staff who want them.

  • Fixed shifts give each person the same hours each week. Easiest to plan a life around, hardest to make fair for the unpopular slots.
  • Rotating shifts move people through days, evenings and nights on a cycle. For 24-hour operations, our rotating shift schedules guide covers the Panama, Pitman, DuPont and 4-on-4-off patterns.
  • Split shifts cover two peaks with a gap in the middle. Some states require a premium; see split shift.
  • On-call shifts hold someone in reserve. Several fair workweek laws pay for unused on-call time, and New York City bans it in retail; see on-call scheduling.

Overlap shifts by 15 to 30 minutes where handover matters, and count the overlap as staffed time in the calculator below.

Step 3: Work out shifts and headcount

Enter your hours and staffing level. The calculator rounds shifts up, because a shift that covers part of the day still has to be staffed in full, and adds a buffer for holidays, sickness and turnover so the headcount is the number you need on the payroll, not the number you need on a perfect week.

Assumes the same staffing level for every hour you are open. If your peak needs more people, run it once for the base level and add peak shifts separately. Nothing you enter leaves your browser.

With the defaults (12 hours open plus an hour of opening and closing, 3 people at a time, 8-hour shifts, 7 days), the day needs two shifts per position with 3 hours of overlap to place, 42 shifts a week and 336 staffed hours. At 32 hours a person that is 10.5 people, and with a 12% buffer, 12 on the payroll. The buffer is a judgment: use your own absence and turnover history if you have it.

Step 4: Collect availability and constraints

Before anyone is placed, collect each person's availability as time ranges per day, their maximum weekly hours and their fixed commitments. Our employee availability form generates the form. Then list the hard constraints that override preference:

  • Skills or certifications a shift needs: a keyholder on every open and close, a licensed pharmacist, a forklift ticket.
  • Minors' hour limits under state child labor law.
  • Minimum rest between shifts where a law or agreement sets one.
  • Approved time off.

Step 5: Fill the grid

  1. Place the constrained shifts first. Keyholders and certified roles, because they have the fewest people who can do them.
  2. Spread the unpopular shifts. Closes, weekends and nights rotate, and the rule for who gets them is written down. Publishing the rule settles most fairness complaints before they start.
  3. Fill the remaining shifts against availability, giving hours first to people below their target.
  4. Name an on-call or first-call person for each day, who has agreed to be asked first when someone is out.

The schedule builder in our work schedule template does this fill automatically from each person's maximum hours and shows the coverage and hours totals live. For more than about 20 people, or more than one site, a scheduling app saves the most time here; our comparison of shift scheduling software ranks eight, from Sling's free plan for 30 users to Deputy's auto-scheduling.

Step 6: Check hours, overtime and rest

Before publishing, run three checks on every person's week.

  • Weekly hours. Under the Fair Labor Standards Act, hours over 40 in a workweek are overtime at one and a half times the regular rate, and the regular rate includes shift differentials. Our overtime calculator does it properly. Overtime to cover a gap can be the cheaper choice, but make it a decision rather than a surprise.
  • Daily hours. California and a few other states add a daily overtime threshold, so a 10-hour shift can owe overtime in a 40-hour week.
  • Rest gaps. Sort each person's shifts and flag any close followed by an open. Fair workweek cities set minimum gaps of 9 to 11 hours with premiums for working inside them; our table of predictive scheduling laws lists each.

Step 7: Publish, then manage changes

Publish the schedule at least two weeks ahead, on the same day every week, where everyone can see it on their phone. In Oregon and the fair workweek cities, 14 days is the legal minimum for covered employers, and changes after that cost a premium. Everywhere else it is what makes planning, and swapping, possible.

After publication, most changes are swaps. Write the rule down so they do not need a manager: both people qualified, neither pushed into overtime or a short rest gap, and both confirming in writing. Our shift swap policy has the wording, and the top shift scheduling tools run swaps and open shifts in the app with those checks applied.

Five mistakes that break a schedule

  • Scheduling to the average day. The average Tuesday is short at lunch and overstaffed at 3 p.m.
  • No buffer. A headcount that only works if nobody is sick fails every week someone is.
  • Copying last week's schedule forward. It carries last week's mistakes and ignores this week's time off.
  • Publishing late. Every day of notice lost becomes a swap request or a no-show.
  • Unwritten rules for good shifts. If people cannot see how weekends and closes are shared, they assume favoritism.

Key takeaways

  • Build the schedule from demand by hour first, then availability, then preferences.
  • Headcount is staffed hours per week divided by average hours per person, plus a buffer for time off and absence.
  • Include opening and closing tasks in the hours to cover, and round shifts up.
  • Place keyholder and certified shifts first and rotate the unpopular ones under a written rule.
  • Check every person for weekly overtime over 40 hours, daily overtime where a state requires it, and close-to-open rest gaps.
  • Publish at least two weeks ahead; it is the law for covered employers in Oregon and the fair workweek cities.

Frequently asked questions

How do I make a shift schedule for employees?

Map how many people you need at each hour, choose shift lengths that cover those hours, calculate the shifts and headcount, collect availability and constraints, fill the constrained shifts first and then the rest, check overtime and rest gaps, and publish at least two weeks ahead.

How many employees do I need to cover a shift schedule?

Multiply shifts per day by staff per shift, days open and shift length to get staffed hours per week, divide by the average hours each employee works, and add a buffer for time off and absence. Twelve hours a day, seven days, with three people on at a time and 8-hour shifts needs 336 staffed hours, or about 12 people at 32 hours each with a 12% buffer.

What is the best shift length?

It depends on your hours. Eight-hour shifts fit a 16-hour day in two, 12-hour shifts cover 24 hours in two but owe daily overtime in states such as California, and four- to six-hour shifts suit peaks and part-time staff.

How far in advance should a shift schedule be posted?

At least two weeks. It is the legal minimum for covered employers in Oregon and in fair workweek cities including Seattle, Chicago, Philadelphia, Los Angeles and New York City for fast food, and changes after posting can owe premium pay.

What is the easiest way to make a shift schedule?

For a small team, a template that totals coverage and hours as you fill it, such as our work schedule template. For 20 or more people or several sites, a scheduling app that collects availability, runs swaps and flags overtime saves the most time.

How do I make a fair shift schedule?

Write down how unpopular shifts such as closes, weekends and nights are shared, rotate them, give hours first to people below their target, and publish the rule with the schedule so everyone can check it.
