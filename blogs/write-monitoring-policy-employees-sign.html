How to Write an Employee Monitoring Policy

WH The WorkIntel Hub editorial team
May 20, 2026 · 8 min read

An employee monitoring policy is a written document that tells staff which workplace data an employer collects, why, who can see it, how long it is kept, and what rights they have over it. In several US states it is also a legal notice with a specific required form. Most drafts fail for the same reason: they are written to protect the employer from a lawsuit rather than to answer the question an employee actually has.

This guide covers the notice rules that shape the document, the sections a workable policy needs, and the failure mode built into each one. Treat it as drafting guidance rather than legal advice, and have counsel confirm the final wording.

What an Employee Monitoring Policy Has to Do

A monitoring policy has two jobs, and most drafts attempt only one. The first is legal notice: setting out what happens to workplace data in terms a regulator would accept. The second is answering the question every employee has, which is some version of "can my manager see what I typed at four o'clock on Thursday?" Miss the second job and the first one stops protecting you.

Why does that matter so much? Because a policy that fails the employee test gets read once and then filled in by rumor. People do not calibrate to what the document says. They calibrate to the worst thing the document does not rule out. Precision is the only fix, and generic protective wording is precisely what removes it.

A policy also cannot rescue a program that should not exist. If the underlying collection is disproportionate, no amount of careful drafting makes it acceptable, and writing the document is a good moment to notice. The tests we use for that live in our ethical playbook for employee productivity monitoring.

What US Notice Law Requires in the Document

In the United States, the requirement most likely to shape your document is advance written notice, and it varies by state. New York is the clearest published example. Under New York Civil Rights Law section 52-c, an employer that monitors telephone conversations, email, or internet usage by electronic device must give prior written notice upon hiring to all employees subject to that monitoring.

The statute is specific about form and timing, which is what makes it useful as a drafting model even outside New York:

  • Notice must be given upon hiring, before monitoring applies to that person.
  • It must be in writing or electronic form and acknowledged by the employee.
  • The employer must also post the notice in a conspicuous place readily available to affected employees.
  • Civil penalties run up to $500 for a first offense, $1,000 for a second, and $3,000 for a third and each subsequent offense.
  • There is a narrow exemption for processes that manage the type or volume of incoming or outgoing email, voicemail, or internet usage and are performed solely for computer system maintenance or protection.

Note what that implies for your paperwork. You need three artifacts, not one: the policy itself, a per-employee acknowledgment captured at hire, and a posted version somewhere staff genuinely see. A policy buried in a handbook portal satisfies neither duty.

One caution before you copy anything. Monitoring law varies by state, so confirm the requirements with counsel in every jurisdiction where you employ people, including any state rules on recording and interception. If you employ staff in the EU, GDPR adds obligations around lawful basis and data minimization that local counsel should cover.

The Eight Sections an Employee Monitoring Policy Needs

Eight sections cover almost every situation. The third column below is where real documents fall apart, and we have seen every one of these failures more than once.

Section What it must answer How it usually fails
Purpose The specific decisions this data will inform "Legitimate business purposes", which rules nothing out
Scope Which people, devices, hours, and locations Silence on personal devices and non-working hours
What is collected Named data fields, one line each Category words such as "usage and system data"
What is not collected Explicit exclusions, written as commitments The section is missing, so people assume the maximum
Who can see it Named roles and the default view each one gets "Authorized personnel", defined nowhere in the document
Retention A number of days and what happens at expiry "For as long as necessary", which means forever
Employee access How to see and correct your own record Routed through a request process nobody ever uses
Raising a concern A named route and a committed response time Points at the manager the concern is about

Three of those deserve more than a table row, because they decide whether anyone believes the rest.

What is not collected

This is the most valuable section in the document and the one most often absent. Write the exclusions as commitments: no keystroke content, no screen recording, no collection on personal devices, nothing outside scheduled hours. Employees read this part first, and they will quote it back to you if something changes. Our list of data types employee monitoring should never collect is a reasonable starting draft.

Scope

Scope is where hybrid work quietly breaks a policy. Say which devices, which hours, and which locations, and say it in a way that survives someone working from a kitchen table. Pew Research Center found in January 2025 that among US workers whose jobs can be done from home, 75% are now required to be in the office certain days, up from 63% in early 2023, so most policies now have to cover two settings for the same person in the same week. Silence about the home setting is read as coverage of it.

Retention

Give a number. Ninety days, one hundred eighty days, whatever your stated purpose genuinely needs, and then say what happens on day ninety-one. Vague retention is the clearest signal that nobody thought about the data lifecycle, and it grows your exposure quietly: every extra month of history is more material in a breach and more work in a records request.

A Plain Language Rewrite of a Monitoring Clause

The gap between a policy that gets accepted and one that gets forwarded to a lawyer is usually specificity, not tone. Here is a clause of the kind that appears in plenty of handbooks, followed by the version we would write.

Before: The Company may monitor use of Company systems and equipment as it deems necessary for legitimate business purposes, and may retain such data for such period as it considers appropriate.

After: On company laptops, during your scheduled hours, we record which applications and websites you have open and for how long. We use this to plan workload and staffing. We do not record what you type, capture your screen, or collect anything on personal devices or outside your hours. Your manager sees team totals by default. You can see your own record at any time. We delete these records after ninety days.

The rewrite is longer in words and far shorter in ambiguity. It also commits you to things, which is the point. A policy nobody can hold you to is not reassuring, it is just quiet. If your collection method makes that clause hard to write honestly, the method may be the problem, which is one of the tradeoffs we compare in manual versus automatic employee productivity trackers.

The test we would apply before publishing

Hand the draft to someone on the team who was not involved in writing it and ask one question: after reading this, what do you think we can see? If their answer is wider than the truth, the document has failed, however legally sound it is.

How to Get the Policy Reviewed and Published

A monitoring policy should clear legal review and an employee read-through before anyone is asked to acknowledge it, in that order. Legal review catches the obligations you missed. The read-through catches sentences that technically say one thing and are read as another, which is where nearly all the trust damage happens.

  1. Draft in plain language first, before anyone adds protective wording.
  2. Have counsel in each state where you employ people review the substance, not just the format.
  3. Build the acknowledgment step into hiring, so new employees get notice before monitoring applies.
  4. Post the notice somewhere staff actually see it, in addition to filing it in the handbook.
  5. Run the read-through with three or four employees and fix whatever they misread.
  6. Publish the whole policy, not a summary, and hold a live session for questions.
  7. Keep every version dated and available, so changes are visible rather than silent.

Step six is the one that gets cut for time, and cutting it is a false economy. The questions people ask in that session are the ones they would otherwise answer for themselves, incorrectly. If you are publishing alongside a new rollout, the sequencing advice in our guide to rolling out time tracking without losing trust applies here too.

What Happens After Employees Acknowledge It

An acknowledgment is the start of the obligation, not the end of it. Policies drift, usually without malice: a system update adds a data field, a report gets shared more widely than planned, a retention job stops running and nobody notices for a year. None of those feels like a policy change to the person making it, and together they turn an honest document into a false one.

Review the policy annually against what your systems actually collect, and again whenever you add a data source or start employing people in a new state. If reality has moved, change the document and tell people it changed. Quiet edits do more damage than the original policy ever did, because they prove the document was never a commitment.

Keep the stakes in view. Gallup reported in April 2026 that global employee engagement declined for a second year in 2025, reaching its lowest level since 2020, with the decline most acute among managers. A monitoring document is a small lever on that, but it points in one direction or the other. Written honestly, an employee monitoring policy is one of the few artifacts that tells a team what you will not do, and it belongs inside a wider approach to employee productivity rather than substituting for one.

Key takeaways

  • Write for the employee's real question first and the regulator's second; both need specifics.
  • New York requires prior written notice on hiring, employee acknowledgment, and a posted notice, so plan for three artifacts.
  • The exclusions section carries more weight with staff than any other part of the document.
  • Replace every vague phrase with a number, a named role, or a named data field.
  • Have counsel in each state review the wording, then re-review it yearly against reality.

Frequently asked questions

What is an employee monitoring policy?

It is a written document telling staff which workplace data the employer collects, why, who can see it, how long it is kept, and how an employee can view their own record or raise a concern. It works as both a legal notice and an internal commitment you can be held to.

What should an employee monitoring policy include?

Eight sections cover almost every situation: purpose, scope, what is collected, what is not collected, who can see the data, retention, employee access rights, and how to raise a concern. The exclusions section carries as much weight as the inclusions, because it stops people assuming the maximum.

Does New York require a written employee monitoring notice?

Yes. New York Civil Rights Law section 52-c requires employers who monitor telephone conversations, email, or internet usage by electronic device to give prior written notice upon hiring to affected employees, in writing or electronic form and acknowledged by the employee, and to post the notice in a conspicuous place readily available to those employees.

What are the penalties for failing to give monitoring notice in New York?

Civil penalties under New York Civil Rights Law section 52-c run up to 500 dollars for a first offense, 1,000 dollars for a second, and 3,000 dollars for a third and each subsequent offense. The statute exempts processes that manage the type or volume of incoming or outgoing email, voicemail, or internet usage and are performed solely for computer system maintenance or protection.

Do employees have to sign a monitoring policy?

Practice varies by state and by what you collect. Where a signature is required it is usually acknowledgment of notice rather than consent, and New York specifically asks that the notice be acknowledged by the employee in writing or electronic form. Ask counsel what your signature process is actually establishing before you rely on it.

Does a signed policy make monitoring lawful?

No. A signature records that someone was told. Lawfulness depends on the substance: whether you have a legitimate purpose, whether you collect no more than that purpose needs, and whether you meet the notice, recording, and interception rules in each state where you employ people. Have counsel review the substance.

How long should an employee monitoring policy be?

Two pages is plenty for most organizations. Length is not what makes a policy defensible; specificity is. If yours runs past four pages, the extra material is usually protective hedging that makes the document harder to read, easier to ignore, and no safer than the short version.

Should the policy say what we do not collect?

Yes, and we would treat it as the most valuable section in the document. Without stated limits, people assume the maximum. Naming the exclusions, such as no keystroke content, no screen recording, and nothing on personal devices, answers the fear directly and gives employees something concrete to hold you to.

What retention period should we set for monitoring data?

Pick a specific number of days tied to your stated purpose and write down what happens at expiry. Workload and capacity planning rarely need more than a few months of history. Open-ended retention grows your breach exposure for no benefit and is one of the first things a reviewer questions.

How often should we review the employee monitoring policy?

Once a year, and again whenever you add a data source, change a system, or start employing people in a new state. The annual review should compare the document against what your systems actually collect, because policy drift usually happens through small technical changes nobody thought to write down.
