Paid Family Leave by State (2026-2027): Benefits and Rates

P Pavan
November 13, 2026 · 15 min read

The United States has no national paid leave law. The Family and Medical Leave Act gives eligible employees of employers with 50 or more workers 12 weeks of unpaid, job-protected leave. Paid leave is a state matter, and by 2026 fourteen states and the District of Columbia run mandatory paid family and medical leave insurance programs funded by payroll contributions, with Delaware, Minnesota and Maine paying their first benefits in 2026, Maryland collecting contributions from January 2027 for benefits in 2028, and Virginia, the newest, enacted in April 2026 for benefits in December 2028. Nine more states authorize voluntary private paid family leave insurance that employers may buy.

This guide is a working reference for payroll and HR: each program's benefit start date, weeks of leave by type, wage replacement formula and 2026 maximum weekly benefit, the 2026 and announced 2027 contribution rates and how they split between employer and employee, job protection, and the small-employer exemptions. It then covers how the programs interact with FMLA and company policies, what multistate employers must do, and the voluntary states. For the sick-leave mandates that sit alongside these programs, see paid sick leave laws by state.

The federal baseline: FMLA

The Family and Medical Leave Act of 1993 covers private employers with 50 or more employees within 75 miles and all public agencies. An employee with 12 months of service and 1,250 hours in the past year may take up to 12 workweeks of unpaid leave in a 12-month period for the birth or placement of a child, a serious health condition of the employee or a spouse, child or parent, or qualifying military exigencies, and up to 26 weeks to care for a covered servicemember. The employer must maintain health coverage and restore the employee to the same or an equivalent job. Nothing in the Act requires pay; employees may use, or be required to use, accrued paid leave concurrently. About 44% of workers are not eligible, because their employer is too small or their tenure too short.

Every state program described below layers paid benefits on top of this, and most run concurrently with FMLA when both apply. The FMLA forms guide covers the federal paperwork.

How state programs work

The mandatory programs share a structure. A payroll contribution, set as a percentage of wages up to a cap, funds a state insurance pool (or, in some states, an approved private plan). Workers who meet an earnings test claim benefits from the state when they take leave for a covered reason: bonding with a new child, caring for a family member with a serious health condition, the worker's own serious health condition (the "medical" leave, which in California, New Jersey, New York, Rhode Island and Hawaii runs through an older temporary disability insurance program), military family needs, and in several states safe leave for domestic violence. Benefits replace a percentage of the worker's average weekly wage, usually on a progressive scale that replaces 80 to 100% of low wages and 50 to 60% above a threshold tied to the state average weekly wage, up to a weekly maximum that is indexed each year. Job protection, which FMLA provides only for employers with 50 or more workers, is written into most of the newer programs for most employees, often after a short tenure.

Differences that matter to payroll: who pays (employee only, employer only, or split), whether small employers are exempt from the employer share, whether the wage base is capped at the Social Security wage base or uncapped, whether a private plan can substitute, and when the benefit year starts. Differences that matter to employees: the number of weeks, whether leave types share a combined cap, the waiting period, and the earnings threshold.

New in 2026 to 2028: Delaware, Minnesota, Maine, Maryland, Virginia

  • Delaware (Healthy Delaware Families Act) began paying benefits on January 1, 2026 after a year of contributions. The structure is unusual: three separate lines (medical 0.4%, family caregiving 0.08%, parental 0.32%) and an exemption for employers with fewer than 10 employees, with 10 to 24 covered for parental leave only. Employers may pass up to half of each contribution to employees. The maximum benefit is a flat $900 a week, the lowest of the new programs.
  • Minnesota (Paid Leave) began January 1, 2026, with up to 12 weeks of medical and 12 weeks of family leave capped at 20 weeks combined in a benefit year, a maximum benefit of $1,423, and a 0.88% premium of which employers must pay at least half. Minnesota already had Earned Sick and Safe Time (2024); the two run separately.
  • Maine (Paid Family and Medical Leave) collected contributions through 2025 and paid its first benefits on May 1, 2026, at 1.0% of wages split evenly, with employers under 15 employees paying only the employee half. The maximum rose to $1,249.12 on July 1, 2026.
  • Maryland (Time to Care Act, delayed twice) now starts contributions on January 1, 2027 at 0.9%, split evenly for employers with 15 or more employees, with benefits from January 3, 2028 at up to $1,000 a week.
  • Virginia enacted a mandatory program on April 22, 2026 (HB 1207 and SB 2), replacing its 2022 voluntary insurance law. Contributions begin April 1, 2028, benefits December 1, 2028, at 80% wage replacement with a cap around $1,507 and job protection after 120 days of employment. The first premium rate is due to be set by October 1, 2027.

Other changes in 2026: Colorado added up to 12 weeks for a newborn's NICU stay and cut its premium to 0.88%; Washington's job-protection threshold fell to 25 employees (15 in 2027, 8 in 2028) and its 2027 premium rises to 1.2%; Rhode Island raised its maximum to $1,150 in July; the District of Columbia added two weeks of prenatal leave and reset its combined cap to 12 weeks from October 1, 2026.

Voluntary and private-insurance states

Nine states have passed laws that create a regulated market for private paid family leave insurance without requiring employers to offer it: New Hampshire (a state-contracted plan since 2023 at 60% wage replacement up to about $2,128 a week, with a tax credit for participating employers), Vermont (a state-contracted voluntary plan launched in stages from 2023 to 2025), and insurance-authorization laws in Arkansas, Tennessee, Alabama, Texas and Florida (both 2023, minimum two weeks of benefit), South Carolina (2023) and Kentucky (2024). These laws let carriers sell group PFL policies as a defined product; they do not create a benefit for workers whose employers do not buy one. Virginia's 2022 voluntary law was superseded by its 2026 mandate. In the remaining states, paid family leave is whatever the employer's policy says, backed by unpaid FMLA where it applies. The leave statistics and wellness statistics pages have the access figures: the Bureau of Labor Statistics found 49% of private-industry workers had access to paid family and medical leave in March 2026, up sharply in the states with programs.

How state leave interacts with FMLA, PTO and disability

  • Concurrent FMLA. Where the employee is FMLA-eligible and the reason qualifies under both laws, employers should designate the leave as FMLA at the same time, so the 12 federal weeks and the state weeks run together rather than in sequence. Most state programs permit this; some (New York) require the employer to notify the employee.
  • PTO. States differ on whether an employer may require employees to use accrued PTO before or alongside state benefits. California allows employers to require two weeks of vacation before PFL; Massachusetts, Washington and Oregon allow the employee to choose to top up with PTO but prohibit forcing it; Colorado prohibits requiring PTO use. Topping up to full pay is permitted everywhere.
  • Short-term disability. In the five states with statutory disability programs (CA, NJ, NY, RI, HI), the medical portion runs through disability and the family portion through PFL, with separate claim processes. Private STD plans in other states usually offset state medical benefits.
  • Employer-paid leave. An employer's own paid parental leave does not reduce the state benefit unless the employer has an approved private plan that replaces the state program; many large employers run an approved plan to integrate the two.
  • Health insurance must be maintained during FMLA leave; most state programs with job protection require the same.
  • Intermittent leave is available in most state programs in increments of a day or more (Washington requires eight consecutive hours), which complicates tracking against both the state and FMLA entitlements; a single leave ledger per employee, in hours, is the only workable method.

Multistate employers

Coverage follows where the employee works, not where the employer is based. A remote employee in Massachusetts triggers Massachusetts contributions and benefits for a company headquartered in Texas; the test in most states mirrors the unemployment insurance localization rules (work localized in the state, or base of operations in the state). Practical steps: map every employee to a state of work; register with each program (contribution accounts in 14 states and DC by 2027); set up the deduction lines with the correct employee and employer shares and wage caps; decide per state whether to use the state plan or an approved private plan; publish the required notices and posters (most states require a notice at hire and when leave is requested); and maintain one leave policy with state addenda rather than fourteen policies. Payroll providers handle the deductions; they do not handle the leave administration, the designation notices or the job-restoration obligations, which remain with HR. The state labor law changes for 2027 roundup lists the dates to watch.

If you are the employee

Find your state in the table. If it has a program, you file the claim with the state agency (or the employer's approved private carrier), usually online, with medical certification or birth documentation, and benefits are paid by the state, not your employer; most programs let you apply up to 30 days before leave starts. Check the earnings threshold (typically a few hundred to a few thousand dollars in the base period), the waiting week, and whether your employer offers a top-up. Job protection comes from the state program in most of the newer states, from FMLA if your employer has 50 or more employees and you have a year's tenure, and from state family leave acts in California, New Jersey, Connecticut and DC. If your state has no program, your rights are unpaid FMLA leave if you qualify, plus whatever your employer's policy provides; ask HR for the written policy and for the time-off request form. Retaliation for taking protected leave is unlawful in every program.

Key takeaways

  • Fourteen states and the District of Columbia run mandatory paid family and medical leave programs in 2026: California, New Jersey, Rhode Island, New York, Washington, Massachusetts, Connecticut, Oregon, Colorado, DC, Delaware and Minnesota (benefits from January 1, 2026), Maine (May 1, 2026), Maryland (contributions January 2027, benefits January 2028) and Virginia (enacted April 2026, benefits December 2028).
  • Most programs provide 12 weeks of family and 12 of medical leave with combined caps of 12 to 26 weeks; wage replacement is progressive (80 to 100% of low wages, 50 to 66% above a threshold) with 2026 maximums from $900 (Delaware) to $1,765 (California).
  • Contributions range from 0.5% (Connecticut) to 1.3% (California) of wages; California, New Jersey, Rhode Island, New York and Connecticut charge employees only, DC charges employers only, and the newer programs split the cost with small-employer exemptions for the employer share.
  • Announced 2027 changes: California 1.4%, New York 0.452% (cap $451.81), Washington 1.2% (max benefit $1,727), Massachusetts unchanged at 0.88% with a $1,284.61 maximum, Maryland's first contributions at 0.9%.
  • Job protection is built into most newer programs after 90 to 180 days; California, New Jersey, Connecticut and DC rely on separate family leave acts; FMLA protection applies only at employers with 50 or more employees.
  • Nine states (NH, VT, AR, TN, AL, TX, FL, SC, KY) authorize voluntary private PFL insurance without mandating benefits.
  • Coverage follows the employee's state of work; multistate employers must register, deduct and post in every program state, run state leave concurrently with FMLA, and keep one leave ledger in hours.

Frequently asked questions

Which states have paid family leave in 2026?

Mandatory programs paying benefits in 2026: California, New Jersey, Rhode Island, New York, Washington, Massachusetts, Connecticut, Oregon, Colorado, the District of Columbia, Delaware (from January 1), Minnesota (from January 1) and Maine (from May 1). Maryland begins contributions in January 2027 and benefits in January 2028; Virginia's program, enacted in April 2026, pays benefits from December 2028.

How many weeks of paid family leave do states provide?

Most programs provide 12 weeks for bonding and 12 for family care, with own-medical leave of 12 to 52 weeks and combined annual caps of 12 weeks (Connecticut, Oregon, Maine, DC) to 26 weeks (New Jersey, New York, Massachusetts). California and Rhode Island provide 8 weeks of family leave; Delaware provides 12 parental and 6 for other reasons per 24 months; Minnesota caps combined leave at 20 weeks.

Who pays for state paid family leave?

It varies. Employees pay the full contribution in California, New Jersey (family portion), Rhode Island, New York and Connecticut; employers pay all of it in the District of Columbia; Washington, Massachusetts, Oregon, Colorado, Delaware, Minnesota, Maine, Maryland and Virginia split it, usually with small employers exempt from the employer share.

What is the maximum weekly paid family leave benefit?

In 2026: California $1,765; Oregon $1,692.16; Washington $1,647 ($1,727 in 2027); Colorado $1,448.02; Minnesota $1,423; Maine $1,249.12; Massachusetts $1,230.39 ($1,284.61 in 2027); New York $1,228.53; Rhode Island $1,150; New Jersey $1,119; DC $1,100; Connecticut $1,016; Delaware $900.

Does paid family leave run at the same time as FMLA?

Usually yes, when the employee is FMLA-eligible and the reason qualifies under both. Employers should designate FMLA leave concurrently so the 12 federal weeks run with the state weeks; otherwise the employee may be entitled to both in sequence. FMLA applies only to employers with 50 or more employees, so for smaller employers the state program is the only source of leave rights.

Is my job protected while on state paid family leave?

In most of the newer programs, yes: Washington (employers with 25+ employees in 2026, falling to 8 by 2028), Massachusetts, Oregon (after 90 days), Colorado (after 180 days), Delaware, Minnesota (after 90 days), Maine (after 120 days), Maryland, Virginia (after 120 days), New York and Rhode Island. California, New Jersey, Connecticut and DC provide job protection through separate family leave acts with their own employer-size thresholds.

Does a remote employee in another state get that state's paid leave?

Yes. Coverage follows where the employee works. An employer anywhere in the country with an employee working in Massachusetts must register, withhold and remit Massachusetts contributions, and the employee may claim Massachusetts benefits. Most states use their unemployment insurance localization rules to decide which state covers a worker.

Which states have voluntary paid family leave insurance laws?

New Hampshire and Vermont run state-contracted voluntary plans; Arkansas, Tennessee, Alabama, Texas, Florida, South Carolina and Kentucky have authorized private insurers to sell paid family leave policies that employers may choose to buy. None of these require employers to provide paid leave.
