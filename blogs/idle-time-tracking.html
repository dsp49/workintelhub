Idle Time Tracking: How Software Decides You Are Idle

P Pavan
November 26, 2026 · 9 min read

Idle time tracking software marks you idle when it has seen no keyboard or mouse input for a set number of minutes, called the idle threshold. It does not know whether you were thinking, reading, on a call or away from the desk; it only knows the input stopped.

That single rule drives a lot: the idle blocks on a timesheet, the prompt that asks whether to keep or discard the time, the active hours behind every productivity score, and, if an employer deducts idle time from pay, a wage and hour question. This page explains how the threshold works, the defaults in the tools people use most, what happens to idle time once it is flagged, and what the federal rules in 29 CFR Part 785 say about not paying for it.

The short answer

Idle means no input, not no work. The agent starts a countdown at your last keystroke or mouse movement and marks the time idle once the threshold passes.

Defaults are short. ActivTrak and Insightful default to two minutes and Hubstaff to five, and all three let the employer change it.

Deducting idle time is risky for hourly staff. Under the FLSA, short rest breaks and time spent waiting for work are hours worked, so idle minutes cannot simply be subtracted from pay.

How software decides you are idle

Every desktop monitoring agent works the same way at the base. It watches for input events from the keyboard and mouse. Each event resets a timer. If the timer reaches the threshold before the next event, the agent marks the period as idle and returns you to active the moment you touch a key again.

  1. Input stops. You finish typing and start reading a long document, take a call on your phone, or leave the desk.
  2. The countdown runs. Nothing is recorded as idle yet; the agent is waiting to see whether input resumes.
  3. The threshold passes. The agent marks the stretch since the last input as idle (ActivTrak calls it passive time).
  4. Input resumes. Depending on the tool and settings, you see a prompt asking what the time was, or the block is simply flagged on your timeline for a manager to see.

Two consequences follow. First, any work that does not involve the keyboard or mouse looks idle: a video call where you only listen, a whiteboard session, reading on screen, a phone call. Second, the threshold sets the sensitivity. A two-minute threshold will flag a lot of reading; a twenty-minute threshold will miss short breaks entirely. Neither is measuring work, and the productivity monitoring tools we compared all build their active-time totals on this same input rule.

Keep, discard or flag: what happens to idle time

Once a stretch is marked idle, tools handle it in one of three ways, and the difference matters far more than the threshold.

  • A prompt to the employee. Hubstaff's idle prompt asks whether to keep the time, assign it elsewhere or discard it. This is the fairest design, because the person who knows what happened makes the call. It also creates friction: people who were on a call have to remember to click keep, and a hurried discard removes time that was work.
  • A flag for the manager. The time stays on the record but is marked idle on the timesheet or timeline. Nothing is removed automatically, but a manager can read a meeting-heavy day as an idle one.
  • Automatic removal. Idle time is dropped from tracked hours without asking. Our research separates tools where idle time is a prompt, such as Clockify, from those where it works as a deduction. Where those hours feed payroll, this is the setting most likely to underpay someone.

Our Hubstaff alternatives guide notes that reviews describe agents marking people idle during presentations and video calls, which then has to be corrected by hand, and that gig workers paid by tracked hours have lost pay to idle detection. If your hours feed pay, choose a tool and a setting where idle time is a prompt or a flag, not a silent deduction.

Can an employer deduct idle time from pay? The FLSA angle

For nonexempt employees under the Fair Labor Standards Act, pay follows hours worked, and the Department of Labor's interpretive rules in 29 CFR Part 785 decide what counts. None of them mention software, but several decide what an idle flag can and cannot do. This is a summary of federal rules, not legal advice; state law can add more, and salaried exempt employees are not paid by the hour at all.

RuleWhat it saysWhat it means for idle time
29 CFR 785.18, restRest periods of about 5 to 20 minutes are common and must be counted as hours worked.A short break the agent flags as idle is still paid time. Deducting it conflicts with the rule.
29 CFR 785.15, on dutyAn employee who must wait while on duty, and cannot use the time for themselves, is "engaged to wait" and working.Waiting for a ticket, a call or a build with no input is still work time.
29 CFR 785.16, off dutyTime is not worked only if the employee is completely relieved from duty, told in advance, and the period is long enough to use for personal purposes.An idle flag alone does not show that someone was relieved from duty.
29 CFR 785.47, de minimisAn employer may not arbitrarily fail to count any part, however small, of the employee's fixed or regular working time.Trimming small idle gaps out of the scheduled day is not covered by the de minimis rule.
29 CFR 785.11 and 785.13Work suffered or permitted is work time, and management must stop work it does not want rather than accept it unpaid.If people work off the timer, or discard idle time that was work, the employer cannot keep the benefit unpaid.

In practice: an idle flag is evidence that input stopped, not evidence that work stopped. The safe approach for hourly staff is to pay tracked time by default and adjust only when the employee confirms the time was personal, for example a genuine meal break or leaving early. Meal periods are a separate rule, and our break time policy template covers how to write breaks down. For overtime math on the hours that remain, use the overtime calculator.

A simple test before you deduct. Could the employee have left, and did they know they could? If not, the idle minutes are probably hours worked.

Setting idle tracking up fairly

  • Pick a threshold that matches the work. A two-minute default suits data entry and flags almost every reading task. Meeting-heavy and reading-heavy roles need a longer one, or idle tracking off.
  • Prompt, do not deduct. Let the employee keep or discard idle time, and default the payroll export to keeping it.
  • Exclude calls. Where the tool allows it, turn idle and activity recording off for call and meeting projects, as Hubstaff's own guidance suggests for activity recording.
  • Read idle with the score. Idle minutes lower active time, which lowers every employee productivity score built on it, and the activity level percentage drops for the same reason.
  • Write it down. Say what the threshold is and what happens to idle time in the monitoring policy. Connecticut, Delaware and New York require written notice of electronic monitoring.
  • Do not create a jiggler incentive. Punishing idle flags is how people end up with a mouse jiggler, and a dishonesty finding is worse for everyone than an honest idle block.

Idle time also feeds how applications are scored: ActivTrak, for example, classifies passive time by the last application used, so how you set up productive and unproductive app classification changes what an idle block counts against. Key takeaways

  • Software marks you idle after a set number of minutes with no keyboard or mouse input; it cannot tell reading, calls or thinking from absence.
  • Documented defaults: ActivTrak and Insightful two minutes, Hubstaff five (10, 20 or custom); Teams goes Away after roughly five minutes and Slack after ten.
  • What happens next matters most: a keep or discard prompt, a flag for the manager, or an automatic deduction.
  • Under 29 CFR 785.18, rest breaks of about 5 to 20 minutes are hours worked, and under 785.15 time spent waiting on duty is work.
  • An idle flag shows input stopped, not that work stopped; pay tracked time by default and adjust only when the employee confirms personal time.
  • Set the threshold per role, exclude calls, and write the rules into the monitoring policy.

Frequently asked questions

How long before software marks you idle?

It depends on the tool and the employer's settings. ActivTrak marks passive time after two minutes by default, Insightful defaults to two minutes with a range of 30 seconds to 10 minutes, and Hubstaff shows an idle prompt after five minutes by default, configurable to 10, 20 or a custom value.

Does idle time mean I was not working?

No. Idle means the agent saw no keyboard or mouse input for longer than the threshold. Listening on a call, reading on screen and thinking all produce idle time.

Can my employer deduct idle time from my pay?

For nonexempt employees under the FLSA, deducting idle time is risky. Rest breaks of about 5 to 20 minutes must be counted as hours worked (29 CFR 785.18), time spent waiting while on duty is work (785.15), and time is off duty only when the employee is completely relieved and knows it (785.16). State law may add protections. This is general information, not legal advice.

What is a keep or discard idle prompt?

When input resumes after an idle stretch, tools such as Hubstaff ask whether to keep the time, reassign it or discard it. It lets the person who knows what happened decide, but discarding time that was work removes paid hours.

Does Microsoft Teams track idle time?

Teams sets your status to Away after roughly five minutes without input, but it keeps no idle history report. Idle logs come from monitoring agents installed on the device, not from Teams.

How do I stop meetings showing as idle?

Ask whether idle and activity recording can be turned off for meeting or call projects, use the keep option on idle prompts, and log meeting time against the right project. Do not use a mouse jiggler; employers treat simulated activity as falsifying records.
