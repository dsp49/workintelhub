Retro Pay Calculator: Late Raises, Overtime and Withholding

P Pavan
November 12, 2026 · 10 min read

Retro pay is the money an employee is owed because a pay change took effect on one date and payroll applied it on a later one. A raise approved from October 1 and keyed in on November 15 leaves six weeks paid at the old rate; the difference, for every hour or pay period in between, is retroactive pay. It is distinct from back pay, which is wages never paid at all for work performed (unpaid overtime, a minimum wage shortfall, a wrongful dismissal award). Retro pay is a correction; back pay is a remedy.

The calculator below computes the retro amount for hourly and salaried employees, adds the extra overtime premium the Fair Labor Standards Act requires when the affected weeks contained overtime, and shows the federal withholding under the flat supplemental rate. Below it: the formulas, the overtime rule in 29 CFR 778.303 that most payroll teams miss, the tax treatment, state deadlines for paying it, how to show it on the pay stub, and worked examples. For the raise itself, see the pay raise calculator.

Retro pay calculator

For hourly employees the retro amount is the rate difference times regular hours, plus 1.5 times the difference for overtime hours and 2 times for double-time hours (29 CFR 778.303). For salaried employees it is the per-period difference times the number of periods. Federal withholding is shown at the optional 22% flat supplemental rate; Social Security 6.2% and Medicare 1.45% also apply. State tax and benefits deductions are not included. Not tax or legal advice.

The formulas

Hourly employees: retro pay = (new rate − old rate) × regular hours in the affected period + (new rate − old rate) × 1.5 × overtime hours + (new rate − old rate) × 2 × double-time hours. Shift differentials and other premiums calculated as a percentage of the base rate also increase; a 10% night differential on a $2 raise adds $0.20 per differential hour.

Salaried employees: retro pay = (new per-period salary − old per-period salary) × number of pay periods paid at the old rate. Per-period salary is annual salary divided by 52, 26, 24 or 12. If the effective date fell mid-period, prorate that period using the method in the prorated salary calculator.

Percentage raises: convert to a rate first. A 4% raise on $62,400 is $2,496 a year, $96 a biweekly period; four late periods owe $384.

Bonuses and commissions that are calculated as a percentage of base pay should be recomputed for the retro period if the plan ties them to the rate in effect when earned; most plans tie them to the rate at payment and need no adjustment.

Overtime in the retro period

The FLSA regulation on retroactive pay increases, 29 CFR 778.303, is explicit: a retroactive increase "operates to increase the regular rate of pay of the employees for the period of its retroactivity." The example in the regulation is a 10-cent raise, which requires an extra 15 cents for every overtime hour worked in the retro period, "no matter what the agreement of the parties may be." Paying the straight difference on overtime hours underpays them by the half-time premium, and the shortfall is an overtime violation with a two-year limitations period (three if willful) and liquidated damages equal to the unpaid amount.

Two further points from Part 778. Under 778.107 the retroactive overtime is due at the time the increase is paid, not at some later reconciliation. And if the retro amount is paid as a lump sum rather than tied to specific weeks, 778.303 requires it to be apportioned back over the hours of the period it covers so the overtime premium can be computed on each week's regular rate; a lump-sum payment described as a "bonus" does not escape the rule if it is in fact a retroactive increase.

In California and the other daily-overtime states, the same logic applies to daily overtime and double time: the premium on every hour over 8 (1.5x) and over 12 (2x) in the retro period increases with the rate.

How retro pay is taxed

IRS Publication 15 lists retroactive pay increases among supplemental wages. The employer has two options for federal income tax withholding: aggregate the retro payment with the regular wages for the period and withhold on the total under the employee's Form W-4, or, if the amount is identified separately and income tax was withheld from the employee's regular wages in the current or preceding year, withhold at the optional flat rate of 22%. Supplemental wages above $1 million in a calendar year from all businesses under common control are withheld at the top rate of 37%. The 22% and 37% rates were made permanent by the 2025 tax law.

Social Security (6.2% up to the annual wage base) and Medicare (1.45%, plus the 0.9% Additional Medicare Tax withheld above $200,000) apply in full. FUTA and state unemployment taxes apply if the employee is under the wage bases. The payment is reported on the Form W-2 for the year it is paid, not the year it was earned; an employee who receives retro pay in January 2027 for 2026 work has 2027 income. Most states follow the federal supplemental treatment, with their own flat rates.

A flat 22% is withholding, not final tax. Employees in the 12% bracket will get the difference back at filing; employees in the 32% bracket may owe. Say so in the pay stub message or the HR note.

When it must be paid

Federal law requires wages to be paid on the regular payday for the period in which they were earned; retro pay discovered later should be paid on the next regular payday. State law adds deadlines and penalties:

  • California: unpaid wages are due on the regular payday, and an employer that willfully fails to pay all wages due at termination owes waiting-time penalties of a day's wages for each day late, up to 30 days (Labor Code 203). Retro pay owed to a departing employee must be in the final check.
  • Oregon: if an underpayment is less than 5% of the gross wages due for the period, it may be paid on the next regular payday; if 5% or more, within three business days after the employer receives notice of it (ORS 652.120(5)).
  • New York, Massachusetts, Illinois and others require payment within the statutory pay frequency and treat knowing underpayment as a wage theft violation with liquidated damages.
  • Collective agreements commonly set a deadline (often 30 days after ratification) for retro pay from a negotiated increase.

Showing it on the pay stub

Retro pay should be a separate earnings line labeled with the period it covers ("Retro pay 10/01 to 11/14, rate change $20.00 to $22.00") rather than folded into regular earnings, for three reasons: several states (California Labor Code 226 among them) require wage statements to show all applicable rates and the hours at each rate; a separate line lets the employee verify the arithmetic; and it documents that the overtime premium was included. Where the retro period crossed overtime weeks, show the retro overtime as its own line. The pay stub template has a layout.

Common causes and how to prevent them

  • Late approvals: the raise is approved after the payroll cutoff for its effective date. Fix: effective dates on the first day of a pay period, approvals due a week before cutoff.
  • Promotions processed after the start date: the employee begins the new role before HR enters the change. Fix: the personnel action form is a prerequisite for the start date, not a follow-up.
  • Annual merit cycles with a fixed effective date (January 1, the fiscal year) and approvals that run late. Fix: budget the retro into the cycle and communicate it, or set the effective date after approvals.
  • Minimum wage increases missed for some locations. Fix: a calendar of state and local changes; see minimum wage by state 2027.
  • Union contract settlements with retroactive effective dates, often months of retro for hundreds of employees. Fix: a documented procedure that pulls hours by week, including overtime, for the whole retro period.
  • Shift differentials and premiums not updated when the base rate changes. Fix: configure differentials as percentages of base in the payroll system.
  • Keying errors: $22.00 entered as $20.20. Fix: a second-person review of rate changes before the first affected payroll.

Worked examples

Example 1: hourly, with overtime. Rate $20 to $22, effective October 1, processed November 15; six weekly periods paid at the old rate. Regular hours 6 × 40 = 240 × $2 = $480. One week included 5 overtime hours: 5 × $2 × 1.5 = $15. Gross retro $495. Federal withholding at 22% = $108.90; Social Security $30.69; Medicare $7.18; net before state tax $348.23. Paying only $2 × 245 = $490 would have shorted the overtime premium by $5, a small sum that becomes an FLSA violation if it is the practice across the workforce.

Example 2: salaried, biweekly. $60,000 to $66,000 effective January 1, applied on the fourth paycheck; three periods late. Per-period difference $2,538.46 − $2,307.69 = $230.77; × 3 = $692.31. Withholding at 22% = $152.31; FICA $52.96.

Example 3: union settlement. A 3% increase retroactive nine months for 300 employees averaging $25 an hour: $0.75 × roughly 1,560 regular hours = $1,170 each, plus $1.125 per overtime hour worked in the period. The payroll team must pull hours by workweek for the nine months to compute the overtime portion correctly; an estimate using average overtime understates it for the employees who worked the most.

Example 4: a cut, not a raise. An employee's rate is reduced from $24 to $22 and the manager asks payroll to apply it from the start of the month. A retroactive pay reduction is unlawful in every state: a wage cut may apply only to hours worked after the employee has been notified, and several states (New York under Labor Law 195(2), Maryland, North Carolina) require advance written notice. The calculator returns a warning rather than a negative figure.

Key takeaways

  • Retro pay is the difference between what was paid and what should have been paid after a late-applied rate change; back pay is wages never paid for work performed. For hourly employees: rate difference × regular hours, plus 1.5 × the difference for overtime hours; for salaried: per-period difference × periods late.
  • 29 CFR 778.303 requires the overtime premium to be recomputed on the retroactive rate for every overtime hour in the retro period; paying the straight difference on overtime hours is an FLSA violation, and the retro overtime is due when the increase is paid (778.107).
  • Retro pay is supplemental wages: withhold federal income tax at the optional 22% flat rate (37% above $1 million from all commonly controlled employers) or aggregate with regular wages; Social Security and Medicare apply in full; it is W-2 income in the year paid.
  • Pay it on the next regular payday. Oregon requires underpayments of 5% or more to be corrected within three business days of notice; California's waiting-time penalties apply if retro pay is missing from a final check.
  • Show retro pay as its own earnings line with the period and rates covered; states with wage-statement laws require rates and hours to be itemized, and a separate line documents that overtime was included.
  • Most retro pay comes from approvals after payroll cutoff, promotions processed late, merit cycles with fixed effective dates, missed minimum wage changes and union settlements; effective dates on the first day of a period prevent most of it.
  • A retroactive pay cut is unlawful everywhere; reductions apply only to hours worked after notice, with advance written notice required in several states.

Frequently asked questions

What is retro pay?

Retroactive pay is compensation owed because a pay change (a raise, promotion, shift differential or corrected rate) took effect on an earlier date than payroll applied it. The employee was paid, but at the wrong rate, and retro pay is the difference for the affected period. Back pay, by contrast, is wages that were never paid at all for work performed.

How do you calculate retro pay for hourly employees?

Multiply the difference between the new and old rates by the regular hours worked in the retro period, then add the difference times 1.5 for each overtime hour (and times 2 for double-time hours). A $2 raise over 240 regular hours and 5 overtime hours is $480 + $15 = $495.

How do you calculate retro pay for salaried employees?

Divide both the old and new annual salaries by the number of pay periods (26 for biweekly, 24 for semi-monthly) and multiply the difference by the number of periods paid at the old rate. $60,000 to $66,000 biweekly is $230.77 a period; three late periods owe $692.31. Prorate any period in which the effective date fell mid-way.

Does retro pay include overtime?

Yes. Under 29 CFR 778.303 a retroactive raise increases the regular rate for the whole retro period, so every overtime hour in it is owed an extra half of the rate difference on top of the difference itself. Paying only the straight difference on overtime hours is an overtime violation.

How is retro pay taxed?

As supplemental wages. Employers may withhold federal income tax at the flat 22% rate (37% on supplemental wages above $1 million in the year) or aggregate the payment with regular wages and use the W-4. Social Security and Medicare taxes apply in full. The payment is reported on the W-2 for the year it is paid.

Is retro pay taxed at a higher rate?

It is often withheld at a higher rate, 22%, but the tax owed is the same as on any other wages; the flat rate is a withholding convenience. Employees in lower brackets get the excess back at filing, and employees in higher brackets may owe more.

When does an employer have to pay retro pay?

Generally on the next regular payday after the error is found. Oregon requires underpayments of 5% or more of the period's wages to be paid within three business days of notice. California requires all wages, including retro, in a departing employee's final check or waiting-time penalties accrue. Union contracts often set their own deadline after ratification.

Can an employer apply a pay cut retroactively?

No. A wage reduction may apply only to hours worked after the employee has been told, and states such as New York, Maryland and North Carolina require advance written notice of a rate decrease. Work already performed must be paid at the rate in effect when it was performed.
