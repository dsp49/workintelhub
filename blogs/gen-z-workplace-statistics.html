Gen Z in the Workplace Statistics (2026)

P Pavan
October 16, 2026 · 12 min read

Workers aged 16 to 24 were 22.1 million people and 12.9% of the US labor force in 2025, and BLS projects that share to fall to 11.4% by 2035. Gen Z, born from 1997, now runs to about age 28 and makes up roughly a fifth of the labor force, a derived estimate because BLS does not count by generation. Their engagement has fallen 8 points since 2020, only 41% strongly agree someone at work cares about them, down from 54%, and only 23% of those in remote-capable jobs want to be fully remote, the lowest of any generation.

The pages that rank for this question claim Gen Z will be 27% or 30% of the workforce by a date that has passed or that no agency projects, that their average tenure is 1.1 years, and that 74% of managers find them difficult, from a vendor poll. This page uses BLS labor force and tenure tables, Gallup's panel, Deloitte's 22,595-person survey with its global caveat, the Federal Reserve's household survey, Pew, the New York Fed and NACE on graduate hiring, and the meta-analysis on whether generational differences exist at all.

How big Gen Z is in the workforce

Pew Research Center defines Gen Z as born from 1997 through 2012, so aged 13 to 28 in 2025. BLS counts by age, not generation. Its labor force projections put the 16 to 24 group at 22.1 million and 12.9% of the 170.8 million civilian labor force in 2025, falling to 20.1 million and 11.4% in 2035. The 25 to 34 group was 37.9 million and 22.2%. Adding the roughly four tenths of that group aged 25 to 28 gives a Gen Z labor force of about 37 million, or about 22%. That is arithmetic from BLS age bands, not a BLS figure, and it is the only defensible one. Workers aged 55 and over, at 39.2 million, still outnumber those under 25 nearly two to one. Participation among 20 to 24 year olds was 71.4% in 2025 with unemployment at 8.3%, against 62.4% and 4.3% for all workers. The claims that Gen Z will be 27% of the workforce by 2025 or 30% by 2030 come from World Economic Forum commentary with no agency source, and BLS projects the under-35 share to fall, not rise.

What Gen Z wants from a job

Deloitte's 2026 Gen Z and Millennial Survey, from 14,384 Gen Zs and 8,211 millennials in 44 countries surveyed between November 2025 and January 2026, is the largest study and is global, not US. Asked how they think about their career, 44% of Gen Zs chose steady progress and 25% fast-paced growth. Only 6% said a leadership position was their primary career goal, though 76% were interested in senior roles over their career; among those not prioritizing leadership, 50% cited stress and burnout and 41% work-life balance. Ninety-six percent said a sense of purpose was important to job satisfaction, and about 40% had rejected an employer on ethical grounds.

The US data agree on purpose. Gallup's December 2025 survey of 2,436 US Gen Zs aged 13 to 28 for the Walton Family Foundation found 79% wanting a job focused on helping others or making a positive difference, with 56% of employed adult Gen Zs who want such a job saying they have one; insufficient pay at 49% and emotionally draining work at 46% were the barriers. On development, Gallup's 2025 panel of 78,945 US employees found 37% of Gen Z and younger millennials agreeing they have opportunities to learn and grow at work, down from 48% in 2020. Pay, the job market and financial stress

The Federal Reserve's 2025 household survey of 12,934 adults found 63% of those aged 18 to 29 doing okay or living comfortably financially, down 3 points on the year and 4 on 2019, against 73% of all adults and 83% of those 60 and over. Thirty-one percent of 18 to 29 year olds called finding or keeping a job a major concern against 14% of older adults, 49% of adults under 30 lived with a parent, up 12 points since 2019, and 47% had received help from outside their household to pay an expense. Deloitte's global sample found 47% of Gen Zs living paycheck to paycheck, down from 52%, and 69% saying housing costs directly affect their career decisions.

The graduate market is the pinch point. The New York Fed's college labor market tracker put unemployment among graduates aged 22 to 27 at 5.6% and underemployment at 42% in the second quarter of 2026. NACE's spring 2026 update of 185 employers projected hiring of the class of 2026 up 5.6% at the median after a flat autumn forecast, with starting salaries projected at $81,535 in computer science, $81,198 in engineering and $68,873 in business. Handshake's surveys found 57% of the class of 2025 pessimistic about starting their careers in 2024, rising to 61% to 62% for the class of 2026, with nearly half attributing it partly to generative AI. Stress, burnout and mental health

In Deloitte's global data, 48% of Gen Zs reported burnout, 34% felt anxious or stressed most or all of the time, 71% had needed time off for stress but 41% had actually taken it, and 21% of those gave their employer a different reason. In the US, Gallup's 2022 panel of 16,586 workers found 68% of Gen Z and younger millennials feeling stress a lot of the time, and its May 2025 panel of 19,043 found Gen Z with the lowest life evaluations at work and nearly three times as likely as boomers to report loneliness the previous day. EY's 2023 study of about 1,500 US Gen Zs found 42% usually or always anxious or depressed in the past year. Gallup's December 2025 survey found 45% of US Gen Zs lacking a sense of meaning, purpose or both. On AI, a Gallup survey of 1,572 US Gen Zs in early 2026 found 48% saying AI's workplace risks outweigh its benefits, up 11 points, even as 51% used it weekly. Our workplace stress statistics and workplace loneliness statistics pages cover the age gradient.

Remote work and flexibility

The common claim that Gen Z demands remote work runs against Gallup's data. Its May 2025 panel of 19,043 US employees found 23% of remote-capable Gen Z workers preferring fully remote work against 35% in every older generation, and Gen Z the generation most likely to want colleagues to work remotely less often. Asked where they do their best work, 37% of Gen Z said remote, 32% on site and 31% said location does not matter, against 49% remote and 19% on site for millennials. Fully on-site was the least popular option for every age group. In Deloitte's data, 73% of hybrid Gen Zs had a close friend at work against 65% of remote ones, and those with a work friend were 15 points more likely to plan to stay five years or more. The Conference Board's job satisfaction data, covered on our return to office statistics page, found 18 to 24 year olds more satisfied with two or three on-site days than with mostly remote work.

Engagement and managers' views

Gallup's January 2026 analysis of 78,945 US employees found engagement among Gen Z and younger millennials, born from 1989, down 8 points since 2020, older millennials down 9, Gen X down 6 and boomers unchanged. The share strongly agreeing someone at work cares about them fell from 54% to 41%. The last absolute reading, from late 2023, put the youngest group at 35% engaged and 14% actively disengaged, against 36% and 15% for boomers, so the young are less engaged than before but not less engaged than their elders. ADP's 2024 survey of about 38,000 workers in 34 markets found 22% of 18 to 26 year olds in North America strongly agreeing they have the skills to advance, and 45% of workers 26 and under saying changing jobs is difficult.

The manager-side figures that circulate are vendor polls. ResumeBuilder's April 2023 Pollfish poll of 1,000 US managers found 74% saying Gen Z is harder to work with than other generations, and Intelligent.com's August 2024 poll of 966 business leaders found 75% calling some of their recent graduate hires unsatisfactory and 17% hesitant to hire them, which also means 83% were not. Both used income and seniority screeners on opt-in panels and neither is weighted. Our employee engagement statistics page covers the generational series.

Job tenure and quitting

BLS's January 2026 tenure data put median tenure with the current employer at 1.5 years for workers aged 20 to 24 and 3.0 years for those 25 to 34, both up from 1.4 and 2.7 two years earlier, against 4.1 for all workers and 9.6 for those 55 to 64. Short tenure at young ages is a life-stage pattern BLS has recorded for decades, and the "1.1 years" and "1.8 years" figures online have no source. The Federal Reserve's 2025 survey found slightly fewer voluntary quits and job changes than in 2024, when 14% of adults started a new job and 9% quit. In Deloitte's data, 48% of Gen Zs with a close friend at work planned to stay more than five years against 33% without. Our employee turnover statistics page covers the national quits series.

Are generational differences real?

The research says mostly not. Costanza, Badger, Fraser, Severt and Gade's 2012 meta-analysis of 20 studies and 19,961 people in the Journal of Business and Psychology found generational differences in job satisfaction, organisational commitment and intent to leave ranging from zero to small, and concluded that "meaningful differences among generations probably do not exist" on those outcomes. Rudolph, Rauvola, Costanza and Zacher's 2021 review in the same journal called the evidence for generations "at best, scant" and recommended thinking in terms of age and career stage instead. Most of what the data above show, lower pay, shorter tenure, more stress and less engagement among the young, has been true of the young in every decade BLS and Gallup have measured. What is new is the fall since 2020, and that happened to every age group under 60.

Figures to stop using

  • "Gen Z will be 27% of the workforce by 2025" or "30% by 2030." World Economic Forum commentary with no agency source. BLS projects the 16 to 24 share falling from 12.9% to 11.4% by 2035.
  • "Gen Z is 18% of the workforce, per BLS." Derived by the Department of Labor from 2024 data; BLS publishes no generation counts.
  • "Gen Z average tenure is 1.1 years." BLS median tenure is 1.5 years at ages 20 to 24 and 3.0 at 25 to 34, and both rose between 2024 and 2026.
  • "74% of managers find Gen Z difficult." A 2023 vendor poll with income and seniority screeners.
  • "81% of Gen Z see remote flexibility as non-negotiable (Deloitte)." Not in the report. Gallup finds Gen Z the least attached to fully remote work.
  • "52% of Gen Z are burned out (Deloitte or APA)." Deloitte 2026 says 48%, globally.
  • Deloitte figures presented as US. The survey covers 44 countries and defines Gen Z as born 1995 to 2007.
  • Gallup "Gen Z" figures are Gen Z and younger millennials, born from 1989.

Key takeaways

  • Workers aged 16 to 24 are 22.1 million and 12.9% of the US labor force in 2025, projected to fall to 11.4% by 2035. Gen Z as a whole is roughly a fifth, by arithmetic from BLS age bands.
  • Only 6% of Gen Zs globally say leadership is their primary career goal, though 76% are interested in senior roles; 50% of the hesitant cite stress and burnout.
  • 63% of US adults aged 18 to 29 are doing okay financially against 73% of all adults, 49% of under-30s live with a parent and graduate underemployment is 42%.
  • 48% of Gen Zs report burnout and 71% have needed time off for stress but only 41% took it. US Gen Z is nearly three times as likely as boomers to report daily loneliness.
  • Only 23% of remote-capable US Gen Z workers want fully remote work against 35% in every older generation. Fully on-site is the least popular option for every age.
  • Engagement among Gen Z and younger millennials fell 8 points since 2020, and the share who feel someone at work cares about them fell from 54% to 41%.
  • Median tenure is 1.5 years at ages 20 to 24 and 3.0 at 25 to 34, both up since 2024. The '1.1 years' figure has no source.
  • A meta-analysis of 19,961 people found generational differences in satisfaction, commitment and intent to leave ranging from zero to small.

Frequently asked questions

What percentage of the US workforce is Gen Z?

BLS does not count by generation. Workers aged 16 to 24 were 22.1 million or 12.9% of the labor force in 2025, and adding those aged 25 to 28 puts Gen Z, born from 1997, at roughly 37 million or about a fifth. BLS projects the under-25 share to fall to 11.4% by 2035.

What years are Gen Z?

Born from 1997 through 2012, per Pew Research Center's definition, so aged 13 to 28 in 2025. Deloitte's survey uses 1995 to 2007 and Gallup's engagement series groups Gen Z with younger millennials born from 1989, which is why figures differ across sources.

Is Gen Z less engaged at work?

Less than before, not less than older workers. Engagement among Gen Z and younger millennials fell 8 points between 2020 and 2025 in Gallup's panel of 78,945 US employees, and only 41% strongly agree someone at work cares about them, down from 54%. The last absolute reading put them at 35% engaged against 36% for boomers.

Does Gen Z want to work remotely?

Less than any other generation. 23% of remote-capable Gen Z workers preferred fully remote work against 35% in every older generation in Gallup's May 2025 panel of 19,043, and Gen Z was the most likely to want colleagues in the office more. Fully on-site remained the least popular option for every age group.

Is Gen Z job-hopping more than previous generations?

No more than young workers always have. Median tenure was 1.5 years for 20 to 24 year olds and 3.0 years for 25 to 34 year olds in January 2026, per BLS, both slightly higher than two years earlier. Short tenure at young ages is a life-stage pattern recorded for decades.

How financially stressed is Gen Z?

More than older adults. 63% of US adults aged 18 to 29 said they were doing at least okay financially in the Federal Reserve's 2025 survey against 73% overall, 49% of under-30s lived with a parent, and 31% called finding or keeping a job a major concern against 14% of older adults. Graduate underemployment was 42% in mid-2026.

Does Gen Z want to be managers?

Eventually, not urgently. Only 6% of Gen Zs globally say a leadership position is their primary career goal, but 76% are interested in senior roles over their career, per Deloitte's 2026 survey of 22,595. Among those not prioritizing leadership, 50% cite stress and burnout and 41% work-life balance.

Are generational differences at work real?

Mostly not, by the research. A meta-analysis of 20 studies and 19,961 people found generational effects on job satisfaction, commitment and turnover intent were small to zero, and the authors concluded meaningful differences probably do not exist. Most measured gaps track age and career stage rather than birth year.
