PTO Policy Template: Accrual, Carryover, Payout, State Law

P Pavan
November 13, 2026 · 12 min read

A PTO policy has to answer a dozen questions the same way for everyone: how time is earned, when new hires can use it, how much carries into next year, how to request it, when requests can be refused, what happens to unused time when someone leaves, and how it fits with the sick-leave and paid-leave laws that now apply in most of the country. Getting those answers wrong is expensive: in a dozen states unused vacation is wages that must be paid at separation, four states forbid use-it-or-lose-it entirely, and several cities and states require paid leave on their own terms regardless of the company policy. The Bureau of Labor Statistics found 80% of private-industry workers had paid vacation in March 2026.

This template gives the policy text section by section with the decisions you need to make, an accrual calculator, the state rules that override company policy, how PTO interacts with exempt salaries and with FMLA, and a rollout checklist. Related tools: the PTO accrual calculator, PTO rollover and payout by state and the unlimited PTO policy.

Accrual rate calculator

Per-period accrual = annual hours / pay periods. Per-hour accrual = annual hours / scheduled hours. An accrual cap stops earning once the balance reaches the cap, which is lawful in every state, unlike forfeiture of earned time in California, Colorado, Montana and Nebraska.

Ten decisions before you write

  1. Combined PTO bank or separate vacation and sick banks. A combined bank is simpler; in states with paid sick leave laws, it must meet the sick-leave rules for at least the statutory amount, and in states where vacation is wages, the whole bank may become payable at separation.
  2. Amount, by tenure band if you use them.
  3. Accrual or front-loading. Accrual per pay period or per hour worked; front-loading grants the year's time on January 1 or the anniversary.
  4. Waiting period before new hires can use time (often 60 to 90 days; state sick-leave laws limit it, typically to 90 days).
  5. Carryover: none, a cap (for example 40 hours), or an accrual cap instead.
  6. Increments: hours, half days or full days (minimum increments are limited by sick-leave laws, often one hour).
  7. Requests and approval: notice period, approver, how conflicts are resolved.
  8. Blackout periods: whether and when.
  9. Payout at separation: required in some states, optional elsewhere.
  10. Negative balances: allowed up to a limit, or not.

State laws that override the policy

  • Payout at separation required (accrued vacation treated as wages): California, Colorado (Nieto v. Clark's Market, 2021), Illinois (vacation; paid leave under the state's Paid Leave for All Workers Act only if credited to a PTO bank), Iowa (if the policy provides it), Louisiana, Maine (employers with 10 or more, since 2023), Massachusetts, Montana, Nebraska, North Dakota and Rhode Island (after one year). Elsewhere the written policy governs; several states require payout unless the policy clearly says otherwise. See PTO rollover and payout by state.
  • No use-it-or-lose-it: California, Colorado, Montana and Nebraska prohibit forfeiture of earned vacation; reasonable accrual caps are allowed.
  • Paid leave for any reason: Illinois (40 hours a year at 1 hour per 40 worked, since January 1, 2024), Maine (40 hours, employers with 10 or more), Nevada (about 40 hours, employers with 50 or more), and Chicago (from July 1, 2024, 40 hours of paid leave plus 40 hours of paid sick leave, each at 1 hour per 35 worked, with payout of the paid-leave bank required for larger employers).
  • Paid sick leave laws in about 18 states and many cities set minimum accrual, carryover, increments, waiting periods and notice rules for at least part of any PTO bank; see paid sick leave laws by state.
  • Final paycheck deadlines: PTO payout is part of final wages and follows the state deadline; see final paycheck laws.

Exempt employees, partial days and negative balances

Under the FLSA salary-basis rule, an exempt employee's salary cannot be reduced for a partial-day absence, but the Department of Labor has said in opinion letters (FLSA2005-7, FLSA2009-18, FLSA2018-14) that an employer may deduct partial days from a bona fide leave bank, even to a negative balance, as long as the salary itself is paid in full. When the bank is empty, full-day personal absences may be deducted from salary; partial days may not. California's labor agency has limited partial-day deductions from exempt employees' leave banks to absences of at least four hours. Recovering a negative balance from a final paycheck requires written authorization and is restricted or barred in several states, including California. The prorated salary calculator checks which deductions are permitted.

How PTO interacts with sick leave, FMLA and paid family leave

  • FMLA: an employer may require employees to use accrued PTO concurrently with unpaid FMLA leave (29 CFR 825.207), which means FMLA weeks and PTO run together.
  • State paid family leave: rules differ. Some states let the employer require PTO alongside state benefits; others, such as Colorado, Massachusetts, Oregon and Washington, let the employee choose whether to use PTO to top up but prohibit requiring it. See paid family leave by state.
  • Sick and safe leave: a combined PTO bank satisfies a sick-leave law only if it meets every term of the law (accrual, use, notice, documentation, carryover) for at least the statutory amount.
  • Holidays and jury duty are separate; several states bar requiring PTO for jury service (see jury duty pay laws).

Benchmarks

  • 80% of private-industry workers had access to paid vacation, 81% to paid sick leave, 81% to paid holidays, 51% to paid personal leave and 49% to paid family and medical leave in March 2026 (BLS Employee Benefits in the United States, released September 25, 2026). Sick-leave access ranged from 58% in leisure and hospitality to 98% in information and finance.
  • Unlimited PTO: 7% of employers offered it in SHRM's 2024 benefits survey; WTW found 15% offered it to exempt staff in 2025, up from 12% two years earlier, and 27% for executives. See the unlimited PTO guide.
  • Nearly one in four workers with PTO took none in the past year (FlexJobs, August 2025); see unused vacation statistics.

Rollout checklist

  1. Map every work location and apply the strictest state and city rules in addenda.
  2. Configure the accrual, cap, waiting period and carryover in the payroll or time-off system; check the rates with the calculator above.
  3. Decide how existing balances convert if you are changing from separate banks to PTO or from front-loading to accrual; in payout states, earned balances cannot be taken away.
  4. Publish the policy with examples, and get acknowledgments through the handbook acknowledgment.
  5. Train managers on approvals, blackouts and protected leave.
  6. Set up the time-off request form or system workflow.
  7. Review annually, especially for new state and city leave laws (2027 state law changes).

Key takeaways

  • A PTO policy must settle combined or separate banks, amount, accrual or front-loading, waiting period, carryover, increments, requests, blackouts, payout and negative balances, and apply them consistently.
  • Accrual per pay period is annual hours divided by pay periods (15 days on biweekly payroll is 4.615 hours a period); accrual caps are lawful everywhere, but forfeiture of earned vacation is barred in California, Colorado, Montana and Nebraska.
  • About a dozen states treat accrued vacation as wages payable at separation, including California, Colorado, Illinois, Louisiana, Maine, Massachusetts, Montana, Nebraska, North Dakota and Rhode Island.
  • Illinois, Maine, Nevada and Chicago require paid leave for any reason, and about 18 states plus many cities require paid sick leave; a combined PTO bank must meet every term of those laws.
  • Exempt salaries cannot be docked for partial days, but partial days may be charged to the PTO bank, even to a negative balance; recovering negative balances from final pay is restricted in several states.
  • Employers may require PTO to run with FMLA leave, but several state paid family leave programs let only the employee choose to top up with PTO.
  • 80% of private-industry workers had paid vacation in March 2026 (BLS); unlimited PTO is offered by 7 to 15% of employers depending on the survey.

Frequently asked questions

What should a PTO policy include?

Eligibility, the amount of PTO and how it is earned (accrual or front-loaded), any waiting period, permitted uses and increments, how to request time and how conflicts are resolved, blackout periods, carryover and accrual caps, payout at separation, negative balances, treatment of exempt employees, interaction with FMLA and state leave, and state or local addenda.

How is PTO accrual calculated?

Divide the annual PTO hours by the number of pay periods, or by the scheduled hours in a year for per-hour accrual. Fifteen 8-hour days (120 hours) on biweekly payroll accrues 4.615 hours per period, or about 0.0577 hours per hour worked at 2,080 hours a year.

Is use-it-or-lose-it PTO legal?

Not in California, Colorado, Montana or Nebraska, where earned vacation cannot be forfeited. Elsewhere it is generally lawful if the policy clearly states it in advance. Accrual caps, which stop employees earning more once a balance is reached, are lawful everywhere and achieve a similar result.

Do employers have to pay out unused PTO?

In about a dozen states, accrued vacation is wages that must be paid at separation, including California, Colorado, Illinois, Louisiana, Massachusetts, Montana, Nebraska and North Dakota, plus Maine and Rhode Island with conditions. Elsewhere the written policy governs, and some states require payout unless the policy clearly says otherwise.

Can an employer deny a PTO request?

Yes, for vacation and personal time, based on business needs, notice rules and blackout periods applied consistently. Employers cannot deny leave protected by law, such as qualifying sick and safe leave, FMLA leave or state family leave, and should not deny requests in a discriminatory or retaliatory way.

Can exempt employees be charged PTO for partial days?

Yes. The Department of Labor allows partial-day absences to be deducted from an exempt employee's leave bank, even to a negative balance, provided the salary itself is paid in full. Partial days cannot be deducted from salary. California limits partial-day leave-bank deductions to absences of at least four hours.

What is a typical PTO waiting period?

Sixty to ninety days for new hires is common. State and local sick-leave laws often require that statutory sick time be usable after no more than 90 days, so a PTO bank that doubles as sick leave must follow that rule for at least the statutory amount.

Should PTO be combined or separate from sick leave?

A combined bank is simpler and gives employees flexibility, but in sick-leave states it must meet every requirement of the law, and in payout states the whole bank may become payable at separation. Separate banks let employers pay out vacation without paying out sick time, which most states do not require.
