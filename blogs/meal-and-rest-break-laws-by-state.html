Meal and Rest Break Laws by State (2026): Every Rule and Penalty

P Pavan
October 28, 2026 · 15 min read

Federal law requires no meal or rest break for adult employees. Twenty-one states and the District of Columbia's neighbors do, and nine of them also mandate paid rest breaks. The rules differ on four things: how long a shift must be before a break is owed, how long the break must be, when in the shift it must fall, and what a missed break costs. California owes an extra hour of pay for each missed meal or rest period; Minnesota, since January 1, 2026, owes the missed time plus an equal amount as liquidated damages; most other states set no specific remedy and rely on their labor department.

This page gives the rule for every state that has one, with the statute, the trigger, the timing, the waiver conditions and the penalty, from the statutes and labor department pages read in October 2026 rather than the Department of Labor's summary table, which was last revised in January 2023 and misses Minnesota's change and Colorado's 2026 order. It also covers the two federal rules that do apply everywhere, paid short breaks and unpaid bona fide meal periods, and the automatic meal deduction practice that has produced the largest settlements.

The federal rules that apply everywhere

The Fair Labor Standards Act does not require a lunch break or a coffee break, and the Department of Labor says so in those words. What it does regulate is pay for the breaks an employer chooses to give. Under 29 CFR 785.18, short rest periods, which the regulation describes as running from five minutes to about twenty, are hours worked and must be paid, and an employer cannot offset them against other working time. Under 29 CFR 785.19, a bona fide meal period is not work time and may be unpaid, but only if the employee is completely relieved from duty for the purpose of eating a regular meal; ordinarily thirty minutes or more is long enough, and an employee who eats at a desk while answering the phone is working. The employee need not be allowed to leave the premises if otherwise free of duties. A Department of Labor opinion letter dated May 28, 2026 confirmed that a thirty-minute unpaid meal period stays non-compensable even where leaving a large secured site leaves only ten to fifteen minutes to eat, because the test is relief from duty, not convenience, while noting that state law may be stricter.

One federal break right does exist. The PUMP for Nursing Mothers Act, signed December 29, 2022, requires reasonable break time to express milk each time the employee needs to for one year after a child's birth, in a space that is not a bathroom and is shielded from view. The breaks are unpaid unless the employee is working or the employer pays other breaks, employers with fewer than 50 employees can claim undue hardship, and full remedies including liquidated damages have applied since April 28, 2023.

Only California and Minnesota attach a specific money remedy to a missed rest break. Colorado, Oregon and Washington owe the unpaid time, and in Washington the state Supreme Court has held that missed paid rest counts as hours worked for overtime. Pages that describe a California-style one-hour premium in those three states are wrong.

New York's noon-day rule

New York Labor Law 162 is the most misdescribed break statute in the country, so here it is in full. Factory workers get at least 60 minutes for the noon-day meal; employees in mercantile and other establishments get at least 30. Noon-day means 11 a.m. to 2 p.m., and a shift of more than six hours that spans that window must include the break inside it. A shift that starts before 11 a.m. and ends after 7 p.m. earns an additional 20 minutes between 5 p.m. and 7 p.m. A shift of more than six hours that starts between 1 p.m. and 6 a.m. earns a mid-shift meal of 60 minutes in a factory and 45 minutes elsewhere. Shorter periods are lawful only under a written permit from the Commissioner of Labor, posted at the main entrance and revocable at any time. The section sets no penalty; enforcement runs through the Department of Labor.

States with rules for minors only

The Department of Labor counts 35 jurisdictions with separate meal rules for minors, including states with no adult rule at all. Pennsylvania, Wisconsin, New Jersey and Ohio are the most cited, each requiring roughly 30 minutes after five to six hours for workers under 18, and Washington revised its minor break rules effective July 1, 2026. If you employ anyone under 18, check the state's child labor page directly; the adult table above does not cover them and the rules change more often.

Automatic meal deductions

An automatic 30-minute deduction from every shift is lawful only to the extent the employee was actually relieved for 30 minutes. Where nurses, technicians or warehouse staff routinely work through lunch and nobody cancels the deduction, the employer has unpaid hours worked under 29 CFR 785.19 and, in the states above, an unpaid state break premium on top. The litigation record is long: a $3.5 million settlement for about 1,400 nurses at SSM Health Care of Wisconsin in 2013, up to $4.25 million at University Medical Center of Southern Nevada in 2019, and collective actions certified against hospital systems in Ohio and Alabama. Courts have protected employers that gave employees a real way to cancel the deduction and trained managers to use it, and certified classes against employers whose override was rarely used. The practical rule is an override every employee knows about, an attestation at clock-out that the break was taken, and a manager report of missed breaks each pay period.

Tracking breaks so the record holds up

29 CFR 516.2 requires a record of hours worked each day and week; it does not require break punches, but a break punch is the only evidence that a 30-minute deduction was real. In California the burden shifts to the employer where records are missing, so a missing meal punch is treated as a missed meal. Time clock apps handle this differently: some enforce a minimum break length before allowing a clock-in, some only record it, and some auto-deduct silently. What the data says about breaks

  • Claims volume. California's Labor and Workforce Development Agency received 8,846 notices under the Private Attorneys General Act in fiscal 2024-25, and calendar 2025 filings approached 9,900 according to the Duane Morris class action review, which also puts the ten largest wage-and-hour settlements of 2025 at $430.6 million. Meal and rest claims are a standard component of California wage class actions; the state publishes no break-specific count.
  • Skipped lunches. Surveys here are brand-commissioned and should be read as such. A Talker Research poll of 2,000 employed Americans for a food brand in 2025 found 55% skip lunch on hectic days and one in three eat at their desks; ezCater's 2025 lunch report of 1,000 workers found 35% take a lunch break away from the desk every day. The Tork "Take Back the Lunch Break" release often cited for desk-lunch figures published no sample or method.
  • Breaks and attention. Ariga and Lleras's 2011 experiment in Cognition found that participants given two brief breaks during a roughly one-hour vigilance task showed no decline in performance, where those without breaks declined. DeskTime's 2014 analysis of its most productive users produced the widely quoted 52 minutes of work to 17 minutes of break; its 2021 rerun found 112 and 26. Both are observational data from one vendor's users, not peer-reviewed workplace studies, and our workplace distraction statistics page puts them in context.

Key takeaways

  • Federal law requires no meal or rest break. It requires that breaks of about 5 to 20 minutes be paid (29 CFR 785.18) and lets a meal period of about 30 minutes be unpaid only if the employee is completely relieved (29 CFR 785.19).
  • Twenty-one states mandate meal breaks for adults; nine mandate paid rest breaks. California, Colorado, Kentucky, Minnesota, Nevada, Oregon and Washington do both.
  • California owes one hour of pay per workday for each missed meal or rest period. Minnesota, since January 1, 2026, owes the missed time plus an equal amount as liquidated damages. No other state has a comparable fixed premium.
  • Minnesota's 2026 change moved the meal trigger from 8 to 6 hours and added a 15-minute paid rest per 4 hours; Colorado's COMPS Order 40 took effect February 1, 2026. The Department of Labor's state table predates both.
  • New York's rule is a 30-minute (60 in factories) break inside 11 a.m. to 2 p.m. for shifts over 6 hours, with extra breaks for long and night shifts.
  • Automatic meal deductions are lawful only when the break was actually taken; a working override and a clock-out attestation are what have protected employers in court.
  • The PUMP Act gives nursing employees federal break rights for a year after birth, with undue-hardship relief for employers under 50 staff.

Frequently asked questions

Does federal law require lunch breaks?

No. The Department of Labor states that federal law does not require lunch or coffee breaks. It regulates pay for breaks that are given: short breaks of about 5 to 20 minutes must be paid, and a meal period of about 30 minutes may be unpaid only if the employee is completely relieved of duty.

Which states require meal breaks?

California, Colorado, Connecticut, Delaware, Illinois, Kentucky, Maine, Maryland (retail), Massachusetts, Minnesota, Nebraska (manufacturing), Nevada, New Hampshire, New York, North Dakota, Oregon, Rhode Island, Tennessee, Vermont, Washington and West Virginia. The trigger ranges from 5 hours in California and Washington to 8 in Nevada.

Which states require paid rest breaks?

California, Colorado, Kentucky, Minnesota, Nevada, Oregon and Washington require roughly 10 to 15 paid minutes per 4 hours worked. Vermont requires reasonable opportunities to eat and use the toilet, and Illinois requires paid rest breaks for hotel room attendants in large counties.

What happens if an employer misses a meal break in California?

The employer owes one additional hour of pay at the employee's regular rate for each workday a meal period is missed, and another hour for each day a rest period is missed, under Labor Code 226.7. The premium is a wage with a three-year limitations period and is the basis of many Private Attorneys General Act claims.

What changed in Minnesota's break law in 2026?

Effective January 1, 2026, Minnesota requires a 30-minute meal break after 6 consecutive hours instead of 8, and a paid rest break of at least 15 minutes within each 4 consecutive hours. A missed break is owed at the regular rate plus an equal amount as liquidated damages, enforceable by the Department of Labor and Industry or a private suit.

Can an employer automatically deduct 30 minutes for lunch?

Only if the employee actually took a duty-free 30-minute break. Where employees work through lunch and the deduction is not reversed, the employer owes unpaid wages under federal law and any state break premium. Employers that provide a working override, train managers and require a clock-out attestation have fared best in litigation.

Can an employee waive a meal break?

It depends on the state. California allows the first meal to be waived by mutual consent on days of 6 hours or less, and the second on days of 12 or less. Oregon and Tennessee allow tipped food-service workers to waive in writing. Minnesota lets employees voluntarily skip and recommends written confirmation. Several states, including Colorado, generally do not allow waiver.

Are employers required to give breaks to nursing mothers?

Yes. The federal PUMP for Nursing Mothers Act requires reasonable break time to express milk for one year after a child's birth, in a private space that is not a bathroom. The breaks are unpaid unless the employee is working or other breaks are paid; employers with fewer than 50 employees may claim undue hardship.
