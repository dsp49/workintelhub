Jury Duty Pay Laws by State: Do Employers Have to Pay?

P Pavan
November 13, 2026 · 11 min read

Every state and the federal courts forbid firing or punishing an employee for serving on a jury. Far fewer require the employer to keep paying them. Federal law requires no pay at all for private employers. Nine states and the District of Columbia do require pay for at least some days, from one day in Louisiana to as long as the service lasts in Alabama, Nebraska and Tennessee, and a separate group of about fifteen states forbid employers from making employees use vacation or sick time for jury service. For exempt salaried staff, the Fair Labor Standards Act adds its own rule: a week's salary cannot be docked for jury duty if the employee did any work that week.

This guide covers the federal rules, the states that require pay with the amount and duration, the PTO and night-shift protections, a 51-jurisdiction table, juror fees, how to handle exempt and non-exempt employees, and a policy you can adopt. Federal rules

The Jury System Improvements Act (28 U.S.C. 1875) prohibits any employer from discharging, threatening or coercing a permanent employee because of service on a federal jury. Remedies include lost wages and benefits, reinstatement, a civil penalty of up to $5,000 per violation for each employee, and attorney's fees; the court may appoint counsel for the employee. The Act requires reinstatement but does not require pay. Federal jurors receive $50 a day from the court under 28 U.S.C. 1871, which a judge may raise by up to $10 a day after 10 days on a trial jury (45 days on a grand jury). State-court jurors are protected by state statutes with the same structure: every state bars retaliation, and many make it a misdemeanor or contempt of court.

States that require pay

  • Alabama (Code 12-16-8): full-time employees receive their usual compensation for the time served, and may not be required to use annual, vacation, sick or unpaid leave. Where the employer has five or fewer full-time employees and one is already serving, the court postpones another's service.
  • Colorado (13-71-126): regular wages up to $50 a day for the first three days, for full-time, part-time, temporary and casual employees with a regular schedule. The state pays $50 a day from day four.
  • Connecticut (51-247): full-time employees receive regular wages for the first five days. The state pays $50 a day from day six.
  • District of Columbia (Code 15-718): employers with more than 10 employees pay full-time employees their usual compensation, less the $30 juror fee, for service of five days or less.
  • Louisiana (R.S. 23:965): one day of leave without loss of wages or leave; fines of $100 to $500 per violation.
  • Massachusetts (ch. 234A, s. 48): regular wages for the first three days, including part-time and temporary employees. The state pays $50 a day thereafter.
  • Nebraska (25-1640): the employee's normal wages less the juror fee, and no forced use of leave.
  • New York (Judiciary Law 519): employers with more than 10 employees may not withhold the first $72 of daily wages for the first three days. The figure was raised from $40 to $72 effective June 8, 2025, matching the state juror fee.
  • Tennessee (22-4-106): employers with five or more employees pay usual compensation, less the juror fee, to employees with six months' service; night-shift employees are excused from the shift around service.
  • Georgia has no express statute, but a 1989 Attorney General opinion is widely read to require employers to pay employees for jury service, and most Georgia employers do.

Florida has no state requirement; Broward and Miami-Dade counties have ordinances. In every other state, an employer may lawfully treat jury service as unpaid leave for non-exempt employees, subject to the PTO and exempt-salary rules below. Many employers pay anyway; surveys of benefit policies consistently find most mid-size and large employers pay full salary for at least a week or two.

PTO, night shifts and other protections

About fifteen to nineteen states, depending on whose count, prohibit employers from requiring employees to use vacation, sick or other paid leave for jury service; Alabama, Ohio (2313.19) and Virginia (18.2-465.1) are confirmed by statute, and lists compiled by employment law publishers add Arizona, Arkansas, Indiana, Louisiana, Maryland, Massachusetts, Mississippi, Missouri, Nebraska, Nevada, New Mexico, New York, Oklahoma, Oregon, Pennsylvania and Utah. The employee may choose to use PTO to be paid; the employer may not insist. Virginia also forbids requiring an employee who served four or more hours to work a shift starting between 5 p.m. and 3 a.m. the same day, and Nevada and Tennessee have similar night-shift rules. Several states extend protection to employees who serve as witnesses or grand jurors, to the time needed to respond to a summons, and to benefits and seniority, which must continue as if the employee had been at work.

Exempt and non-exempt employees

Exempt salaried employees. Under 29 CFR 541.602(b)(3), an employer may not reduce an exempt employee's salary for absences caused by jury duty, attendance as a witness or temporary military leave in any week in which the employee performs any work. The employer may offset the juror fees the employee receives against the salary for that week. If the employee serves a full week and does no work at all, the week may be unpaid without losing the exemption, though few employers do this. Docking a partial week for jury service risks the exemption under the salary-basis test; the prorated salary calculator flags it.

Non-exempt employees. The FLSA requires pay only for hours worked, so in the states without a pay mandate, jury hours may be unpaid. Hours on jury service are not hours worked and do not count toward overtime. Where an employee returns to work after a half-day of service, the hours worked that day are paid normally. State pay mandates apply to non-exempt employees on their regular schedule.

Juror fees and offsets

Juror fees are modest: $50 a day in federal court; $72 a day in New York state courts since June 2025; $50 a day after the employer-paid days in Colorado, Connecticut and Massachusetts; $30 in the District of Columbia; and between about $5 and $50 a day in most state courts, often lower on the first day. Where state law requires pay "less the juror fee," the employer may deduct the fee; where it requires "regular wages," it generally may not. A common voluntary policy is to pay full salary and let the employee keep the fee, which avoids collecting receipts; the alternative is to require employees to remit the fee or show proof of it. Both are lawful if written down.

Employers may ask the court to excuse or postpone an employee's service for business hardship, but only through the employee and the court's process; pressuring an employee to seek an excuse is itself a violation in most states.

If you are summoned

Give your employer the summons promptly; most state protections assume notice. Check the table for whether your state requires pay and whether your employer may make you use PTO. If you are exempt and did any work that week, your salary for the week should not be reduced. Keep your juror fee receipts in case your employer offsets them. If you are disciplined or threatened for serving, tell the court clerk; judges treat interference with jurors as contempt, and federal and state laws give you a claim for lost wages, reinstatement and penalties. If serving would cause real hardship (you are the only person covering a shift, or you would lose wages you cannot afford), ask the court for a postponement; courts grant them routinely.

Key takeaways

  • Every state and federal law prohibits firing or threatening an employee for jury service; federal law (28 U.S.C. 1875) adds lost wages, reinstatement and penalties up to $5,000 per violation, but no pay requirement.
  • States that require pay: Alabama (usual compensation), Colorado (up to $50 a day for 3 days), Connecticut (regular wages for 5 days), DC (5 days, employers with 10+), Louisiana (1 day), Massachusetts (3 days), Nebraska (normal wages less fee), New York (first $72 a day for 3 days, employers with 10+), Tennessee (usual compensation less fee); Georgia by Attorney General opinion.
  • About fifteen states prohibit requiring employees to use PTO for jury service, including Alabama, Ohio and Virginia by statute; Virginia, Nevada and Tennessee protect employees from night shifts on jury days.
  • Exempt employees must receive full salary for any week in which they did any work; juror fees may be offset (29 CFR 541.602(b)(3)). Non-exempt employees may be unpaid for jury hours where no state mandate applies.
  • Juror fees are $50 a day federally, $72 in New York since June 2025, and between about $5 and $50 in most state courts; 'less the juror fee' statutes allow an offset, 'regular wages' statutes generally do not.
  • A good policy grants leave, sets the paid days, protects exempt salaries, sets a return-to-work rule, bars forced PTO, continues benefits and states non-retaliation.

Frequently asked questions

Do employers have to pay employees for jury duty?

Not under federal law. Alabama, Colorado, Connecticut, the District of Columbia, Louisiana, Massachusetts, Nebraska, New York and Tennessee require at least some pay, from one day in Louisiana to the full period in Alabama, Nebraska and Tennessee; Georgia is widely read to require it. Exempt employees must receive their full salary for any week in which they do any work.

Can I be fired for jury duty?

No. Federal law protects employees serving on federal juries and every state protects employees serving on state juries. An employer that fires, threatens or penalizes an employee for jury service can be ordered to reinstate them and pay lost wages, and may face fines and contempt of court.

Can my employer make me use vacation days for jury duty?

Not in about fifteen states, including Alabama, Ohio and Virginia by statute, and states such as Arizona, Indiana, Maryland, Massachusetts, Nevada, New York, Oregon and Pennsylvania on most published lists. Elsewhere, an employer may require PTO for jury days if its policy says so, although the employee usually may choose to use PTO to be paid.

Do salaried employees get paid for jury duty?

Exempt salaried employees must be paid their full salary for any week in which they perform any work, under the FLSA salary-basis rule; the employer may offset juror fees received. Only a full week of service with no work at all may be unpaid. Non-exempt salaried employees are treated like hourly employees unless state law requires pay.

How much does jury duty pay?

Federal courts pay $50 a day, rising by up to $10 after 10 days. New York pays $72 a day. Colorado, Connecticut and Massachusetts pay $50 a day after the days the employer must cover. Most other state courts pay between about $5 and $50 a day, sometimes less on the first day.

Can my employer deduct the jury fee from my pay?

Where state law requires pay 'less the juror fee' (DC, Nebraska, Tennessee) or the employer's policy pays full wages less fees, yes. For exempt employees, the FLSA allows offsetting juror fees against salary. Where the statute requires regular wages without mentioning the fee, an offset is risky.

Do I have to go back to work after jury duty?

Federal law does not say. Many employers require employees released early to return if a substantial part of the shift remains. Virginia prohibits requiring an employee who served four or more hours to work a shift starting between 5 p.m. and 3 a.m., and Nevada and Tennessee have similar night-shift protections.

Does jury duty count toward overtime?

No. Jury service hours are not hours worked under the FLSA, so they do not count toward 40 hours for overtime, even if the employer pays for them, unless a contract or policy says paid leave counts.
