Child Labor Laws by State (2026): Hours, Permits, Changes

P Pavan
November 13, 2026 · 13 min read

Federal child labor law sets one detailed schedule and leaves the rest to the states. Fourteen and fifteen year-olds may work only 3 hours on a school day, 18 hours in a school week and not after 7 p.m. for most of the year; sixteen and seventeen year-olds have no federal hour limits at all, only a list of hazardous jobs they may not do. States fill that gap very differently. New York, California, Washington and Massachusetts cap teenage hours tightly; Alabama, Minnesota and Ohio regulate only late nights; about half the states add nothing. Between 2023 and 2026 more than a dozen states rewrote their laws, some loosening permits and hours, others raising penalties after a wave of federal enforcement cases.

This guide gives the federal rules, a 51-jurisdiction table of the limits for 16 and 17 year-olds and the major recent changes, the federal penalties for 2026, how work permits are changing, the hazardous-occupation rules, and what employers that hire teenagers should build into scheduling and timekeeping. For the adult rules, see mandatory overtime laws by state and meal and rest break laws.

Federal rules

The Fair Labor Standards Act and the regulations at 29 CFR Part 570 set the federal floor for non-agricultural work:

A "school week" is any week in which school is in session for any part of the week. Work-experience and career exploration programs and some apprenticeship programs allow 14 and 15 year-olds to work during school hours and up to 23 hours in a school week. Agricultural employment has separate, looser rules. Where state law is stricter, the stricter rule applies; where it is looser (Iowa allows 14 and 15 year-olds to work until 9 p.m. during the school year), federal law still binds any employer covered by the FLSA, which is almost every employer with $500,000 in annual sales or engaged in interstate commerce.

Hazardous occupations

Seventeen Hazardous Occupations Orders bar anyone under 18 from jobs the Secretary of Labor has declared particularly dangerous, including driving a motor vehicle on public roads as a regular part of the job (with narrow exceptions for 17 year-olds), operating forklifts, meat slicers and bakery mixers, power-driven woodworking and metal-forming machines, roofing, excavation, demolition, logging, mining and work with explosives or radioactive materials. The most frequent violations found in restaurants and grocery stores are minors operating meat slicers, dough mixers, balers and compactors; in meatpacking and manufacturing, minors cleaning machinery on overnight sanitation shifts. Fourteen and fifteen year-olds face a longer list, including any baking, most cooking, ladders and scaffolds, and loading or unloading trucks.

Youth wages

The FLSA allows a youth minimum wage of $4.25 an hour for workers under 20 during their first 90 consecutive calendar days with an employer, provided no adult is displaced to hire them. Most states with their own minimum wage either do not allow a youth wage or set a higher one; Nebraska's LB 258, effective July 17, 2026, created a $13.50 youth rate for 14 and 15 year-olds and a 90-day training wage for 16 to 19 year-olds below the adult minimum. Overtime rules apply to minors exactly as to adults. The minimum wage by state table covers state youth rates.

Where states are stricter for under-16s

Most states copy the federal rules for 14 and 15 year-olds; a few are stricter. Florida limits them to 15 hours in a school week, Maryland to 4 hours on a school day and 23 in a school week, and Washington to 3 hours on a school day and 16 in a school week. Nevada cut its under-16 weekly cap from 48 to 40 hours from October 1, 2025. A few states allow more than federal law does (Iowa permits 6 hours a day and 28 a week in school weeks and work until 9 p.m., later in summer; Idaho, Nebraska and Arkansas allow 48 to 54-hour weeks), but those provisions apply only to employers outside FLSA coverage, which in practice means very few.

Work permits and age certificates

Federal law does not require work permits; it recognizes state-issued age certificates as proof that an employer acted in good faith. About half the states require an employment certificate or work permit for some minors, usually under 16 and sometimes under 18, issued by the school or the state labor department. The direction since 2023 has been toward fewer permits and more electronic ones: Arkansas (2023) and Iowa (2023) eliminated permits; West Virginia (2025) replaced them for 14 and 15 year-olds with parental consent and an age certificate; Indiana repealed its employer registration requirement on July 1, 2026; Michigan moved permits from schools to the state from October 2, 2026; New York will issue working papers electronically from May 9, 2027; Illinois requires an employment certificate for every worker under 16 from January 1, 2025. Whether or not the state requires a permit, an employer should keep proof of age for every worker under 18, because the burden is on the employer to show it did not employ an underage worker.

What changed in 2023 to 2026

The Economic Policy Institute counted 13 states that introduced bills to weaken child labor protections in the 2026 sessions and four that enacted them, against three that introduced strengthening bills and one that passed. The loosening laws extended evening hours for 14 and 15 year-olds (Ohio, Indiana), removed hour caps for 16 and 17 year-olds (Florida, Indiana), eliminated permits (Arkansas, Iowa, West Virginia) and allowed limited hazardous work in training programs (Iowa, West Virginia). Kentucky's 2024 bill to defer to federal rules passed the House and died in the Senate. The tightening laws responded to the 2023 enforcement cases, in which a sanitation contractor was found to have employed more than 100 children on overnight shifts in meatpacking plants and paid $1.5 million in penalties: Illinois raised penalties to $60,000 per violation; New York raised them to $10,000 for a first violation and up to $175,000 for a repeat violation causing injury; Nevada tightened hours; Colorado and Oregon wrote new rules; Washington tiered its penalties. Federal law did not change.

Penalties

Federal civil money penalties, adjusted for inflation, for violations assessed after January 16, 2026 (unchanged from 2025 because the 2026 adjustment was frozen):

  • $16,035 for each employee who was the subject of a child labor violation;
  • $72,876 for each violation that caused the death or serious injury of a minor;
  • $145,752 when that violation is repeated or willful.

Willful violations are also federal crimes, and goods produced in violation can be embargoed from interstate shipment under the FLSA's "hot goods" provision, which is how the Department of Labor has pressured meatpacking and auto-parts suppliers. State penalties add to these, now reaching $60,000 per violation in Illinois and $175,000 in New York for the worst cases.

For employers: scheduling minors safely

  1. Record date of birth for every hire under 18 and keep proof of age; flag minors in the HRIS and the scheduling tool.
  2. Build the limits into the scheduler, not the manager's memory: daily and weekly hour caps, school-week rules, curfews and the gap between shifts, per state. Most scheduling systems can block a shift that breaks a configured rule; see the shift scheduling software comparison.
  3. Lock the clock. Configure timekeeping so a minor cannot clock in before the earliest permitted time or after the curfew, and alert when weekly hours approach the cap. Late clock-outs at closing are the most common hour violation.
  4. Tag equipment and tasks: no meat slicers, mixers, balers, compactors, forklifts or delivery driving for anyone under 18; post the rule by the machines.
  5. Track the school calendar. School-week limits apply whenever school is in session for any day of the week, including the first week of term.
  6. Get the permit where required, before the first shift.
  7. Audit quarterly: pull every minor's punches against the limits. The time tracking discrepancy template works for this.
  8. Check contractors and staffing agencies. The 2023 cases involved sanitation contractors; the host employer's name was on the press release.

Key takeaways

  • Federal law limits 14 and 15 year-olds to 3 hours on a school day, 18 in a school week, 8 on a non-school day and 40 in a non-school week, between 7 a.m. and 7 p.m. (9 p.m. from June 1 to Labor Day); it sets no hour limits for 16 and 17 year-olds.
  • Seventeen Hazardous Occupations Orders bar under-18s from jobs such as driving on public roads, forklifts, meat slicers, bakery mixers, balers, roofing and demolition; 14 and 15 year-olds face a longer list including most cooking and baking.
  • About half the states add limits for 16 and 17 year-olds, from tight caps (New York, California, Washington, Massachusetts, Pennsylvania) to curfews only (Alabama, Minnesota, Ohio, North Carolina); the rest follow federal law.
  • From 2023 to 2026 Arkansas, Iowa, Florida, Indiana, West Virginia and Ohio loosened permits or hours, while Illinois, New York, Nevada, Colorado, Oregon and Washington tightened rules or raised penalties.
  • 2026 federal penalties: $16,035 per minor affected, $72,876 for a violation causing death or serious injury, $145,752 if repeated or willful; Illinois penalties reach $60,000 and New York's $175,000.
  • The youth minimum wage of $4.25 applies only for the first 90 days and only under 20; Nebraska created a $13.50 youth wage for 14 and 15 year-olds from July 2026.
  • Employers should record dates of birth, configure scheduling and timekeeping to block prohibited hours, tag hazardous equipment, follow the school calendar and audit minors' punches quarterly.

Frequently asked questions

How many hours can a 16 year-old work?

Federal law sets no limit for 16 and 17 year-olds. State law may: New York limits students to 4 hours on school days and 28 a week in school weeks, California to 4 hours on a school day and 48 a week, Washington to 4 hours on a school day and 20 a week, and Pennsylvania to 28 hours in a school week. About half the states set no hour limit for 16 year-olds.

How many hours can a 15 year-old work?

Under federal law, 3 hours on a school day, 18 hours in a school week, 8 hours on a non-school day and 40 hours in a non-school week, between 7 a.m. and 7 p.m. (9 p.m. from June 1 through Labor Day). Some states are stricter: Florida caps school weeks at 15 hours and Maryland at 23 with 4 hours a day.

What time can a 16 year-old work until?

There is no federal curfew for 16 and 17 year-olds. State curfews before a school day include 10 p.m. in California, New York, Washington and Massachusetts, 10:30 p.m. in Michigan, 11 p.m. in Florida, Kentucky, Minnesota, New Jersey, North Carolina and Ohio, and midnight in Pennsylvania.

Do minors need a work permit?

It depends on the state. About half require an employment certificate or work permit for some minors, usually under 16. Arkansas and Iowa eliminated permits in 2023 and West Virginia replaced them for 14 and 15 year-olds in 2025; Illinois requires certificates for all under-16s from 2025. Federal law does not require permits but accepts state age certificates as proof of age.

What jobs can a 14 year-old do?

Retail cashiering and stocking, office work, bagging, some food service such as serving and cleaning (not most cooking or baking), lifeguarding at 15, and similar non-hazardous work, within the 14 and 15 year-old hour limits. They cannot work in manufacturing, mining, construction, warehouses, with power-driven machinery, on ladders or in the hazardous occupations.

What is the penalty for violating child labor laws?

Federal civil penalties in 2026 are up to $16,035 per minor affected, $72,876 for a violation causing death or serious injury and $145,752 if that violation is repeated or willful, plus possible criminal prosecution and embargo of goods. State penalties are added; Illinois allows up to $60,000 per violation and New York up to $175,000 for repeat violations causing injury.

Can minors work overtime?

Minors 16 and older can work more than 40 hours a week where state law allows it, and must receive time and a half for hours over 40 like any other non-exempt employee. Fourteen and fifteen year-olds cannot exceed 40 hours in any week under federal law, and 18 in a school week.

Which states loosened child labor laws recently?

Arkansas (2023, ended permits), Iowa (2023, longer hours and fewer permits), Florida (2024, longer hours for 16 and 17 year-olds), Indiana (2025 and 2026, adult hours for 16 and 17 year-olds and ended registration), West Virginia (2025 and 2026) and Ohio (2026, later hours for 14 and 15 year-olds). Federal limits still apply to employers covered by the FLSA.
