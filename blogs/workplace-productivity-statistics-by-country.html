Workplace Productivity Statistics by Country

P Pavan
August 21, 2026 · 8 min read

Workplace productivity by country is measured as GDP per hour worked: everything an economy produced in a year, divided by every hour its workers spent producing it, converted into a common currency so the numbers can sit next to each other. It is the only cross-country productivity measure with a real methodology behind it, and it is not a measure of effort. A worker in a country near the top is not trying harder than one near the bottom. They are standing next to more capital, better infrastructure, and a more valuable industry mix.

The figures on this page come from Penn World Table 11.0, released through Our World in Data, covering 128 countries for 2023 in international dollars at 2021 prices. We are naming the source and the edition because the numbers you find elsewhere disagree with each other by more than the gap between many neighbouring countries, and almost nobody says which dataset they used.

What this actually measures

GDP per hour worked answers one question: how much economic value does an hour of work in this country produce. It says nothing about how hard anyone works, how long the working week is, or whether the work is worth doing. A country can raise the number by shedding low productivity jobs entirely, which looks like progress in the statistic and like unemployment on the ground.

Two adjustments make cross-country comparison possible at all, and both introduce error. Purchasing power parity converts currencies by what money actually buys locally rather than by the exchange rate. Hours worked come from national labour surveys that define a working hour slightly differently in each country. Neither is a rounding error, and together they explain most of the disagreement between published tables.

The median country in the dataset produces $26.80 an hour, almost exactly Mexico's figure. The distance from top to bottom is a factor of 96: Ireland at $158.20 against Madagascar at $1.70. Nothing about workplace culture explains a gap that size. Capital, infrastructure, and which industries a country happens to host explain nearly all of it.

Why Ireland is at the top, and why that is misleading

Ireland leads every table of this kind, and the reason has little to do with Irish workplaces. A number of multinationals book intellectual property and profits through Irish subsidiaries. That output lands in Irish GDP while the hours that produced it were largely worked elsewhere, which inflates output per hour for everyone counted in the denominator.

Ireland's own statistics office publishes a modified measure, GNI*, specifically to strip out those distortions, and it puts Irish productivity far closer to its western European neighbours than the headline suggests. If you are using this table to think about your own workplace, treat the top of it as an accounting artefact rather than a target.

This is the single most useful thing to know about country productivity tables, and it is missing from almost every version of this article published elsewhere.

Why every version of this table disagrees

Search for productivity by country and you will find figures that conflict badly. For the United States in the same year we found $97.05, $83.62, and $83.50 across three sources. For Ireland, $132.50, $136, $149.31, and $158.20. These are not typos. They come from different datasets, different price base years, different PPP conversions, and different definitions of an hour worked.

Three things separate a usable figure from a decorative one. It names the dataset and its edition. It names the year the data covers, not the year the article was written. And it states the price base, because international dollars at 2021 prices are not comparable with international dollars at 2017 prices. A table with none of those is a table you cannot check.

What this means for a manager

Very little, directly, and that is the honest answer. National productivity is set by capital intensity, industry mix, infrastructure, and regulation. None of those are inside a team leader's control, and no amount of internal process change moves a country's position.

What does transfer is the definition. Productivity is output over input at every level, which is why adding hours reliably makes the ratio worse rather than better. Our guide to how to improve employee productivity works through the levers that operate at team scale, and measuring employee productivity covers what to watch once you stop counting hours.

Where these figures are genuinely useful is in resisting a bad argument. When someone claims a national work ethic explains a performance gap, the data says otherwise. Countries with famously long hours sit well below countries with short ones, because hours are the denominator.

Key takeaways

  • Productivity by country means GDP per hour worked. It measures the value an hour produces, not how hard anyone is working.
  • Ireland tops every table because multinational profit shifting inflates its GDP. Its own GNI* measure puts it much closer to its neighbours.
  • The median country in the dataset produces $26.80 an hour and the top to bottom gap is a factor of 96.
  • The United States sits ninth at $83.50, close enough to Germany, Belgium, and the Netherlands that the differences are inside the measurement error.
  • Published tables disagree by up to 20% for the same country and year because they use different datasets, price bases, and PPP conversions.
  • Any figure worth quoting names its dataset, its edition, the year covered, and the price base. Most do not.
  • None of this is actionable at team level. The definition transfers, the ranking does not.

Frequently asked questions

Which country has the highest workplace productivity?

Ireland, at $158.20 of output per hour worked in 2023, according to Penn World Table 11.0. That figure is distorted by multinational profit shifting: profits booked through Irish subsidiaries land in Irish GDP while the hours that produced them were worked elsewhere. Norway at $130.10 is the highest figure not affected by that particular distortion.

How productive is the United States compared to other countries?

The United States produced $83.50 per hour worked in 2023, ninth of the 128 countries in the dataset. It sits within a few dollars of Germany, Belgium, the Netherlands, and Austria, a spread small enough that it is inside the measurement error of the conversions involved.

Why do productivity figures differ so much between sources?

Different datasets, different price base years, different purchasing power parity conversions, and different national definitions of an hour worked. We found three published figures for the United States in the same year ranging from $83.50 to $97.05. A figure is only checkable if it names the dataset, the edition, the year covered, and the price base.

Does working longer hours make a country more productive?

No, and usually the reverse in this measure. Hours worked are the denominator, so a country that produces the same output in fewer hours scores higher. Several countries known for long working hours sit well below countries with shorter weeks.

What is GDP per hour worked?

Everything an economy produced in a year divided by every hour its workers spent producing it, converted to a common currency using purchasing power parity so countries can be compared. It is an economy-wide measure and says nothing about any individual workplace.

Can a company improve its position in these rankings?

No. National productivity is driven by capital intensity, industry mix, infrastructure, and regulation, none of which a single employer controls. The definition of productivity as output over input does transfer to team level, which is the part worth taking from this data.

What year does this data cover?

2023, the most recent year in Penn World Table 11.0, released through Our World in Data and last updated in October 2025. Figures are in international dollars at 2021 prices.
