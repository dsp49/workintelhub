Employee Recognition Statistics (2026)

P Pavan
October 16, 2026 · 12 min read

Twenty-two percent of US employees strongly agree they receive the right amount of recognition for the work they do, down from 23% two years earlier. Fifty-five percent get either no recognition at all or recognition that fails every quality test Gallup applies. Those are the findings from Gallup and Workhuman's April 2024 survey of 4,439 US workers, the most recent national measure. Only 29% of employees strongly agree they received recognition or praise in the past seven days, which is the item Gallup has tracked since the 1990s.

Every page that ranks for this question is a recognition software vendor, and the figures they share most, that recognition cuts turnover by 31% or that 85% of employees prefer a thank-you, are a 2012 report nobody can find and a sum of two unrelated percentages. This page uses Gallup and Workhuman's six reports with their samples and dates, Deloitte's 16,000-person preference survey, two field experiments and the longitudinal turnover study, and sorts observed findings from modelled estimates.

How often employees are recognised

Gallup and Workhuman's Human-Centered Workplace report, from 4,439 US employees surveyed in April 2024, found 22% strongly agreeing they receive the right amount of recognition, against 23% in the February 2022 survey of 7,636. Peers recognise more often than managers: 46% receive peer recognition at least a few times a month, against 38% from a manager. The report's sharper finding is on quality. Gallup scores recognition on five pillars, fulfilling, authentic, personalised, equitable and embedded in the culture, and 55% of employees either receive no recognition or recognition that meets none of the five.

The 29% figure is from Gallup's engagement indicator and is one of the twelve items that make up its engagement score. Globally, about one in four employees in Gallup's database strongly agree with it. The Achievers Workforce Institute's 2025 survey, a vendor study with a stated sample, found the share who said their manager regularly recognises them fell from 20% to 15% in a year. Recognition and engagement

The 2024 report found employees whose recognition meets at least four of the five pillars are 9 times as likely to be engaged as those whose recognition meets none, and 12 times as likely to feel connected to the culture. Even one pillar nearly triples the odds. The 2022 report put it in simpler terms: employees who strongly agree they get the right amount of recognition are 4 times as likely to be engaged, 5 times as likely to feel connected to the culture, and 73% less likely to always or very often feel burned out. Employees recognised by leaders only a few times a year or less are 5 times as likely to be actively disengaged.

These are cross-sectional odds ratios. They show that recognised employees are engaged, not that recognising them caused it. The one peer-reviewed field experiment on this, by Bradler and colleagues in Management Science in 2016, gave thank-you cards to about 300 data-entry workers partway through a three-hour shift and measured a 5.2% rise in subsequent output, rising to 7.3% when the cards went to the best performers. Most of the gain came from workers who did not receive a card and worked harder anyway. Kosfeld and Neckermann's earlier experiment found a symbolic award raised performance by about 12%. Both are German studies with small samples, which is why they are quoted here as the causal evidence and the Gallup ratios as the descriptive evidence.

Recognition and turnover

The strongest turnover evidence is longitudinal. Gallup and Workhuman tracked the career paths of 3,447 employees from 2022 to 2024 and found those who had received high-quality recognition in 2022 were 45% less likely to have left their employer by 2024. The 2024 cross-section adds that employees whose recognition meets four or more pillars have 65% lower odds of actively looking or watching for another job, and the 2022 data found 74% higher odds of planning to leave within a year among employees recognised by leaders only a few times a year or less.

Gallup's 2023 equity report, from 10,026 working US adults, found the right amount of recognition was associated with 37% lower odds of job hunting overall, 43% for women and 26% for Black and Hispanic employees. The 2024 report's cost framing uses Gallup's replacement estimates of 40% of salary for frontline roles, 80% for technical roles and 200% for leaders. What kind of recognition works

Deloitte's 2019 survey of more than 16,000 professionals in more than 4,000 organisations remains the largest study of preference. For day-to-day accomplishments, 54% preferred a verbal thank-you and 31% a written one, with celebrations and gifts at 7% each. For a significant accomplishment, 47% preferred a new growth opportunity, ahead of a salary increase at 23%, a high performance rating at 21% and a bonus at 10%. Forty-nine percent wanted recognition shared with a few people, 34% private and 18% broadcast. Women were more likely than men to prefer written thanks, 36% against 28%.

Gallup's data add the gaps. Only 11% of employees say anyone at work has ever asked how they like to be recognised, and 13% strongly agree they know how their colleagues like to be recognised. About one in five receive a monetary reward alongside recognition, and where financial rewards are offered, employees are 28% more likely to recommend the organisation. Recognition programmes exist at 40% of organisations in 2024, up from 34% in 2022, but only 38% of employees with a programme rate it very good or excellent, and only 24% of them strongly agree they get the right amount. A programme is not the same as recognition. Our employee recognition ideas guide is built on the preference data above.

Manager against peer recognition

Managers matter most and recognise least. In Gallup's 2016 analysis, the most memorable recognition came from a manager for 28% of employees, a senior leader or CEO for 24%, the manager's manager for 12%, a customer for 10% and peers for 9%. Deloitte found 37% preferred recognition from leadership above their direct supervisor, 32% from the supervisor and 31% from colleagues. Yet in 2024 peers recognised more often than managers, 46% against 38% at least monthly, and in 2022, 67% of leaders and 61% of managers said they gave recognition a few times a week or more while only 21% of employees said they received it that often from them. The gap between what managers think they give and what employees report receiving is the finding most programmes are built to close. What leaders think, and budget

In 2022, nearly two in three senior leaders said their organisation had no budget allocated to recognition, and 81% said it was not a major strategic priority. Two years later, the share of senior leaders strongly agreeing that their leadership values recognition had risen from 28% to 42%. Employees did not see it: only 20% of managers and 22% of individual contributors strongly agreed that senior leaders value recognition in 2024. SHRM and Globoforce's 2017 survey of 738 HR leaders at organisations with 500 or more staff found 80% had a recognition programme, 70% of programmes were values-based, and programmes funded at 1% of payroll or more were 86% more likely to be rated good or excellent than low-cost e-thanks programmes.

Cost and ROI, and which numbers are models

The ROI figures on this topic are models, and they should be labelled as such. Gallup and Workhuman's 2023 ROI report applied Gallup's meta-analysis of 112,312 business units to a hypothetical 10,000-employee US company. If that company doubled the share of employees who strongly agree they received recognition in the past week, from one in four to one in two, the model estimates 9% higher productivity worth about $92 million a year, 22% fewer safety incidents worth $2.8 million, and 22% less absenteeism worth $3.8 million. The 2022 report modelled a $16.1 million annual turnover saving for the same hypothetical company. None of these are observed savings at a real organisation, and the inputs are 2021 salary and 2022 labour-share figures.

What can be observed is cheaper. A verbal thank-you costs nothing and is what 54% of employees prefer. The field experiments found output gains of 5% to 12% from a card. And the one practice that 89% of employees have never experienced, being asked how they want to be recognised, is a question. Figures to stop using

  • "Recognition programmes cut voluntary turnover by 31%." From Bersin and Associates' 2012 State of Employee Recognition, a survey of organisations comparing those with highly effective and ineffective programmes. The report is not public, and it is attributed online to Deloitte, SHRM and others. Use the 2024 longitudinal 45% instead.
  • "85% of employees prefer a simple thank-you." Deloitte's 54% verbal plus 31% written, summed. "59% prefer growth opportunities" should be 47%.
  • "Recognised employees are 5 times more engaged." Five different Gallup ratios from 2022 to 2024 measure different things. The 2024 figure for high-quality recognition is 9 times.
  • "Recognition reduces burnout by 90%." The upper bound across twelve countries in a 2022 opt-in sample. The US figure is 73%, and the 2023 figure for the stricter burnout measure is about 30%.
  • "Recognition saves $16 million" or "$92 million." Modelled estimates for a hypothetical 10,000-employee company.
  • "Only 1 in 3 received recognition in the last week." Gallup's 2016 US figure; the current US indicator reads 29% and the global database one in four.

Key takeaways

  • 22% of US employees strongly agree they receive the right amount of recognition, and 55% receive none or recognition meeting none of Gallup's five quality pillars, per the April 2024 survey of 4,439 workers.
  • 29% strongly agree they received recognition or praise in the last seven days. Only 11% have ever been asked how they like to be recognised.
  • Employees whose recognition meets four or more pillars are 9 times as likely to be engaged and have 65% lower odds of job hunting. These are cross-sectional odds, not causal effects.
  • Employees who received high-quality recognition in 2022 were 45% less likely to have left by 2024, in a longitudinal study of 3,447 workers.
  • 54% prefer a verbal thank-you and 31% a written one for everyday work. For major achievements, 47% prefer a growth opportunity over a raise at 23%.
  • Peers recognise more often than managers, 46% against 38% monthly, but managers are the most memorable source at 28%.
  • A thank-you card raised output 5.2% in a field experiment of about 300 workers. The $92 million and $16 million figures are models for a hypothetical company.
  • The '31% lower turnover' figure is from an unavailable 2012 report. '85% prefer a thank-you' is two Deloitte percentages added together.

Frequently asked questions

What percentage of employees feel recognised at work?

About one in five. 22% of US employees strongly agree they receive the right amount of recognition, per Gallup and Workhuman's April 2024 survey of 4,439 workers, and 29% strongly agree they received recognition or praise in the past seven days. 55% receive no recognition or recognition that meets none of Gallup's quality criteria.

How often should employees be recognised?

Gallup's benchmark is recognition within the past seven days, the wording of its engagement item. In 2022, only 21% of US employees received recognition from a manager a few times a week or more, and 40% received it a few times a year or less.

Does recognition reduce turnover?

The evidence says yes. Employees who received high-quality recognition in 2022 were 45% less likely to have left their employer by 2024 in Gallup and Workhuman's tracking of 3,447 workers. Employees whose recognition meets four of five quality pillars have 65% lower odds of looking for another job.

What type of recognition do employees prefer?

A thank-you. In Deloitte's 2019 survey of more than 16,000 professionals, 54% preferred a verbal thank-you and 31% a written one for day-to-day work. For a significant accomplishment, 47% preferred a new growth opportunity, 23% a salary increase, 21% a high performance rating and 10% a bonus.

Is manager or peer recognition more important?

Manager recognition is more memorable and peer recognition more frequent. The most memorable recognition came from a manager for 28% of employees and from peers for 9%, per Gallup. In 2024, 46% received peer recognition at least monthly against 38% from a manager.

Does recognition improve productivity?

In the one field experiment on it, a thank-you card raised subsequent output by 5.2% among about 300 workers, and a symbolic award raised it by about 12% in an earlier study. Gallup's model estimates a 9% productivity gain if an organisation doubled the share recognised weekly, but that is a projection.

What is the ROI of employee recognition?

Gallup and Workhuman's 2023 model estimates a hypothetical 10,000-employee US company would gain about $92 million a year in productivity, $3.8 million in reduced absenteeism and $2.8 million in fewer safety incidents by doubling weekly recognition, plus up to $16.1 million in turnover. These are modelled estimates, not observed savings.

Is it true that recognition reduces turnover by 31%?

That figure comes from a 2012 Bersin and Associates survey of organisations that is not publicly available and is routinely misattributed to Deloitte or SHRM. The better-supported figure is the 45% lower likelihood of leaving among well-recognised employees in Gallup and Workhuman's 2022 to 2024 longitudinal study.
