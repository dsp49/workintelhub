How to Build a People Analytics Function

P Pavan
November 26, 2026 · 7 min read

To build a people analytics function, hire or appoint one analyst who knows HR data, agree written definitions for headcount, turnover and hires, join the HRIS, applicant tracking and payroll systems, write the privacy rules before the first analysis, and start with three projects leaders have already committed to act on. Software comes after that, not before.

The order matters more than the budget. People analytics fails more often on data and definitions than on tools, and a function that launches with a platform and no agreed definitions spends its first year reconciling numbers. This guide covers staffing, data sources, privacy governance and the first three projects, with a 90-day plan at the end.

Staffing: who to hire, and when

The first hire matters most. Look for someone who understands HR data, including how the HRIS stores job changes and why the termination date and the last day worked differ, and who can write SQL or build in Power BI or Tableau. Domain knowledge is harder to teach than the tools. An internal HRIS analyst who has been doing reporting is often the right person.

StageWhoWhat they deliver
StartOne HR or people analyst, part or full timeTrusted monthly reporting, written definitions, the first diagnostic project
GrowA data engineer or a shared resource from the central data teamAutomated, joined data from HRIS, ATS, payroll and survey systems
ExtendSomeone with statistics or industrial-organizational psychology trainingPay equity models, survey design, attrition risk, testing whether programs worked
ScaleA lead who translates between analysts and executivesA roadmap tied to business decisions, and analysis built into planning cycles

Where it reports. Most functions report into the CHRO, which keeps access to sensitive data inside HR and keeps the work tied to HR decisions. The risk is that it becomes a report factory for HR operations. A dotted line to finance or the central data team helps with definitions and infrastructure. Whatever the line, give the function a named executive sponsor who brings it questions.

When to stop at one. In a company of a few hundred, one analyst with clean exports covers headcount, turnover, hiring and pay reporting, and that may be the whole function for years. Add people when the questions outgrow the analyst's time, not because the organization crossed a headcount threshold.

Before the data: agree the definitions

Write a one-page data dictionary before connecting anything. It needs, at minimum:

  • Headcount: who counts (employees only, or contractors, interns and temps), on which date, and whether it reconciles to finance's number.
  • Turnover: leavers divided by average headcount, with voluntary, involuntary and regretted defined, and whether internal transfers count. The turnover rate entry covers the choices.
  • Hire, rehire and transfer: what each means in the HRIS and the ATS, which often disagree.
  • Time to fill, time to hire, cost per hire, absence: the formulas and cost categories in our HR metrics guide.
  • Organization: which field is the manager, which is the team, and how job families and locations are coded.

Get the CHRO and the CFO to sign it. Every later argument about a number becomes an argument about the dictionary, which is quicker to settle.

The first three answer most early questions. Surveys come fourth because joining them to HR data is where anonymity promises and analysis collide: if the survey said responses would never be linked to individuals, report by team above a minimum group size and do not join at person level. Our engagement survey guide suggests a floor of five responses; our eNPS entry explains why team eNPS below about 30 respondents is mostly noise.

The sixth row is where people analytics becomes workforce analytics and, if attributed to individuals, monitoring. Our comparison of workforce and people analytics covers that boundary.

Privacy governance: write it before the first analysis

A people analytics function holds the most sensitive data in the company: pay, performance, health-related absence, protected attributes and survey answers. Write these rules down and publish them to employees before the first project:

  1. Purpose statement. What the function analyzes and why, and what it will not do, such as judging individuals from activity data or using survey answers in performance reviews.
  2. Minimum group size. No report on a group below a set number; report the next level up instead. Apply it to pay, diversity and survey results especially.
  3. Access tiers. Analysts see person-level data; managers see their team's aggregates; executives see the organization. Log who accessed what.
  4. Model rules. Attrition risk and other predictions are used for populations, reviewed for bias, and never used alone for a decision about a person. In New York City, automated employment decision tools used in hiring or promotion need a bias audit; other states and California's automated decision rules add their own requirements.
  5. Retention and deletion. How long raw extracts are kept and when they are deleted.
  6. Jurisdiction checks. GDPR and works council consultation in Europe, the California Consumer Privacy Act's employee provisions, and monitoring notice laws in four states for any activity data attributed to individuals.

The test for every output is whether it could be shown to the people it describes without difficulty. Our guide to analytics without surveillance has the design rules.

The first three projects

Pick projects that use the first three data sources, answer a question a leader has asked, and end in a decision. These three meet all three tests in most organizations.

1. Where turnover concentrates

Calculate voluntary and regretted turnover by manager, tenure band, job family and location, with first-year turnover separately. Add coded exit reasons from your exit interviews. The decision is where to put manager support and stay conversations. It is the right first project because the data is in the HRIS, the result is usually striking, and turnover by manager is typically the most concentrated pattern in the data.

2. Pay equity review

Compare pay by gender and, where data allows, race, controlling for grade, role, location, tenure and performance. Report the adjusted gap and the individuals who fall outside their band, to HR and counsel only. The decision is the adjustment budget. Several states now require pay analysis or reporting, which makes this the most common first use case for platforms; our pay transparency laws guide lists them. Run it under legal privilege where counsel advises.

3. Quality of hire by source

Join ATS source to first-year turnover and cost per hire for each channel: referral, job board, agency, internal move. The decision is next year's recruiting budget. It proves the value of joining two systems, and it puts a money figure on the answer that recruiting leaders can act on directly.

Each project should end in a one-page memo: the question, the finding, the decision taken, and the date the result will be re-measured. Our workforce analytics examples list seven more questions to queue after these.

When to buy a platform

Stay on spreadsheets and Power BI while one analyst can keep the joins current. Buy when data lives in several systems that disagree, definitions need governing across a large organization, predictive models are wanted, or analyst time is the bottleneck. ChartHop is priced for mid-sized companies, from $2 per employee for its Basic plan as read in October 2026; Visier, One Model and Crunchr are quoted. Key takeaways

  • Start with one analyst who knows HR data and can use SQL or Power BI; add engineering and statistics as questions grow.
  • Write a signed data dictionary for headcount, turnover, hires and organization fields before connecting any system.
  • Connect the HRIS, payroll and ATS first; add surveys, performance and activity data later, with anonymity and notice rules in mind.
  • Publish privacy rules first: purpose, minimum group size, access tiers, model rules, retention and jurisdiction checks.
  • First projects: turnover concentration, pay equity review and quality of hire by source, each ending in a recorded decision.
  • Buy a platform when data lives in several disagreeing systems or analyst time runs out, not before.

Frequently asked questions

Who should be the first people analytics hire?

An analyst who understands HR data and can write SQL or build in Power BI or Tableau. An internal HRIS or HR reporting analyst is often the best candidate, because domain knowledge is harder to teach than tools.

Where should people analytics report?

Usually to the CHRO, which keeps sensitive data inside HR and ties the work to HR decisions, with a dotted line to finance or the central data team for definitions and infrastructure. A named executive sponsor matters more than the reporting line.

What data sources does a people analytics team need?

Start with the HRIS, payroll and the applicant tracking system. Add engagement and exit surveys, performance and learning data, and time, absence and collaboration data as projects require them.

What should the first people analytics project be?

Turnover by manager, tenure and job family, split into voluntary and regretted, with first-year turnover separate. The data is already in the HRIS, the result is usually actionable, and it builds trust for the projects that follow.

How do you protect employee privacy in people analytics?

Publish a purpose statement, set a minimum group size for every report, restrict person-level data to analysts, never let a model decide about an individual, delete raw extracts on a schedule, and check GDPR, CCPA, AI-in-employment and monitoring notice rules for each data source.

Do you need software to start people analytics?

No. Most organizations under a few hundred employees can run the first projects on HRIS exports in a spreadsheet or Power BI. Platforms pay when data sits in several systems or the analyst's time runs out.
