Headcount Planning: How to Build a Headcount Plan

P Pavan
November 26, 2026 · 9 min read

A headcount plan states how many people the organization will have in each role and team at the end of each quarter, what they will cost, and how many hires it takes to get there once leavers are replaced. You build it by starting from today's roster, applying attrition by role, adding the new roles the business plan needs, and converting every hire into a requisition date using time to fill.

The number most plans get wrong is hires, not headcount. A team that stays flat at 15 percent attrition still has to hire 15 percent of its people in a year, and a growth plan that ignores backfills understates recruiting load by exactly that amount. This guide covers the columns a plan needs, the seven steps to build one, a calculator that turns attrition, growth and time to fill into hires per quarter, and the point at which a spreadsheet stops being enough.

The short answer

Hires needed = growth hires + backfills. Backfills are headcount times the role's attrition rate, so a flat plan still has a hiring line.

Plan by role and quarter, not by department and year. Departments balance in total and run short in the two roles that matter.

Every hire needs a requisition date. A role needed on April 1 with a 44-day time to fill and a notice period is a January requisition.

What a headcount plan is, and what it is not

Headcount planning is the operational and annual layer of workforce planning: how many people, in which roles and teams, approved against a budget, usually one year out and reviewed each quarter. Strategic workforce planning looks three to five years ahead at skills, locations and roles against the supply the organization will have after attrition and retirement; the strategic workforce planning guide covers that horizon. A headcount plan is narrower and more concrete. It ends in approved requisitions with names of hiring managers on them.

It is also not a capacity plan. Capacity planning matches a team's hours to its commitments over a quarter. When a capacity gap persists for two or three quarters, it becomes a line in the headcount plan. The workforce planning templates on this site include a headcount sheet by role and month, a gap analysis and a scenario model that this page's method fills in.

How to build a headcount plan in seven steps

  1. Pull the roster. Export current employees by role, team, level, location and start date from the HRIS. Add accepted offers; leave open requisitions out, because they are not people yet.
  2. Calculate attrition by role. Leavers in the past twelve months divided by average headcount for the role. The turnover rate entry covers the formula and why average headcount, not the start figure, is the right denominator. Where a role has too few people for a stable rate, use the rate for its job family.
  3. Collect demand from the business plan. Ask each budget owner for the end-of-quarter headcount the plan requires, and require one sentence of justification per new role: the revenue, volume or commitment it supports.
  4. Add the management layer. New individual contributors imply new managers. Check each team's span against the ranges in the span of control entry; roughly every ten new hires implies a manager somewhere.
  5. Compute hires per quarter. Backfills plus growth hires, by role. The calculator below does this for one role or team at a time.
  6. Convert hires to requisition dates. Subtract time to fill and the typical notice period from each need date. SHRM's 2025 benchmarking reports put median time to fill at 44 days; use your own figure by role where you have it.
  7. Cost it and get it signed. Multiply headcount by loaded cost per FTE, phase new hires by start month, and reconcile the total with finance before anyone opens a requisition.

Headcount calculator: hires needed per quarter

Backfills use the annual rate divided by four on each quarter's starting headcount, and growth hires are spread evenly. Open requisitions in flight are hires per year times time to fill divided by 365. Results are estimates for planning, not a forecast of who will leave.

Worked example: 120 people growing to 140

A company of 120 expects 15 percent attrition and wants 20 net new roles this year, with a 44-day time to fill. Growth adds five people a quarter. Backfills start at 4.5 a quarter (120 times 15 percent divided by four) and rise to about 5.1 by the fourth quarter as the base grows. Over the year that is roughly 19 backfills on top of 20 growth hires: about 39 hires to add 20 people.

Two numbers in that result change the conversation. First, almost half the hiring is replacement, so a recruiting budget sized to the 20 new roles is short by half. Second, with 39 hires a year and 44 days to fill each, about five requisitions are open at any moment. If one recruiter can carry ten, recruiting capacity is fine; if hiring managers can interview for only two roles at once, it is not. Run the calculator by role rather than for the whole company, because an engineering team at 8 percent attrition and a support team at 30 percent produce very different hiring loads from the same headcount.

From headcount to cost

A headcount plan that finance can sign is priced at loaded cost, not salary. The employee cost calculator builds that figure from payroll taxes, benefits, equipment and recruiting; the commonly used range is 1.25 to 1.4 times salary for white-collar roles. The BLS Employer Costs for Employee Compensation release for June 2026 puts benefits at about 30 percent of total private-industry compensation.

  • Phase by start date. A hire starting in October costs a quarter of a year's salary in this budget and a full year in the next. The run-rate column matters as much as the in-year total.
  • Price backfills too. Replacement hires carry recruiting cost and a ramp period even when salary is unchanged.
  • Include planned pay changes. Merit increases and promotions move cost without moving headcount.

Five mistakes that break headcount plans

  1. Company-average attrition. Frontline and early-career roles often run two to three times the average. Use the role's own rate.
  2. Need dates with no lead time. Approving a role for March and opening the requisition in March delivers a person in May or June.
  3. Forgetting ramp time. A new hire is not full capacity on day one. If the plan exists to meet a commitment, the start date has to land before the commitment, not on it.
  4. One scenario. Build base, growth and downturn versions with the same structure and change assumptions (volume, attrition, time to fill, cost), not outputs.
  5. A plan that lives apart from requisitions. If the approved plan and the applicant tracking system are reconciled only at year end, the plan stops describing reality by the second quarter.

When a spreadsheet stops being enough

A spreadsheet works for a company under a few hundred people with one entity and a stable structure. It fails when several budget owners edit at once, when scenarios multiply, when the plan has to be the same model finance uses, and when the org chart changes faster than the sheet.

At that point our review of workforce planning software sorts the options by horizon. For next quarter's openings and backfills, ChartHop plans headcount on the org chart from a published entry price. For an annual plan that must reconcile with the financial model, Anaplan, Pigment and Workday Adaptive Planning are connected-planning tools, quoted rather than priced publicly. Before buying any of them, check which horizon your problem is on; the workforce planning tools comparison explains why a five-year platform for a quarterly problem is the usual mistake. If the gap you are planning for is skills rather than seats, run a skills gap analysis alongside the headcount plan.

Key takeaways

  • A headcount plan states the headcount, cost and hires needed by role and quarter, ending in approved requisitions.
  • Hires needed equal growth hires plus backfills; a flat team at 15 percent attrition still hires 15 percent of its people each year.
  • Use the role's own trailing twelve-month attrition rate, not the company average.
  • Convert every need date into a requisition date with time to fill and notice period; SHRM's 2025 median time to fill is 44 days.
  • Price the plan at loaded cost and phase it by start date, so finance sees both the in-year total and the run rate.
  • Move off a spreadsheet when several owners edit at once or the plan must be finance's model.

Frequently asked questions

What is headcount planning?

Headcount planning sets how many people an organization will have in each role and team by quarter, what they will cost, and how many hires are needed to get there after attrition. It is usually done annually with the budget and refreshed each quarter.

How do you calculate how many hires you need?

Add growth hires (net new roles) to backfills. Backfills are headcount times the role's attrition rate for the period. For a quarter, use the annual rate divided by four on that quarter's starting headcount. A team of 120 at 15 percent annual attrition needs about 4.5 backfills a quarter before any growth.

What is the difference between headcount planning and workforce planning?

Headcount planning is the operational and annual layer: roles, numbers, cost and requisitions for the next year. Workforce planning also includes the longer strategic horizon of three to five years, covering skills, locations and the supply of people after attrition and retirement.

When should a requisition be opened?

Subtract time to fill and the typical notice period from the date the person is needed. With a 44-day time to fill and a four-week notice period, a role needed on April 1 should be opened in mid-January.

What attrition rate should a headcount plan use?

The role's own trailing twelve-month rate, calculated as leavers divided by average headcount. Where a role is too small for a stable rate, use its job family. Company averages understate attrition in frontline and early-career roles.

What software is used for headcount planning?

Spreadsheets for small organizations; ChartHop for headcount planning on the org chart; Anaplan, Pigment or Workday Adaptive Planning when the plan must be the same model as the financial plan. Our workforce planning software review compares them by planning horizon.
