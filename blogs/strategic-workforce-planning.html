Strategic Workforce Planning: Guide and Free Templates

WH The WorkIntel Hub editorial team
October 9, 2026 · 12 min read

Strategic workforce planning asks what workforce the organization will need in three to five years to deliver its strategy, what workforce it will have if nothing changes, and what it will take to close the difference. It differs from annual headcount planning in horizon and in what it varies: not just how many people, but which roles exist at all, which skills matter, where the work is done, and whether it is done by employees, contractors or software.

This guide sets out a six-step method that HR and finance teams can run without a consultancy, the data each step needs, the mistakes that make plans unusable, and a set of templates: a strategy-to-workforce translation sheet, a demand forecast by role family, a supply projection, a gap and action plan, a scenario comparison and a one-page plan summary for the board. The workforce planning templates hold the operational sheets this method feeds.

How to run the process, step by step

The table above is the map. The steps below are the order of work most teams follow in practice, starting with supply because the HRIS already holds it and it is the quickest way to show leadership why a plan is needed. Each step names the output the next one depends on, so the plan can be built in a few working sessions rather than a quarter.

  1. Analyze supply. Export headcount by role family, with start dates, ages and the past twelve months of leavers, promotions and transfers. Calculate attrition for each role family as leavers divided by average headcount; the turnover rate entry explains why average headcount is the right denominator. Apply that rate year by year, subtract known retirements, and add or subtract internal moves. The output is projected FTE per role family for each of the next three years if nobody is hired.
  2. Forecast demand. Agree the driver for each role family with its business owner, take the driver forecast from finance, and divide current FTE by the current driver to get a ratio. Apply the ratio to each year's forecast, then adjust for any productivity change you can evidence. Role families with no driver are listed as policy choices with the size leadership sets.
  3. Analyze the gap. Subtract demand from supply for each role family and year. A negative number is a shortage; a positive one is a surplus to redeploy or hold. Then repeat the comparison for the six to ten skills that the strategy depends on, because a role family that is full in number can still be short of the skills it needs. The skills gap analysis guide sets out the 0 to 4 rating scale and the coverage count for that part.
  4. Choose solutions. For each gap, decide the mix of build, buy, borrow, automate and bind. Build where the gap is a level of depth and there is time; buy where the skill is common and the date is near; borrow for peaks, temporary needs and rare skills; automate only where the productivity gain can be shown; bind first wherever attrition is driving the shortage. Give every action an owner, a cost and a start date, and turn hiring actions into requisition dates using time to fill.
  5. Model scenarios. Run the same sheets with a growth and a downturn case alongside the base, changing assumptions (driver volume, attrition, lead time, loaded cost) rather than outputs, so the plan shows which actions hold in every case and which depend on one future.
  6. Monitor and refresh. Each quarter, replace the assumptions with actuals: attrition by role family, hires against plan, open requisitions and the gap. Rebuild the plan annually alongside the budget, and revisit the scenarios whenever the strategy or the outlook changes. The metrics section below lists what to put on the quarterly page.

Forecasting demand without a crystal ball

Demand is forecast through drivers: the thing the role family's workload scales with. Customer service scales with customers or tickets, engineering with product roadmap, sales with revenue targets, operations with volume. Divide current FTE by the current driver to get a ratio, apply the ratio to the three-year driver forecast, and adjust for productivity changes you expect from process or automation. The sheet does this per role family. Where a role family has no driver, it is probably overhead whose size is a policy choice, and the plan should say so.

Resist forecasting at job-title level. Role families (customer-facing, engineering, operations, corporate) are stable enough to plan with; titles change every reorganization. The plan stops at role families and years; staffing those people against hourly or daily demand once they are hired is a shorter-horizon job, and our comparison of workforce management software covers the tools that forecast, schedule and track it.

Projecting supply honestly

Supply compounds: a role family with 100 people and 18 percent attrition has about 55 of today's people in three years before any hiring. Retirements are known from ages and can be large in some trades. Internal promotions out of a family are a supply loss for it and a gain for the one above. The turnover rate entry covers the calculation, and the retention strategies guide the levers that change it, which is the "bind" action in step five.

Build, buy, borrow, bot, bind

  • Build: develop existing people into the roles needed; slowest, cheapest per head, best for retention. The development plan templates are the tool.
  • Buy: hire; fastest for common skills, expensive and slow for scarce ones; lead times go in the plan.
  • Borrow: contractors and partners for peaks and rare skills; watch the classification rules.
  • Bot: automate work so fewer people are needed; the productivity assumption in the demand sheet, which must be evidenced, not hoped.
  • Bind: retain the people you have, especially in critical roles; cheaper than any of the others and usually the first thing to do.

Worked example: a customer service role family

The figures below are illustrative, chosen to show the arithmetic, not drawn from any real organization. A customer service role family has 100 FTE today supporting 50,000 customers, a ratio of one FTE per 500 customers. The business plan expects 70,000 customers in three years. Trailing attrition for the role family is 18 percent, three people are expected to retire over the period, and about five a year are promoted out into team lead and operations roles.

A shortfall of 89 against a team of 100 looks alarming, and most of it comes from attrition rather than growth: growth adds 26 FTE of demand, while attrition, retirements and promotions remove 63 of today's people. That is why the actions start with bind. If retention work brings attrition from 18 to 14 percent, supply after attrition becomes 100 × 0.86 × 0.86 × 0.86, about 64, and the gap shrinks to 80. Nine fewer hires is nine fewer recruiting cycles; at the average cost per hire of $5,475 reported in the SHRM 2025 benchmarking reports, that is about $49,000 in recruiting cost alone, before ramp time.

The remaining 80 are then split across the other levers: most bought through phased hiring, a handful borrowed from a contact-center partner for seasonal peaks, and the team lead pipeline built internally through development plans so the promotions out are planned rather than a surprise. In a real sheet, people hired during the period also leave at the role family's rate, so the hiring line is larger than the gap; this example leaves that out to keep the arithmetic visible. Each hire then needs a requisition date: with a median time to fill of 44 days in the same SHRM data, plus notice periods, a hire needed in April is a January or February requisition.

Why strategic workforce plans fail

  • Built by HR alone, without finance's forecast or the business's operating plan, so nobody owns the numbers.
  • Forecast at job-title level, obsolete at the first reorganization.
  • Supply projected without attrition, so the gap is understated by a third.
  • One scenario; the plan breaks the moment the business outlook changes.
  • Actions without owners, costs and dates; a gap is not a plan.
  • Never reviewed. Quarterly refresh of attrition and requisitions, annual rebuild; the HR metrics guide lists the measures to track.

The metrics to track

A strategic plan is tested by a short set of measures refreshed every quarter. Most come from the HR system and finance; the formulas and the definitional choices behind them are in the HR metrics guide, which also has a calculator for the turnover and hiring figures.

Benchmarks from SHRM, the BLS and other sources use different definitions, so compare the direction of your own numbers over time before comparing their level with anyone else's. The measure that matters most for this plan is the gap itself: if it is not shrinking by role family each quarter, one of the assumptions is wrong, and the quarterly refresh should find which.

When software helps

The templates on this page are enough for a company of a few hundred people with one entity and a stable structure. A spreadsheet starts to fail when several teams plan at once, when the plan has to reconcile with finance's model, when scenarios multiply, and when the org chart changes faster than the sheet. The strategic horizon adds its own strain: skills tracked across thousands of people, linked to the job architecture in the HR system and projected by location.

The horizon decides the tool. Next year's headcount and cost plan, the layer covered by our headcount planning guide, is a connected-planning problem for tools such as Anaplan, Pigment and Workday Adaptive Planning, with ChartHop planning headcount on the org chart for the next quarter. The three-to-five-year skills and location plan this guide describes is the layer that SAP SuccessFactors Workforce Analytics and Planning, Visier, Anaplan and One Model are positioned for. Our guide to the best workforce planning software compares eight tools by horizon, with pricing checked in October 2026; all but ChartHop are quoted.

Before buying, settle the method. Software speeds up the arithmetic and keeps several planners in one model, but it does not choose the drivers, set the attrition assumptions or agree the actions; a tool bought before those decisions are made automates a plan nobody owns. Run the gap by skill as well as by seat with a skills gap analysis, and check which horizon your problem sits on, because buying a five-year platform for a quarterly problem is the usual mistake. Key takeaways

  • Strategic workforce planning asks what workforce the strategy needs in three to five years, what you will have, and how to close the gap.
  • Forecast demand through drivers at role-family level; project supply with compounding attrition, retirements and moves.
  • Close gaps with build, buy, borrow, bot and bind, each with an owner, a cost and a date; bind first.
  • Build it with finance and the business, model more than one scenario, review quarterly.

Frequently asked questions

What is strategic workforce planning?

The process of forecasting the workforce an organization will need in three to five years to deliver its strategy, projecting the workforce it will have after attrition, retirement and moves, and planning how to close the gap through development, hiring, contracting, automation and retention.

How is it different from workforce planning or headcount planning?

Headcount planning sets next year's numbers against a budget. Workforce planning adds attrition, skills and succession over one to three years. Strategic workforce planning starts from the strategy, works at role-family level over three to five years, and questions which roles and skills should exist at all.

What data do I need?

From the business: the strategic plan and a three-year forecast of the drivers (revenue, volume, projects). From HR: headcount by role family, attrition by role family, ages for retirement projection, internal mobility, and a skills inventory for critical roles. From finance: loaded cost per FTE and hiring costs.

What are build, buy, borrow, bot and bind?

The five ways to close a workforce gap: develop existing people, hire, use contractors, automate the work, and retain the people you have. Most plans use several; retention is usually cheapest and fastest.

How often should the plan be reviewed?

Refresh attrition, requisitions and the gap quarterly; rebuild the plan annually alongside budgeting; revisit scenarios whenever the strategy or the business outlook changes materially.

How do you calculate a workforce gap?

Projected supply minus projected demand, by role family and year. Supply is current headcount reduced by the role family's attrition rate each year, minus known retirements, plus and minus internal moves. Demand is current FTE divided by the current driver, applied to the driver forecast and adjusted for evidenced productivity changes. A negative result is a shortage to close; a positive one is a surplus to redeploy or hold.

Which metrics should a strategic workforce plan track?

Turnover rate and voluntary turnover by role family, first-year turnover, time to fill, cost per hire, loaded cost per FTE, coverage of critical skills, and actual headcount and gap against the plan. Refresh them quarterly and compare the direction of your own numbers over time before comparing them with external benchmarks.

When do you need workforce planning software?

When several teams plan at once, when the plan must reconcile with finance's model, when scenarios multiply, or when skills must be tracked across thousands of people by location. Below a few hundred people with one entity and a stable structure, spreadsheet templates are usually enough. Settle the method first, then choose a tool that matches the planning horizon.
