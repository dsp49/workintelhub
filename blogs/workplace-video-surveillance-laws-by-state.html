Workplace Video Surveillance Laws by State (2026 Table)

P Pavan
November 12, 2026 · 14 min read

No federal law stops an employer from pointing a silent camera at a cash register, a loading dock or an open-plan office. The limits come from four other places: state statutes that name the rooms where cameras are forbidden (five states), state laws that require written notice of electronic monitoring (three states), the wiretap laws that apply the moment a camera records sound (and that in eleven states require everyone's consent), and the National Labor Relations Act, which bars surveillance aimed at union or concerted activity. Everywhere else, a general criminal law against recording people where they have a reasonable expectation of privacy fills the gap.

This guide gives the federal position, the state statutes with citations, a 51-jurisdiction table covering restroom and locker-room bans, notice requirements and the audio consent rule, the retention and access norms courts expect, and a camera policy employers can adopt. For the wider rules on monitoring computers and phones, see employee monitoring laws by state; for the consent rules on recording conversations, see recording conversations at work.

The federal baseline

Federal law contains no statute on workplace video. The Electronic Communications Privacy Act of 1986 (18 U.S.C. 2511) regulates the interception of oral, wire and electronic communications, which is why a camera that records audio is treated differently from one that does not: silent video is outside the Act, audio is inside it, and the federal rule for audio is that one party to the conversation must consent. The Video Voyeurism Prevention Act (18 U.S.C. 1801) criminalizes capturing images of a person's private areas without consent where they have a reasonable expectation of privacy, but only on federal property.

The result is that in most of the country an employer may install silent cameras in work areas, entrances, parking lots and stockrooms without notice or consent, subject to three constraints that apply everywhere: the common-law tort of intrusion upon seclusion (a camera in a place where privacy is reasonably expected, such as a restroom, is actionable even without a statute), the National Labor Relations Act, and state criminal laws against recording in private places. Courts have consistently held that an open office, a factory floor or a retail counter is not a place of seclusion, so cameras there do not give rise to a privacy claim. A camera aimed at one employee's desk to build a case against them is a closer question, and is where most litigation arises.

Where cameras are prohibited

Five states have statutes written specifically for employers that forbid cameras in named rooms:

  • California: Labor Code 435 prohibits an employer from causing an audio or video recording to be made of an employee in a restroom, locker room or room designated for changing clothes, except by court order, and bars use of any such recording. A new law signed September 30, 2026, AB 1331, effective January 1, 2027, adds a bar on using workplace surveillance tools in workplace bathrooms; an earlier draft that would have covered break rooms and off-duty conduct was narrowed before passage.
  • New York: Labor Law 203-c prohibits video recording of employees in restrooms, locker rooms or rooms designated for changing clothes, except by court order, and bars use of such recordings.
  • Connecticut: General Statutes 31-48b prohibits electronic surveillance in areas designed for the health or personal comfort of employees or for safeguarding their possessions, which the statute lists as restrooms, locker rooms and lounges.
  • Rhode Island: General Laws 28-6.12-1 prohibits audio or video recording of employees in restrooms, locker rooms or changing rooms unless authorized by a court, and gives employees a civil action including punitive damages and attorney's fees.
  • West Virginia: Code 21-3-20 prohibits cameras in areas designated for employee health or personal comfort or for safeguarding possessions, including locker rooms and lounges.

Connecticut and West Virginia are the broadest because they reach lounges and break areas, not just restrooms and changing rooms. In the other 45 states and the District of Columbia, the same conduct is almost always a crime under a general voyeurism or unlawful-surveillance statute (Michigan's MCL 750.539d makes recording in a private place without consent a felony; Minnesota's 609.746 criminalizes surreptitious imaging where privacy is reasonably expected), and it is a tort everywhere. The practical rule is identical in all 51 jurisdictions: no cameras in restrooms, locker rooms, changing areas, lactation rooms or medical rooms. The difference is who enforces it and what the penalty is.

States that require notice

Three states require employers to tell employees about electronic monitoring before it starts, and their definitions are broad enough to cover video in some circumstances:

  • Connecticut (31-48d): prior written notice of the types of electronic monitoring that may occur, posted conspicuously. "Electronic monitoring" means collecting information about employees' activities by any means other than direct observation, which includes cameras. Civil penalties are $500 for a first violation, $1,000 for a second and $3,000 for each thereafter. An exception allows unannounced monitoring where the employer reasonably believes employees are breaking the law or creating a hostile environment.
  • Delaware (19 Del. C. 705): notice before monitoring or intercepting telephone, email or internet use, either a daily electronic notice or a one-time written notice acknowledged by the employee. The statute is aimed at communications rather than cameras, so silent video is generally outside it; audio recording of calls is inside.
  • New York (Civil Rights Law 52-c, effective May 7, 2022): written notice on hiring, acknowledged in writing or electronically, plus a posted notice, for any employer that monitors telephone, email or internet access or usage. Enforced by the Attorney General with escalating civil penalties; there is no private right of action. As in Delaware, the trigger is communications monitoring, so cameras alone do not trigger it, but a video system that also captures screens or calls does.

Texas (Government Code 2054.008) and Colorado (C.R.S. 24-72-502) require notice from public employers only. No other state has a general notice statute, although several, including Illinois through its Biometric Information Privacy Act, impose notice and consent requirements when cameras are paired with facial recognition (see retention and biometrics below). Notice is nonetheless the standard recommendation everywhere, because a posted sign and a handbook paragraph defeat the "reasonable expectation of privacy" element of a tort claim and support the consent exception to wiretap law if audio is recorded.

When audio turns video into a wiretap

The moment a camera records sound, the recording is an interception of an oral communication and state and federal wiretap law applies. Under federal law and in most states one party's consent suffices, and courts have held that an employer whose policy discloses audio recording has the consent of its employees; customers and visitors are a separate matter. In eleven states the rule is all-party consent for private conversations: California, Delaware (under its privacy statute), Florida, Illinois, Maryland, Massachusetts, Michigan (with a disputed participant exception), Montana, New Hampshire, Pennsylvania and Washington. In those states, a camera with a live microphone in a break room records conversations between people who have not consented, and each recording can be a separate felony. Connecticut and Nevada apply all-party rules to phone calls only, Missouri and Hawaii to in-person conversations in private places, and Oregon's in-person rule has been in litigation since 2023.

The California penalty illustrates the exposure: Penal Code 632 carries a fine of up to $2,500 and up to a year in jail per violation, and Penal Code 637.2 gives a civil action for the greater of $5,000 per violation or three times actual damages. Florida's statute is a third-degree felony; Illinois's eavesdropping law is a Class 4 felony. The simple fix is to disable audio on surveillance cameras unless there is a specific, disclosed reason to record it, and to post audio-recording notices wherever it is enabled. The full state-by-state consent table is in recording conversations at work.

Unions and the NLRA

Section 8(a)(1) of the National Labor Relations Act makes it an unfair labor practice to surveil employees engaged in union or other protected concerted activity, or to create the impression of surveillance. Installing cameras in response to an organizing campaign, pointing them at a bulletin board where union notices are posted, or filming a lawful picket are classic violations. In a unionized workplace, installing cameras is also a mandatory subject of bargaining under Colgate-Palmolive (1997), so the employer must bargain over the decision and its effects before installing them.

The NLRB General Counsel's 2022 memorandum GC 23-02 proposed treating intrusive electronic monitoring as presumptively unlawful; it was rescinded on February 14, 2025 by GC 25-05. The Board's Stericycle (2023) standard for judging workplace rules, which asks whether a reasonable employee would read a rule as chilling protected activity, remained in force as of late 2026, although the current General Counsel asked the Board in August 2026 to overturn it. A camera policy should therefore still state that cameras are not used to monitor protected activity, and should avoid placement that could be read that way.

Hidden cameras and private-place laws

Every state has a criminal statute against secretly recording people in places where they have a reasonable expectation of privacy; the Reporters Committee's hidden-camera survey lists them. Representative examples are Michigan 750.539d (felony, up to two years), Delaware 11 Del. C. 1335, Maryland Criminal Law 3-902 and 3-903, Georgia 16-11-62, Alabama 13A-11-32, Utah 76-9-702.7, Tennessee 39-13-607 and Washington 9A.44.115. Most are written around the victim's state of undress or the privacy of the place rather than the identity of the recorder, so they apply to employers as fully as to anyone else.

Covert cameras in work areas occupy a middle ground. Courts have generally allowed hidden cameras in non-private work areas when installed to investigate specific suspected theft or misconduct, and have been hostile to them when aimed at a particular employee for reasons unrelated to security (several state courts have allowed intrusion claims over cameras hidden in private offices). Connecticut's notice law carves out an exception for unannounced monitoring where the employer reasonably believes employees are breaking the law, which is the model most counsel follow: a covert camera should be tied to a documented suspicion, limited in time and scope, placed only in areas where there is no expectation of seclusion, and never record audio in an all-party state.

Retention, access and biometric add-ons

No US statute sets a retention period for workplace camera footage; 30 to 90 days is the industry norm, and footage relevant to an incident, a complaint or litigation must be preserved under the ordinary duty to preserve evidence. Deleting footage after a complaint is spoliation. Footage used in discipline becomes a personnel record, and in states with personnel-file access laws (about 20, including California Labor Code 1198.5, Illinois, Massachusetts, Connecticut and Minnesota) the employee may be entitled to see it. For the broader retention rules, see payroll record retention by state.

Adding facial recognition or other biometrics to cameras changes the legal picture. Illinois's Biometric Information Privacy Act requires written notice, a written retention policy and informed written consent before collecting a face geometry scan, with statutory damages of $1,000 per negligent and $5,000 per intentional violation (per person after the 2024 amendment limiting per-scan accrual). Texas and Washington have biometric statutes enforced by the Attorney General; Colorado's privacy law covers employee biometrics from July 2025; New York City's biometric identifier law requires signage in commercial establishments. The biometric privacy laws by state table has the details. Camera analytics that count people or detect motion without identifying individuals generally fall outside these laws.

A camera policy that holds up

  1. Purpose: state the reasons (safety, security, theft prevention, incident investigation) and commit not to use cameras for productivity measurement or to monitor protected activity.
  2. Locations: list the areas covered (entrances, cash handling, warehouse, parking) and the areas excluded (restrooms, locker rooms, changing areas, lactation rooms, medical rooms, break rooms where the state requires, union notice areas).
  3. Audio: state that cameras do not record audio, or list the exact locations where they do and the notice posted there.
  4. Notice: signage at entrances and in covered areas; a handbook paragraph; in Connecticut, Delaware and New York, the statutory written notice and acknowledgment.
  5. Access: who may view live feeds and recordings, logged access, and the rule that footage is reviewed for stated purposes only.
  6. Retention: a fixed period (for example 45 days), automatic deletion, and a litigation-hold procedure.
  7. Covert cameras: permitted only on written authorization from a named officer, for a documented investigation, for a limited period, in non-private areas, without audio.
  8. Biometrics: none without a separate written policy and, where required, written consent.
  9. Union workplaces: bargain before installing or expanding.

If you are the employee

A camera in an open work area, a hallway, a stockroom or a parking lot is almost always lawful, with or without a sign. A camera in a restroom, locker room, changing area or lactation room is unlawful everywhere; report it to HR in writing, and if nothing happens, to the state labor department (Connecticut, New York, Rhode Island, West Virginia and California have specific statutes to cite) or the police under the state's voyeurism law. A camera with a microphone in a break room in an all-party consent state is a wiretap problem for the employer, and recordings made that way are generally inadmissible against you. If cameras appeared during a union campaign or point at the union bulletin board, the NLRB regional office takes charges without a lawyer. Ask HR, in writing, where cameras are, whether they record audio and how long footage is kept; in Connecticut and New York you are entitled to a written answer. Key takeaways

  • No federal statute regulates silent workplace video; limits come from state camera bans, state notice laws, wiretap law when audio is recorded, the NLRA and the common-law tort of intrusion.
  • Five states ban employer cameras in restrooms, locker rooms and changing rooms by statute: California (Labor Code 435, plus AB 1331 from January 1, 2027), New York (Labor Law 203-c), Connecticut (31-48b, including lounges), Rhode Island (28-6.12-1, with punitive damages) and West Virginia (21-3-20). Everywhere else the same conduct is a crime under general voyeurism law.
  • Connecticut requires prior written notice of all electronic monitoring; Delaware and New York require notice before monitoring phone, email and internet use, which catches camera systems that also capture communications.
  • Audio changes everything: a camera that records sound is a wiretap, and eleven states (California, Delaware, Florida, Illinois, Maryland, Massachusetts, Michigan, Montana, New Hampshire, Pennsylvania, Washington) require all parties' consent. Disable microphones unless there is a disclosed reason.
  • Surveilling union or concerted activity violates the NLRA, and in unionized workplaces camera installation must be bargained; the 2022 NLRB memo on monitoring was rescinded in 2025 but the underlying rules remain.
  • No statute sets a retention period; 30 to 90 days is the norm, footage relevant to a complaint must be preserved, and adding facial recognition triggers biometric laws such as Illinois BIPA.
  • A defensible policy names purposes, covered and excluded areas, the audio rule, notice, access logging, retention, a covert-camera authorization process and a no-biometrics default.

Frequently asked questions

Is it legal for employers to have cameras in the workplace?

Yes, in work areas, entrances, stockrooms and parking lots, in every state, as long as the cameras do not record in places where employees have a reasonable expectation of privacy and are not used to surveil union activity. Restrooms, locker rooms and changing areas are off limits everywhere, by specific statute in California, New York, Connecticut, Rhode Island and West Virginia and by general criminal law elsewhere.

Do employers have to tell employees about security cameras?

Only Connecticut requires prior written notice of all electronic monitoring, including cameras. Delaware and New York require notice before monitoring communications, which applies if the system captures calls or screens. No other state requires notice for silent video, but posting signs is standard practice because it defeats privacy claims and supports consent if audio is recorded.

Can workplace security cameras record audio?

Only with consent under wiretap law. In most states one party's consent is enough, and a disclosed policy supplies the employees' consent, but visitors and customers have not consented. In California, Florida, Illinois, Maryland, Massachusetts, Michigan, Montana, New Hampshire, Pennsylvania, Washington and under Delaware's privacy statute, every person recorded must consent. Most employers disable audio.

Can my employer put a camera in the break room?

In most states, yes, if it is silent and disclosed, because a shared break room is not a place of seclusion. Connecticut and West Virginia prohibit cameras in employee lounges by statute. A break-room camera with a microphone in an all-party consent state is unlawful. Cameras aimed at union notices or installed during an organizing campaign raise NLRA problems anywhere.

Are hidden cameras at work legal?

Hidden cameras in non-private work areas have generally been upheld when installed to investigate specific suspected theft or misconduct and limited in scope. Hidden cameras in restrooms, locker rooms or changing areas are crimes in every state. Connecticut requires notice of monitoring but exempts unannounced monitoring where the employer reasonably believes the law is being broken.

How long can employers keep security camera footage?

No US law sets a limit. Most employers keep footage 30 to 90 days and overwrite it. Footage relevant to an accident, complaint, investigation or threatened lawsuit must be preserved, and footage used in discipline may be part of the personnel record that employees can inspect in states with personnel-file access laws.

Can employers use facial recognition on security cameras?

Only after complying with biometric privacy laws. Illinois requires written notice, a retention policy and informed written consent, with damages of $1,000 to $5,000 per person. Texas and Washington regulate biometric identifiers through their attorneys general, Colorado covers employee biometrics, and New York City requires signage. Several other states treat biometric data as sensitive personal information under consumer privacy laws.

What can I do if I find a camera in the restroom at work?

Report it to HR in writing and photograph its location. It is a crime in every state and a specific labor violation in California, New York, Connecticut, Rhode Island and West Virginia; Rhode Island and California give employees a civil action. You can also report it to the police under the state's voyeurism law and to the state labor department. Retaliation for reporting is unlawful.
