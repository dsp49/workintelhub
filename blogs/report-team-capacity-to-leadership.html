How to Report Team Capacity to Leadership

WH The WorkIntel Hub editorial team
May 6, 2026 · 7 min read

A capacity report is a short document that tells leadership how much work a team can take on in a defined period, what it has already committed to, and what would have to change for it to accept more. Most managers write something closer to a complaint: a feeling (the team is stretched), a percentage nobody can verify, and an implied request for more people. Executives discount that, and they discount it faster each time it arrives.

The work splits into two parts. The first is making your numbers defensible. The second is framing them as a decision rather than a grievance, and it matters more than the first. A rough number attached to a clear tradeoff beats a precise number attached to an emotion, every time.

Headcount, capacity, and committed capacity are three different numbers

Headcount is people, capacity is available working time, and committed capacity is the part of that time already promised to something. Conflating the three is the single most common reason a capacity conversation goes nowhere. A leader who hears "we have eight people" assumes eight people of delivery. You know that two are split across another project, one carries the on-call rotation, and the whole team loses roughly a day a week to standing meetings.

The meeting deduction is usually the one that surprises people, including the manager doing the math. Microsoft's 2025 Work Trend Index, which combined anonymized Microsoft 365 telemetry with a survey of 31,000 knowledge workers across 31 markets, found that half of all meetings fall inside the 9 to 11 am and 1 to 3 pm windows, and that 57% of meetings are ad hoc, called without a calendar invite. A calendar-based deduction therefore undercounts, and it undercounts precisely in the hours when concentrated work would otherwise happen.

Term What it means Why it matters in the report
Headcount People employed on the team The number leadership already knows; never the answer on its own
Theoretical capacity Headcount multiplied by working days in the period Useful only as a starting point before deductions
Available capacity Theoretical capacity minus PTO, company holidays, meetings, and standing duties The honest ceiling for planned work
Committed capacity Available capacity already allocated to agreed work Shows what a new request would displace
Reserve A named allowance for unplanned and interrupt-driven work Makes invisible work visible and budgeted
Free capacity What genuinely remains The only number a leader can actually spend

Once those six lines exist, the report almost writes itself. What is left is tone, and tone is where most capacity reports die. The wider context for why these numbers behave the way they do sits in our complete guide to employee productivity.

Why "we are at 110%" gets ignored

A figure above one hundred percent reads as a complaint rather than a data point. It also fails an obvious test in the room: if the team were genuinely at 110 percent, something would already be late, so the executive reasonably concludes the number is rhetorical. Worse, the claim offers nothing to decide. It asks a leader to feel bad and then work out the implications alone, which is not a thing busy people do.

Percentages fail for a second reason. They compress the shape of the problem into one scalar, and the shape is the useful part. Being 10 percent over because of one deadline is a different problem from being 10 percent over every period for six months, and the second one is the pattern that turns into people working evenings. If your numbers show that shape, treat it as a workload issue rather than a planning issue and read our guide to the signs of employee burnout hiding in workload data before you write the report.

What should you say instead? Say what fits, what does not, and what a new request would displace. "We can deliver the migration and the reporting rebuild this quarter. Adding the partner integration means one of those two moves to next quarter. Which do you want?" That sentence gets a decision. The percentage never did.

How to calculate available capacity honestly

Start from working days and subtract everything that is not planned delivery work. Do the subtraction with recorded data rather than assumption, because assumption always flatters the plan. Where you have time records, use them. Where you do not, ask the team to log a rough split for two weeks, which is enough to expose the pattern without becoming a project.

  1. Count working days in the period per person, then subtract booked PTO and company holidays.
  2. Subtract recurring meetings, ceremonies, and any standing rotation such as on-call or triage.
  3. Subtract an observed allowance for interruptions and support, based on recent periods.
  4. Subtract onboarding, training, and any partial allocation to another team or project.
  5. What remains is available capacity. Express it in days or person-weeks, never in percent.
  6. Allocate committed work against it and keep the reserve line visible on the page.

Days and person-weeks travel better than percentages because they map onto scope. A leader can picture what fifteen person-days buys and can weigh it against the thing they want. Nobody can picture 82 percent, and nobody has ever changed a roadmap because of it.

Size the interruption allowance from observation rather than instinct. The same Microsoft research reported that employees are interrupted every 2 minutes during work hours, which adds up to 275 interruptions a day from meetings, emails, and notifications. Your team's number will differ, but the direction is reliable: whatever you were going to deduct for fragmentation is probably too small. Protecting what remains is its own discipline, covered in our guide to setting focus blocks that survive a busy calendar.

One honest limitation. Capacity figures for knowledge work carry real uncertainty, and anyone presenting them to two decimals is selling something. Round generously, put the assumptions on the page, and be willing to say which numbers you trust least. Naming the soft spots makes the firm numbers more believable, not less.

Present a tradeoff, not a complaint

A capacity report should always arrive with options attached. Two or three of them, each stating what gets delivered and what gets dropped, turn you from a person reporting bad news into a person running a plan. That is a different relationship, and leaders remember which of their managers brings the second kind of document.

Build the options around what is cheap to drop. The Eisenhower matrix is a serviceable filter here: separate the genuinely urgent from the genuinely important, and be willing to show leadership the items that are urgent to someone but important to nobody. Those are the easy yes. An option list where every choice is painful gets deferred, and a deferred decision defaults to doing everything badly.

Never send a problem without an options list

If the report contains a capacity problem and no options, you have handed your workload math to someone with less context than you and asked them to solve it. They will either ignore it or solve it in a way you dislike. Write the options yourself, mark the one you recommend, and say what you will do if no decision comes back by a stated date. Silence is also an answer, and naming that in advance keeps the report from going nowhere.

What the one-page capacity report looks like

The format that works is one page with five blocks, read top to bottom in the order an executive thinks. Keep it to a single page even when the underlying model is large. The detail can live in an appendix nobody opens, and that is fine, because the appendix exists to be available rather than to be read.

  • Position. One sentence: what the team can deliver this period.
  • Committed. The agreed work, with owners and target dates.
  • At risk. What is threatened, why, and the date a decision is needed by.
  • Options. Two or three tradeoffs, each stating plainly what drops.
  • Assumptions. PTO, rotation coverage, reserve, and anything you estimated.

Put the decision you want in the first block, not the last. If the report reaches an executive who reads only the opening line, that line should still be actionable. Keep roles and workstreams in the committed list rather than individual names, and keep individual utilization out entirely; the reasons it corrupts a planning document are the same reasons we set out in why productivity metrics fail knowledge teams.

How to make capacity reporting repeatable

Credibility comes from sending the same report, in the same format, on the same date, whether the news is good or bad. A capacity report that only appears when you want something reads as lobbying. One that arrives every month becomes the reference document people quote back to you, which is exactly the position worth holding.

Keep an archive and close the loop. When last quarter's at-risk item slipped for the reason you stated, note it in one line in the next report. Do not gloat and do not bury it. Two or three rounds of that and your forecasts start being treated as facts, which is worth more than any single argument you could win in a meeting.

A word on padding. Parkinson's Law, the observation that work expands to fill the time available, is the standard objection to generous capacity estimates and it has some force. Our position is that the answer is transparency rather than secret buffers: show the reserve as an explicit line, explain what it absorbed last period, and let it be challenged. Hidden padding is the thing that destroys trust the moment somebody finds it.

Who should skip this approach? If your organization plans continuously, with priorities reset weekly and a shared queue everyone can see, a formal monthly capacity report adds ceremony you do not need. Report the queue instead. And if you have never measured where the hours actually go, do that before you build a model on top of guesses; a one-week productivity audit will give you the deduction figures this report depends on.

Key takeaways

  • Separate headcount, available capacity, committed capacity, reserve, and free capacity. Only the last one is spendable.
  • Report in days or person-weeks. Percentages above one hundred read as complaints and get discounted.
  • Always attach two or three options showing what gets delivered and what drops.
  • Keep the report to one page with the decision you want in the opening line.
  • Send it on a fixed schedule and close the loop on previous forecasts.

Frequently asked questions

What is team capacity?

Team capacity is the amount of work a team can realistically take on in a defined period, after removing PTO, company holidays, meetings, support duties, and other standing commitments. It is a planning figure rather than a headcount, and it changes every period as people and obligations change.

How is capacity different from headcount?

Headcount counts people. Capacity counts the working time those people can actually give to planned work. Two teams of six can have very different capacity if one carries an on-call rotation, a support queue, and two people split across another project. Leadership routinely conflates the two, and correcting that is most of the job.

What is committed capacity?

Committed capacity is the portion of available capacity already promised to agreed work. It is the number that tells a leader what a new request would displace. Without it, every new request looks free, because there is nothing on the page showing what already occupies the time.

What proportion of time should I plan as available?

Do not borrow a rule of thumb. Measure your own team for one or two periods: record how much time went to planned work versus interruptions, support, meetings, and admin. Use that observed ratio and revise it each quarter as duties change. A borrowed percentage is a guess wearing a suit.

Why does leadership ignore it when I say we are at 110 percent?

Because a percentage above one hundred describes a feeling, not a decision. It also fails an obvious test: if the team were truly over capacity, something would already be late. Executives cannot act on distress. They can act on a list showing what fits this period, what does not, and what would have to move for a new request to be accepted.

Should I report capacity in percentages or in days?

Days or person-weeks. Those units map directly onto scope, so a leader can picture what fifteen person-days buys and can compare it against the thing they want. Nobody can picture 82 percent, and any figure over 100 percent reads as an argument rather than a measurement.

How often should I send a capacity report?

Match the cadence of the planning rhythm around you: monthly for most teams, every two weeks if priorities shift constantly. Send it on the same date every period, even when nothing has changed. Predictability is what turns the report into a reference point instead of an alarm.

Should the report name individual people?

Name roles and workstreams, not individuals, unless one person is a genuine single point of failure worth escalating. Individual utilization figures invite comparison between colleagues and turn a planning document into a performance document, which is not what a capacity report is for.

How do I account for unplanned work?

Reserve a named block for it, sized by what your team actually absorbed in recent periods, and show that block on the report. Unplanned work that is invisible in the plan gets paid for with overtime and evening hours. A visible reserve makes it a budgeted cost leadership can see and challenge.

What do I do if leadership rejects the tradeoff?

Record the decision in writing and move on. Note what was added, what you flagged as at risk, and who chose to proceed. Do not argue it further in the meeting. When the risk materializes, the record makes the argument for you, and it does so without assigning blame to anyone.
