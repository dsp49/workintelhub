How to Clock In Remotely: Mobile Time Clocks Explained

P Pavan
November 26, 2026 · 7 min read

To clock in remotely, an employee opens a time clock app on a phone, a web browser or a shared tablet at the job site and taps clock in. The app records the time and, depending on how the employer has set it up, a GPS location, whether the phone is inside a geofence around the site, the network it came from, or a photo. Those checks are what make a remote punch trustworthy enough to pay from.

This page explains the five common ways to clock in away from a wall-mounted time clock, what each verifies, the federal wage rules that apply when people work where the employer cannot see them, and a setup checklist. It is written for employers choosing a method and for employees who have been asked to install one.

The short answer

Field and multi-site crews: a mobile app with a GPS stamp, or a geofence if punches must happen on site.

Home-based staff: a web or desktop clock, with IP restriction only if they must work from a known network.

Shared job sites: a kiosk tablet with a PIN or photo, so nobody needs a personal phone.

How a remote punch is verified

A remote punch answers three questions: when, where and who. The time is the easy part; the app records it on the server. The other two are where methods differ.

  • Where. GPS stamps record a location at the moment of the punch. Geofences go further and check it against the site. Indoors and in dense cities, GPS can be off by a block, so a geofence needs some margin. IP checks confirm the network, not the person's location.
  • Who. A PIN proves someone knew the PIN. A photo taken at the punch lets a manager check it later. Face recognition matches the face automatically, but it collects a biometric identifier, which in Illinois, Texas and Washington triggers notice and consent rules; our biometric time clock entry explains them.
  • Edits. An audit trail showing who changed a punch, and when, matters more than any single check. It is what lets payroll trust the record.

These checks are also the main defense against one employee punching for another. Our page on buddy punching sets out which controls stop it and what they cost in goodwill.

The wage rules for remote hours

Moving the clock off premises does not change what counts as work. Three federal rules matter for non-exempt employees:

  1. Work away from the site is work. 29 CFR 785.12 says the hours-worked rule applies to work done away from the premises, "or even at home": if the employer knows or has reason to believe the work is being done, it must be counted.
  2. A reporting procedure protects both sides. In Field Assistance Bulletin 2020-5 (August 24, 2020), the Wage and Hour Division said one way to exercise reasonable diligence is to provide a reasonable reporting procedure for unscheduled time and pay for all reported hours. It does not count as diligence if the employer prevents or discourages accurate reporting.
  3. Short breaks stay paid. Under 29 CFR 785.18, rest breaks of 5 to about 20 minutes are hours worked wherever the employee is. A remote clock that makes staff punch out for a coffee break should treat that time as paid.

In practice that means the remote clock must let people record time they actually worked, including a forgotten punch or work after a shift, through an edit request that a manager approves rather than a lockout. State meal and rest break rules follow the employee's work location; our meal and rest break laws by state table lists them. If the app rounds punches, read our page on time clock rounding rules first. Records must still be kept as the FLSA requires: hours each workday and each workweek, with time cards kept two years and payroll records three.

Personal phones, location and notice

Most remote clock-in runs on employees' own phones, which raises three questions.

Location while off the clock. Configure the app to capture location only at the punch or only while clocked in, and say so in writing. Continuous tracking of a personal phone after hours is hard to justify and easy to resent. Our employee GPS tracking guide and GPS tracking policy template cover the consent and notice rules, and New York, Connecticut and Delaware have electronic monitoring notice laws.

Cost of the phone. In California, the Court of Appeal held in Cochran v. Schwan's Home Service (2014) that when employees must use personal cell phones for work calls, Labor Code 2802 requires the employer to reimburse a reasonable percentage of the bill. Requiring a personal phone for clocking in raises the same question; confirm the position for your state.

A choice for those who refuse. Offering a kiosk or a company device for employees who do not want the app on their own phone removes most objections. Our page on whether an employer can monitor a personal phone covers the employee side.

How to set up remote clock-in

  1. Pick the method by crew, not by company: GPS for drivers and field crews, geofences for fixed sites, a web clock for home-based staff, a kiosk where phones are a problem.
  2. Turn on only the checks you need. A GPS stamp at the punch is enough for most teams. Add photos if buddy punching is a real problem, and face recognition only with written consent.
  3. Allow offline punches for sites without signal, synced with the original time when the phone reconnects.
  4. Set up an edit-request flow so missed punches are fixed with a reason and a manager's approval, and keep the audit trail.
  5. Put break rules in the app for each state where staff work, and do not auto-deduct meal breaks people may not have taken.
  6. Write it down. Tell staff what is recorded, when, and who can see it, before the first punch.
  7. Run one pay period in parallel with the old method and compare hours before switching.

Key takeaways

  • Remote clock-in uses a phone app, web clock, desktop timer or site kiosk, verified by GPS, geofence, IP address, PIN or photo.
  • GPS stamps record where a punch happened; geofences check it against a site; IP checks confirm only the network.
  • Under 29 CFR 785.12, work at home or away from the site is hours worked if the employer knows or has reason to believe it is done.
  • DOL's Field Assistance Bulletin 2020-5 says a reasonable reporting procedure for unscheduled time, with all reported hours paid, is one way to show diligence.
  • Capture location only at the punch or while clocked in, offer a kiosk or company device to staff who refuse the app, and check phone reimbursement rules.

Frequently asked questions

How do I clock in remotely?

Open your employer's time clock app on your phone or in a browser and tap clock in. Depending on the setup, the app may record your location, require you to be inside a geofence around the site, or take a photo. If a punch is missed, submit an edit request with the correct time.

Can my employer see my location when I clock in from my phone?

If GPS is switched on, yes, at the moment of the punch and, depending on settings, while you are clocked in. A well-configured app does not track location when you are off the clock. Ask your employer what is recorded and when.

Do remote employees have to clock in?

Non-exempt employees must have their hours recorded under the FLSA wherever they work, so most employers ask them to clock in. Exempt employees usually do not need to, though some employers track their time for projects.

What is a geofence time clock?

A mobile time clock that allows or flags punches based on whether the phone is inside a virtual boundary drawn around a work site. It checks that the employee was at the site, not just where they were.

Do I have to use my personal phone to clock in?

Practices vary. Many employers offer a kiosk or company device as an alternative. In California, an employer that requires personal phone use for work may owe reimbursement of a reasonable part of the bill under Labor Code 2802.

Are remote employees paid for short breaks?

Yes. Under 29 CFR 785.18, rest breaks of about 5 to 20 minutes count as hours worked whether the employee is on site or at home.
