Do You Have to Track Hours for Salaried Employees?

P Pavan
November 26, 2026 · 7 min read

You have to track hours for salaried employees who are non-exempt, but not for salaried employees who are exempt. Under the federal recordkeeping rules in 29 CFR 516.2, employers must record the hours worked each workday and the total hours worked each workweek for every employee covered by the minimum wage and overtime rules. 29 CFR 516.3 drops that requirement for bona fide executive, administrative, professional and outside sales employees.

The catch is that a salary does not make someone exempt. Exemption needs a salary basis, a salary at or above the threshold, and duties that qualify, all three. A salaried employee who fails any test is non-exempt, owed overtime, and needs an hours record. This page covers what the regulation requires for each group, how long to keep it, and when tracking exempt staff is worth doing anyway. It is general information, not legal advice; state law can add to it.

The short answer

Salaried non-exempt: yes. Record hours worked each workday and each workweek, and pay overtime beyond 40 hours in a workweek.

Salaried exempt: not under federal law. You must keep identifying details and the basis of pay, but not hours worked.

Check the classification first. Salary is a method of payment, not an exemption, and misclassifying a salaried employee as exempt is the arrangement employers most often get wrong.

Salaried is not the same as exempt

The Fair Labor Standards Act's white collar exemptions are set out in 29 CFR Part 541. An employee is exempt only if all three tests are met:

TestWhat it asksCommon failure
Salary basisIs the employee paid a fixed, predetermined amount that does not vary with the quantity or quality of work?Docking pay for partial days
Salary levelDoes the salary meet the threshold: $684 a week ($35,568 a year) federally, higher in several states?Using an out-of-date or wrong-state figure
DutiesIs the primary duty executive, administrative or professional as the regulation defines it?Judging by job title rather than the work

A salaried receptionist, a salaried junior analyst doing routine work under close supervision, or a "manager" who spends the shift doing the same work as the team is non-exempt and must have hours recorded. Exempt status cannot be agreed between employer and employee, and overtime rights cannot be waived. Our exempt vs non-exempt entry covers the duties tests and why pages quoting $43,888 or $58,656 are out of date, and salary vs hourly sets out the 2026 state thresholds, including California's $1,352 a week.

What 29 CFR Part 516 requires for each group

Part 516 lists the items employers must keep. 29 CFR 516.3 applies all of the general list in 29 CFR 516.2(a) to exempt white collar and outside sales employees except paragraphs (a)(6) through (a)(10), which are the rate, hours and overtime items, and adds the basis of pay.

For this purpose a workday is any fixed period of 24 consecutive hours, and a workweek is a fixed and regularly recurring period of seven consecutive workdays. Overtime is owed by workweek, so a salaried non-exempt employee who works 46 hours one week and 34 the next is owed six hours of overtime, not an average.

Salaried non-exempt staff on fixed schedules

29 CFR 516.2(c) offers a simpler option for employees on fixed schedules. Instead of recording hours each day, an employer may keep the schedule of daily and weekly hours the employee normally works, mark each week in which the schedule was actually worked, and record the exact hours each day and week in any week the employee worked more or less than the schedule.

That is exception reporting, and it only works if the exceptions are actually captured. A salaried non-exempt coordinator who stays late twice a week without anyone noting it has a schedule on file and an overtime liability off it. If the schedule is fixed but the work is not, record the hours.

Form, retention and where to keep the records

  • No particular form. 29 CFR 516.1 prescribes no order or form of records, only that the required information is kept. A spreadsheet, a paper timesheet or time tracking software all qualify.
  • Three years for payroll records. 29 CFR 516.5 requires payroll records containing the Part 516 information to be kept for at least three years from the last date of entry.
  • Two years for time cards. 29 CFR 516.6 requires the basic time and earnings cards or sheets showing daily starting and stopping times to be kept for at least two years.
  • Accessible on request. 29 CFR 516.7 requires records to be kept safe and accessible at the place of employment or a central recordkeeping office, and made available within 72 hours of a request from the Wage and Hour Division.
  • State law on top. Part 516 does not excuse compliance with any other federal, state or local recordkeeping rule, and several states require more, such as meal and rest break records.

When to track exempt employees' time anyway

Federal law does not require hours for exempt staff, but many employers record time for them for other reasons. The common ones:

  • Client billing and project costing. Consultants, lawyers and agency staff are often exempt and still bill by the hour. The billable hours calculator covers the arithmetic.
  • Evidence for the classification. A record of what the person actually does can support, or warn you about, the duties test. If an "exempt" manager's time is mostly spent on the team's own work, the exemption is fragile.
  • Workload and leave. Hours data shows sustained overwork and tracks paid time off.
  • State rules. Some states set stricter tests or higher thresholds, so a role exempt federally can be non-exempt locally.

Do not use exempt hours records to dock pay. Under the salary basis test, an exempt employee is paid a fixed amount that does not vary with the quantity of work, and deducting pay for partial days destroys the salary basis. Record exempt time for billing, planning or evidence, not to cut a salary for a short day.

Paying overtime to salaried non-exempt employees

A salaried non-exempt employee is owed at least one and a half times the regular rate for hours over 40 in a workweek, with the rate derived from the salary. The salary to hourly calculator converts the salary, the time and a half calculator handles the premium, and the overtime calculator covers a full week. Some states add daily overtime: per the California Division of Labor Standards Enforcement, California pays time and a half beyond eight hours in a workday and double time beyond twelve. If clock times are rounded, 29 CFR 785.48 requires the rounding to be neutral over time. Private employers cannot give time off instead of overtime pay; comp time is for public agencies only.

Key takeaways

  • Salaried non-exempt employees must have hours worked recorded each workday and each workweek under 29 CFR 516.2(a)(7).
  • Exempt executive, administrative, professional and outside sales employees do not need an hours record under 29 CFR 516.3, but the basis of pay must be kept.
  • Salary is a method of payment, not an exemption: the salary basis, salary level ($684 a week federally) and duties tests must all be met.
  • Employees on fixed schedules can be recorded by schedule plus exceptions under 29 CFR 516.2(c), if the exceptions are actually captured.
  • Keep payroll records at least three years and time cards at least two, and produce them within 72 hours of a request.
  • Tracking exempt staff is lawful and often useful, but do not use the record to dock pay for partial days.

Frequently asked questions

Do salaried employees have to clock in and out?

Non-exempt salaried employees need their hours worked recorded each workday and each workweek, which in practice usually means clocking in and out or a timesheet. Exempt salaried employees have no federal hours-recording requirement, though an employer may choose to track their time.

What records must I keep for exempt employees?

Under 29 CFR 516.3: name, identifying number, address, date of birth if under 19, sex and occupation, when the workweek begins, total wages each pay period, date of payment and pay period, and the basis of pay in enough detail to calculate total remuneration including fringe benefits. Hours worked are not required.

Can a salaried employee be non-exempt?

Yes. A salaried employee who is paid below the threshold, or whose duties do not meet an exemption, is non-exempt. They are owed overtime at one and a half times the regular rate derived from the salary for hours over 40 in a workweek, and their hours must be recorded.

How long do I have to keep time records?

Under 29 CFR 516.5, payroll records must be kept for at least three years. Under 29 CFR 516.6, time cards and sheets showing daily start and stop times must be kept for at least two years. State law may require longer.

Can I track an exempt employee's hours?

Yes. Federal rules set the minimum records to keep, not a ban on more. Employers track exempt staff for client billing, project costing and workload. Do not use the record to deduct pay for partial days, which undermines the salary basis an exemption depends on.

Is there a required format for time records?

No. 29 CFR 516.1 prescribes no particular order or form, only that the required information is kept. Paper timesheets, spreadsheets and time tracking software all qualify, provided the records are accurate and available for inspection.
