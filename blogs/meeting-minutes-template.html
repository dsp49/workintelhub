Meeting Minutes Template: Formats, Examples and Legal Rules

P Pavan
November 13, 2026 · 10 min read

Minutes are the record of what a meeting decided, not a transcript of what was said. Robert's Rules of Order puts it in one sentence: minutes should contain mainly a record of what was done at the meeting, not what was said by the members. That principle keeps minutes short, makes them useful a year later, and, for boards and public bodies, keeps them legally safe. The rest of the work is format: a consistent header, the decisions with their votes, the action items with owners and dates, and an approval at the next meeting.

This page gives four templates you can copy (board, team, project and one-on-one), a minutes builder that turns your notes into the standard format, the legal requirements for corporations, nonprofits and public bodies, what to do about AI note-takers, how long to keep minutes, and examples of good and bad entries. What minutes must contain

For any meeting, minutes should record:

  1. Header: name of the body or team, type of meeting (regular, special, annual), date, time, place or platform.
  2. Attendance: who chaired, who took minutes, who was present and absent, and, for boards, whether a quorum was present.
  3. Approval of prior minutes: approved as circulated or as corrected.
  4. Decisions: each motion or decision in the exact words adopted, with who moved it (for formal bodies), the result and, where required, the vote count.
  5. Action items: what, who, by when.
  6. Reports received: noted by title and presenter, with the report attached rather than summarized.
  7. Next meeting and adjournment time.

Robert's Rules of Order Newly Revised (12th edition, section 48) adds what to leave out: the minute-taker's opinions, summaries of debate, and who said what, except where the body directs otherwise. A guest speaker is noted by name and topic only. The principle is that minutes record actions; discussion belongs in a separate summary if the body wants one.

Four templates

1. Board of directors meeting

Header[Company] Board of Directors, regular meeting, [date], [time], [location or video platform]
PresentDirectors [names]; absent [names]; also present [officers, counsel, guests]. Quorum present.
Call to orderThe Chair called the meeting to order at [time]. [Name] acted as secretary.
Prior minutesThe minutes of [date] were approved [as circulated / as corrected].
ResolutionsUpon motion duly made and seconded, the Board RESOLVED that [exact text]. Vote: [for / against / abstain], or approved unanimously.
ReportsThe CFO presented the Q3 financial report (attached as Exhibit A).
Executive sessionThe Board met in executive session from [time] to [time]; no action was taken.
AdjournmentThere being no further business, the meeting adjourned at [time].
Signature[Secretary], Secretary. Approved [date].

2. Team meeting: header; attendees; decisions (numbered); action items (task, owner, due date); open issues carried forward; next meeting. Five to ten lines is normal.

3. Project meeting: header; status by workstream (on track, at risk, off track, one line each); decisions; risks and issues raised with owners; action items; changes to scope, schedule or budget approved. Link to the status report rather than repeating it.

4. One-on-one: date; topics discussed (one line each); commitments by the manager and by the employee; follow-ups for next time. Share the notes with the employee after each meeting; see the one-on-one meeting template.

Legal requirements

  • Corporations. State corporate law generally requires a corporation to keep minutes of shareholder and board meetings among its books and records. Delaware's General Corporation Law requires written consents in lieu of meetings to be filed with the minutes (section 141(f)), permits records to be kept electronically if they can be converted to paper in a reasonable time (section 224), and lets stockholders inspect board minutes for a proper purpose (section 220). Minutes are the main evidence that directors were informed and deliberated, which matters for the business judgment rule, and a principal source in litigation over board decisions.
  • Nonprofits. IRS Form 990, Part VI, line 8 asks whether the organization contemporaneously documented the meetings of its governing body and committees; the instructions treat documentation as contemporaneous if made by the later of the next meeting or 60 days afterward. State nonprofit corporation acts require minutes as a corporate record.
  • LLCs. Generally no statutory requirement; the operating agreement governs. Keeping minutes of major decisions still helps preserve limited liability.
  • Public bodies. State open-meeting laws (California's Brown Act, New York's Open Meetings Law, Texas's Open Meetings Act) require public access and, in most states, minutes available to the public within a set period; closed-session minutes are typically confidential. Check the state statute.
  • Employment matters. Minutes of meetings that discuss discipline, layoffs or complaints may be discoverable in litigation. Record decisions and the reasons in neutral terms; do not record speculation about individuals.

AI note-takers and transcripts

AI note-takers have become common: a 2025 vendor survey by Fellow found 75% of respondents used one, and 84% said people change what they say when one is present; a July 2026 survey found a third of US workers had had an AI note-taker in a meeting and two-thirds were not consistently asked first. Three cautions:

  1. Consent. Recording a meeting is governed by wiretap law. About a dozen states require all participants' consent; a note-taker that joins and records without notice can create liability, and class actions over AI meeting recording have been filed in California. See AI notetaker recording consent and recording laws by state.
  2. A transcript is not minutes. An hour of conversation produces about 16 pages of transcript. Minutes should still be a short record of decisions and actions, reviewed by a person; AI summaries routinely misattribute statements and miss decisions made by implication.
  3. Retention and privilege. Transcripts are discoverable records. Board and legal meetings should not be recorded by default; if they are, retention should be short and the transcript should not replace approved minutes.

Approval, distribution and retention

  • Draft within 48 hours, while memory is fresh, and circulate to attendees for corrections.
  • Approve at the next meeting (formal bodies) or treat as final if no corrections within a set time (teams). Corrections are made by amending the draft, not by recording the correction separately.
  • Distribute action items to owners immediately, separately from the full minutes if needed.
  • Retain: board, shareholder and committee minutes of corporations and nonprofits are kept permanently under standard records schedules; team and project minutes for the life of the project plus a reasonable period. Follow your records retention schedule and suspend destruction under any litigation hold.

Tips for the minute-taker

  1. Use the agenda as the skeleton; one heading per agenda item.
  2. Capture motions and decisions verbatim; ask the chair to restate a decision if it is unclear.
  3. Write action items in the room, with an owner and date, and read them back at the end.
  4. Record facts, not characterizations: "carried 5 to 2," not "after heated debate."
  5. Note arrivals and departures that affect quorum.
  6. Attach reports instead of summarizing them.
  7. Use a consistent template so readers can find decisions quickly across meetings.

Key takeaways

  • Minutes record what was done, not what was said: Robert's Rules says decisions, motions and actions belong in minutes, while opinions and debate summaries do not.
  • Every set of minutes needs a header, attendance and quorum, approval of prior minutes, decisions in the words adopted with results, action items with owners and dates, reports attached, and the next meeting.
  • Use different formats for board, team, project and one-on-one meetings; team minutes of five to ten lines are normal.
  • Corporations must keep board and shareholder minutes as corporate records (Delaware requires written consents filed with them and lets stockholders inspect them); nonprofits report on Form 990 whether meetings were documented within the later of the next meeting or 60 days; public bodies follow open-meeting laws.
  • AI note-takers raise consent issues in all-party states and produce transcripts, not minutes; a person should still write and approve a short record of decisions.
  • Draft within 48 hours, approve at the next meeting, send action items immediately, and keep board minutes permanently.

Frequently asked questions

What should be included in meeting minutes?

The name and type of meeting, date, time and place; who chaired, who took minutes, who attended and whether a quorum was present; approval of prior minutes; each decision or motion as adopted, with the result; action items with an owner and due date; reports received; and the next meeting date and adjournment time.

What should not be included in meeting minutes?

Verbatim discussion, who said what, the minute-taker's opinions, characterizations such as 'heated debate,' and speculation about individuals. Robert's Rules says minutes should record mainly what was done at the meeting, not what was said. Guest speakers are noted by name and topic only.

How do you write meeting minutes quickly?

Use the agenda as the outline, write decisions and action items in the room as they happen, read the action items back before the meeting ends, and send the draft within 48 hours. A template with fixed headings (attendance, decisions, actions, next meeting) halves the writing time.

Are meeting minutes legally required?

For corporations, yes: state corporate laws treat board and shareholder minutes as required corporate records, and Delaware lets stockholders inspect them. Nonprofits must say on Form 990 whether board and committee meetings were contemporaneously documented. Public bodies must keep minutes under open-meeting laws. Ordinary team meetings have no legal requirement.

How long should you keep meeting minutes?

Board, shareholder and committee minutes of corporations and nonprofits are normally kept permanently. Project and team minutes are kept for the project's life plus a period set by your records retention schedule. Any minutes relevant to threatened litigation must be preserved under a legal hold.

Can I use an AI note-taker for meeting minutes?

You can use one to draft, with care. Recording requires consent, and about a dozen states require all participants to consent. AI transcripts are long and summaries can misattribute statements, so a person should review and approve short minutes of decisions and actions. Avoid recording board and legal meetings by default.

Who approves meeting minutes?

For formal bodies, the members approve the minutes at the next meeting, with corrections made by amending the draft. The secretary then signs them. For informal team meetings, minutes are usually treated as final if no one corrects the draft within a set time.

What is the difference between minutes and a transcript?

A transcript records every word; minutes record decisions, actions and essential facts. An hour-long meeting produces roughly 16 pages of transcript but usually under a page of minutes. Minutes are the official record; transcripts are raw material and a discoverable record of their own.
