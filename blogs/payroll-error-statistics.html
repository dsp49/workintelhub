Payroll Error Statistics (2026)

P Pavan
October 16, 2026 · 12 min read

One in five US payrolls contains an error, and fixing each one costs about $291, according to an EY survey of 508 payroll practitioners at mid-sized and large companies. The IRS assessed 4.46 million civil penalties on employment tax returns in fiscal 2025. The Department of Labor recovered $259 million in back wages for 176,957 workers, the most since 2019. And 72% to 78% of US workers say a one-week paycheck delay would make it hard to meet their financial obligations.

The payroll statistics that rank are built on an IRS claim that 40% of small businesses pay $845 a year in penalties, which no IRS document contains and which first appeared in a 2013 vendor interview, and on a handful of EY figures presented as 2026 findings. This page uses the EY survey with its date, Remote's three-country employee survey, IRS Data Book tables, Department of Labor and state enforcement data, the Economic Policy Institute's wage theft analysis and PayrollOrg's annual surveys, with sample and date on each, and explains why the IRS dollar figures for 2025 cannot be read at face value.

How common payroll errors are

The only US employer-side error rate with a stated sample is EY's survey of 508 payroll practitioners at US companies with 250 to 10,000 employees, published through Paycom in December 2022. It found one in five payrolls containing errors and the following annual error counts per 1,000 employees:

Error typeErrors per 1,000 employees per yearAnnual cost per 1,000 employees
Time, attendance and expenses1,139$250,000
Vacation, PTO and sick time721$220,000
Benefits503$140,000
Scheduled earnings and deductions410$135,000
W-4 and tax allocation229$135,000
Direct deposit159$45,000

Time and attendance errors happen more than once per employee per year. On the employee side, Remote's 2024 survey of 2,539 workers in the US, UK and Germany found 40% had experienced a payroll error in the past year and 53% at some point in their career; of those with an error, 43% had one and 18% had three or more. US workers were 26% more likely than UK workers to report one, and a quarter had an issue in the previous three months. Forty-two percent of errors were underpayments, 24% late payments and about 10% overpayments. Kronos's 2017 survey, the source of the "82 million US workers" figure, found 54% had experienced a paycheck problem in their career, 26% of hourly workers had been paid too little and 15% paid late; its sample size is not on the surviving page. What an error costs

EY put the average cost of fixing a payroll error at $291, and the time a 1,000-employee organization spends correcting its five most time-consuming error types at 29 forty-hour weeks a year. A missing or incorrect time punch took 26 minutes to fix. The six categories above sum to about $925,000 a year per 1,000 employees. More than 40% of organizations that cut jobs faced litigation over payroll errors and more than 50% faced compliance issues; 41% reported lower morale and 36% reputational damage. UKG and KPMG's March 2026 survey of more than 300 payroll leaders at multinationals with over 10,000 employees put "payroll leakage" at 2% to 4% of total labor spend, with 38% reporting $1 million to $5 million in annual losses. Remote found 49% of HR teams spending five or more hours a month resolving pay issues. These are self-reported figures from large employers, and no probability-sample cost estimate exists. IRS employment tax penalties

The IRS Data Book's Table 4-2 counts civil penalties by return type. In fiscal 2025, 4,457,891 penalties were assessed on employment tax returns, against 4,410,799 in 2024: 2.26 million for failure to pay, 1.22 million for late or incorrect federal tax deposits and 614,000 for late filing. The dollar figures for 2025 cannot be used as they stand. Assessments jumped from $26.8 billion in 2024 to about $1.15 trillion in 2025 while abatements rose from $23.4 billion to $1.15 trillion, with counts barely moving, which indicates a large systemic assessment and reversal rather than a change in employer behavior. The 2024 figures, $26.8 billion assessed and $23.4 billion abated, are the usable ones. Trust fund recovery penalties against individuals who failed to remit withheld taxes were $739 million in 2025, up from $387 million.

For scale, employment taxes were $1.69 trillion or 31.9% of gross IRS collections in fiscal 2025. Of 34.2 million employment tax returns filed, 3,651 were examined, under 0.05%. The IRS's tax gap estimate for 2022 puts employment taxes at $127 billion of a $696 billion gross gap. A 2017 Treasury Inspector General report found 1.4 million employers owed $45.6 billion in unpaid employment taxes, interest and penalties as of December 2015, and that enforcement against responsible individuals had fallen 38% in five years. Back wages and wage theft

The Department of Labor's Wage and Hour Division recovered $259.3 million in back wages for 176,957 workers in fiscal 2025, an average of $1,465 per worker, up from $202.7 million in 2024 and the highest since the $322.5 million peak in 2019. Civil money penalties rose to $58.7 million from $35.9 million, while concluded actions fell to 16,924 from 27,914 in 2015, so enforcement has shifted to fewer, larger cases. Under the Fair Labor Standards Act alone, $184.4 million was recovered, $146.4 million of it overtime. By industry, health care led at $53.3 million for 32,884 workers, followed by construction at $43.4 million and food services at $42.7 million across 4,088 cases.

Federal recoveries are a fraction of the total. The Economic Policy Institute's 2017 analysis of Current Population Survey data from 2013 to 2015 found 2.4 million workers in the ten most populous states were paid below the minimum wage, losing $8 billion a year or $3,300 each, 23.9% of their earned wages, which the authors extrapolated to more than $15 billion nationally. EPI's 2021 update found $3.24 billion recovered through federal, state and class-action channels between 2017 and 2020. States add their own: New York recovered more than $35 million in 2025, the most since 2015, and more than $130 million since 2021; California's Labor Commissioner recovered $78 million in 2025 and announced a $272.5 million Lyft misclassification settlement in October 2026. What a late paycheck does to employees

PayrollOrg's Getting Paid in America survey, a large self-selected online poll run each September, asks how hard it would be to meet financial obligations if a paycheck were a week late. In 2024, 77% of 36,729 respondents said somewhat or very difficult, 49% very; in 2025, 78% of 23,005; in 2026, nearly 72% of 9,541, the lowest in four years, with 40% having no emergency fund. Direct deposit covered 91.9% of respondents in 2024 and 92.7% in 2025. Remote's 2024 data put the effects in sequence: 47% of employees paid late said it caused stress or anxiety, about two thirds said it caused late bills, rent or overdrafts, 42% said their relationship with the employer deteriorated and 32% lost trust. Kronos's 2017 survey found 37% of workers had paid a bill late because of a payroll error and 42% found their pay stub's taxes and deductions confusing. What causes payroll errors

  • Time and attendance data. The largest category at 1,139 errors per 1,000 employees and the slowest to fix, per EY. Manual timesheets filled in weekly are half as accurate as daily ones, per the one survey on the subject.
  • Fragmented systems. 74% of multinationals use more than two payroll vendors and only 33% run a standardized model, per UKG and KPMG. Paychex-commissioned research found employers averaging 6.17 HR and payroll providers.
  • Manual review. An American Payroll Association survey quoted by Kronos found 40% of members with fully automated payroll in 2016.
  • Misclassification. New York identified more than a million misclassified workers in a decade; Lyft's California settlement covered drivers from 2016 to 2020.
  • Non-remittance. 1.4 million employers were delinquent on employment taxes in 2015, per the Treasury Inspector General.
  • Employees not checking. 34% do not check every paycheck and 11% would not notice an overpayment of $100 or less, per Remote.

What reduces them

The evidence is thinner here than on prevalence. Remote found 71% of HR decision-makers with outsourced payroll reporting a positive effect on accuracy. UKG and KPMG found 89% of large multinationals using automated cycle-to-cycle comparison tools, 69% tracking payroll accuracy as a metric, and 47% using AI in production payroll, with 48% of the rest citing data accuracy concerns as the reason for waiting. Given that time data is the largest error source, the practical lever is capturing it daily and exactly rather than weekly and rounded, which our timesheet automation guide covers, and reconciling before the run rather than after. Figures to stop using

  • "40% of small businesses pay an average of $845 a year in IRS penalties." First appears in a June 2013 trade-press interview with a payroll software executive, attributed to the IRS. No IRS document contains it, and IRS tables report penalty counts and dollars, not a share of businesses.
  • "33% of employers make payroll errors each year, according to the IRS." No IRS publication says so.
  • "The IRS collects $4.5 billion" or "$7 billion a year in payroll penalties." Undated. Employment tax penalties assessed were $26.8 billion in 2024 with $23.4 billion abated.
  • "77% of Americans live paycheck to paycheck, per PayrollOrg." The question was about difficulty if a paycheck were a week late.
  • "$291 per error" and "1 in 5 payrolls" as 2026 findings. One EY survey of 508 practitioners, released December 2022, fieldwork undated.
  • "15 payroll corrections per pay period" and "$922,131 a year for a 1,000-employee company." Not in the EY release; the second is a sum of its category costs.
  • "78% payroll accuracy, per ADP" and "80.15% accuracy." No primary source.
  • "82 million US workers have had a paycheck error." A 2017 extrapolation from a survey whose sample is not published.

Key takeaways

  • One in five US payrolls contains an error and fixing one costs about $291, per EY's 2022 survey of 508 practitioners. Time and attendance errors occur more than once per employee per year.
  • 40% of employees had a payroll error in the past year and 53% in their career, in a 2024 survey of 2,539 workers in three countries. 42% of errors were underpayments.
  • A 1,000-employee employer spends about 29 forty-hour weeks a year fixing its five most common error types. Large multinationals estimate payroll leakage at 2% to 4% of labor spend.
  • The IRS assessed 4.46 million civil penalties on employment tax returns in fiscal 2025. The 2025 dollar figures show a $1.15 trillion assessment almost fully abated and should not be used; 2024's were $26.8 billion assessed and $23.4 billion abated.
  • The Department of Labor recovered $259.3 million in back wages for 176,957 workers in fiscal 2025, the most since 2019, with health care, construction and food services leading.
  • Minimum wage violations alone cost 2.4 million workers in ten states $8 billion a year, about $3,300 each, per EPI's analysis of 2013 to 2015 data.
  • 72% to 78% of US workers say a one-week paycheck delay would be hard to manage, and 37% have paid a bill late because of a payroll error.
  • The '40% of small businesses pay $845 in IRS penalties' figure first appeared in a 2013 vendor interview and has no IRS source.

Frequently asked questions

How common are payroll errors?

About one in five US payrolls contains an error, per EY's survey of 508 payroll practitioners at companies with 250 to 10,000 employees, released in December 2022. Time and attendance errors were the most common at 1,139 per 1,000 employees per year, followed by PTO errors at 721 and benefits errors at 503.

How much does a payroll error cost?

About $291 to fix on average, per EY. A 1,000-employee organization spends roughly 29 forty-hour weeks a year correcting its five most time-consuming error types, and a single missing or incorrect time punch takes about 26 minutes to resolve.

What percentage of employees have been paid incorrectly?

40% of employees in a 2024 survey of 2,539 workers in the US, UK and Germany had experienced a payroll error in the past year, and 53% at some point in their career. A 2017 Kronos survey found 54% of US workers had experienced a paycheck problem.

How many employers get IRS payroll tax penalties?

The IRS assessed 4,457,891 civil penalties on employment tax returns in fiscal 2025, up from 4,410,799 in 2024, including 2.26 million for failure to pay and 1.22 million for late or incorrect federal tax deposits. IRS data count penalties, not the share of employers penalized.

How much does the Department of Labor recover in back wages each year?

$259.3 million for 176,957 workers in fiscal 2025, an average of $1,465 per worker and the highest total since 2019. $146.4 million of the Fair Labor Standards Act recoveries was unpaid overtime. Civil penalties reached $58.7 million.

Which industries have the most wage violations?

Health care led fiscal 2025 back-wage recoveries at $53.3 million for 32,884 workers, followed by construction at $43.4 million and food services at $42.7 million across 4,088 cases, per the Department of Labor.

What happens to employees when a paycheck is late?

72% to 78% of workers in PayrollOrg's 2024 to 2026 surveys said a one-week delay would make meeting financial obligations somewhat or very difficult. 47% of employees paid late reported stress or anxiety and about two thirds paid bills late or overdrew, per Remote's 2024 survey. 40% have no emergency fund.

What is the most common cause of payroll errors?

Time and attendance data. It is the largest error category at 1,139 errors per 1,000 employees per year and the slowest to fix at 26 minutes per time-punch error, per EY. Fragmented systems and manual review follow: 74% of multinationals use more than two payroll vendors.
