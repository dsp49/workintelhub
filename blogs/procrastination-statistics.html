Procrastination Statistics (2026)

P Pavan
October 16, 2026 · 11 min read

Roughly one in five adults describes themselves as a chronic procrastinator, a figure that traces to a 1996 study of 211 people at public meetings in Chicago. The best multi-country data, from 1,347 adults in six countries including the US, puts the share of "pure" chronic procrastinators at about 14%. Among university students, at least half procrastinate consistently enough to cause problems, and in a 2022 sample of 732 students, 53% met the threshold for severe procrastination.

The pages that rank for this question carry a $70 billion national cost with no source, a claim that 88% of employees procrastinate for over an hour a day from a blogger's poll, and a fourfold rise since 1978 that no study measured. This page uses the peer-reviewed prevalence studies, the 2007 meta-analysis of 691 correlations, the JAMA Network Open cohort of 3,525 students, the twin study on heritability, the 22,053-person study on income, and the intervention meta-analysis, with the sample and date next to each. Our guide on how to stop procrastinating covers the practice; this page covers the evidence.

How common it is among adults

The 20% figure that every page uses is the 1996 convenience sample. The 14% figure from the six-country study is the better estimate of chronic procrastination, and the 15% to 17% figures from later studies sit with it. "About one in seven adults, up to one in five" is the honest range. These are self-reports on validated scales, and most of the samples are outside the US, which is a gap the research has not filled.

How common it is among students

Student procrastination is two to three times as common as adult procrastination, which the research attributes to the structure of academic work: distant deadlines, aversive tasks and unsupervised time. Johansson and colleagues' 2023 paper in JAMA Network Open summarizes the literature as "at least half of university students engage in consistent and problematic procrastination." Rozental and colleagues' 2022 study of 732 Swedish students found 53% above the cut-off for severe procrastination on the Pure Procrastination Scale, 46% meeting pathological delay criteria, and 71% considering their procrastination a problem, of whom 29% had considered seeking help. A 2009 study of 784 Turkish undergraduates found 52% reporting frequent academic procrastination.

Steel, Svartdal, Thundiyil and Brothen's 2018 study tracked 171 US undergraduates across 77 time points in a semester and found work follows a hyperbolic curve toward the deadline: the highest procrastinators averaged more than nine assignments on the final day, about eleven times the busiest day of non-procrastinators. They intended to work as much as everyone else. The gap was between intention and action, and it widened the further away the deadline was.

What causes it: the research, not the folklore

Piers Steel's 2007 meta-analysis in Psychological Bulletin, covering 691 correlations, found the strongest and most consistent predictors were task aversiveness, how far away the deadline was, low self-efficacy, impulsiveness and low conscientiousness. Neuroticism, rebelliousness and sensation-seeking were only weakly related. Procrastination is a self-regulation problem, not a planning problem, and the 2018 study found attention control, energy regulation and automaticity explained 74% of the variance in it.

Gustavson, Miyake, Hewitt and Friedman's 2014 twin study in Psychological Science estimated procrastination at 46% heritable and impulsivity at 49%, with a genetic correlation of 1.0, meaning the two are genetically indistinguishable. The shared component was largely the ability to manage goals. A follow-up found procrastination's links to anxiety and to externalizing problems both ran through shared genes. Severe student procrastinators in the Rozental study differed from milder ones most on impulsivity, with an effect size of 1.36, followed by stress, depression and anxiety; 19% had a psychiatric diagnosis against 8%.

Health and wellbeing effects

The JAMA Network Open cohort followed 3,525 Swedish university students for nine months. Each standard deviation of procrastination at baseline predicted higher depression, anxiety and stress at follow-up, a 27% higher risk of disabling arm or shoulder pain, and 9% higher risk of poor sleep, 7% of inactivity, 7% of loneliness and 15% of economic difficulty. The authors call the associations weak and non-causal, and they are the best prospective evidence available. Tice and Baumeister's 1997 studies found procrastinating students were less stressed and less ill early in the semester and more stressed, more ill and lower-scoring by the end, so the short-term relief is real and the cost comes later.

Sirois and Monaghan's 2026 meta-analysis of 36 samples and 8,603 people found procrastination correlated at 0.21 with poorer self-rated health, surviving adjustment for stress. On sleep, Li and colleagues' study of 8,742 students at eleven US universities found each point on a five-point procrastination scale associated with 27% higher risk of insomnia symptoms, 32% higher risk of daytime sleepiness and about six fewer minutes of weeknight sleep. Bedtime procrastination correlated at 0.61 with insufficient sleep in a separate study. Our workplace stress statistics page covers the wider stress evidence.

Work, income and career effects

Nguyen, Steel and Ferrari's 2013 study of 22,053 adults in the International Journal of Selection and Assessment found higher procrastination associated with lower salaries, shorter employment, and higher odds of being unemployed or underemployed rather than in full-time work. Jobs demanding more self-motivation were less likely to retain procrastinators. The German representative sample found the same income pattern, and found unemployed respondents procrastinated more than employed ones. The widely quoted "$15,000 less salary per scale point" is in the body of the Nguyen paper and could not be verified for this page; "associated with lower salaries" is what the abstract supports.

Workplace prevalence data are thin. D'Abate and Eddy's 2007 study of 115 US employees found about 1 hour 20 minutes of a typical workday spent on personal business, which they costed at $8,875 per employee per year, and found it related to procrastination but not to performance, satisfaction or commitment. Salary.com's reader polls, which supply most of the "time wasted at work" figures online, are self-selected and undated in places; the 2014 poll of 750 people found 89% wasting time daily and 31% wasting about an hour. Microsoft's 2023 Work Trend Index, often cited for procrastination figures, contains none; it found 57% of time spent communicating and 68% lacking focus time. Our time management statistics page covers how the workday actually splits.

Age and gender

Procrastination falls with age in every dataset. The German sample found it highest at 14 to 29 and declining in each older band, with people in partnerships procrastinating less than singles. Steel and Ferrari's 2013 study of 16,413 English-speaking adults found procrastinators were disproportionately young, single men with less education. Men procrastinated more than women only at ages 14 to 29, with a small effect size of 0.25, and not at all in older groups; the six-country study found no sex difference in chronic procrastination. Our Gen Z workplace statistics page covers the youngest cohort at work.

What the evidence says works

Less than the self-help market suggests. Rozental and colleagues' 2018 meta-analysis of 12 studies and 718 participants found an overall effect of psychological treatment of 0.34, which the authors call small, with cognitive behavioral therapy at 0.55 across three studies. A 2025 randomized trial of guided online CBT with 403 students found an effect of 0.40 at four weeks that held at six months. Two goal-setting interventions in a 2017 study did not significantly reduce procrastination over three weeks. The implementation-intention research, covered on our time management statistics page, is the best-supported single technique. Our guide to stopping procrastination is built on what has evidence rather than what has a brand name.

Figures to stop using

  • "20% of adults are chronic procrastinators." A 1996 sample of 211 people at public meetings. The six-country study says about 14%. Cite both with dates.
  • "20% to 25%" or "a quarter." No study found 25%.
  • "80% to 95% of college students procrastinate." Steel's 2007 summary of a 1977 clinical book and a 2002 dissertation. The verifiable figures are "at least half consistently" and 46% to 53% severe.
  • "Procrastination costs the US economy $70 billion." No source exists.
  • "88% of employees procrastinate 60+ minutes a day" and "218 minutes a day." A 2019 self-selected poll on a productivity blog.
  • "Chronic procrastination rose from 5% to 25% since 1978." A misreading of a line in Steel's 2007 paper; no study measured 5% in 1978.
  • "54% of procrastinators are men." No traceable sample. The measured sex difference exists only under age 30.
  • "CBT reduces procrastination 50%" and "Pomodoro boosts completion 25%." No study. The real CBT effect size is 0.55 in three small studies.
  • "Microsoft found workers procrastinate X hours." Microsoft's data measure communication load, not procrastination.

Key takeaways

  • About 14% of adults are chronic procrastinators in the best six-country data, and about 20% in the 1996 US sample that most pages quote. One in seven to one in five is the honest range.
  • At least half of university students procrastinate consistently and problematically, and 53% of 732 students met a severe threshold in 2022. Student rates run two to three times adult rates.
  • The strongest predictors are task aversiveness, deadline distance, low self-efficacy, impulsiveness and low conscientiousness, across 691 correlations. It is a self-regulation problem, not a planning problem.
  • Procrastination is about 46% heritable and genetically indistinguishable from impulsivity in a twin study.
  • Each standard deviation of procrastination predicted higher depression, anxiety and stress nine months later in 3,525 students, plus 27% higher risk of disabling arm pain. The authors call the effects weak.
  • Higher procrastination is associated with lower salaries, shorter tenure and higher odds of unemployment across 22,053 adults.
  • Men procrastinate more than women only under age 30, with a small effect, and procrastination declines in every older age band.
  • Psychological treatments have a small overall effect of 0.34, with CBT at 0.55. The '$70 billion cost' and '88% of employees' figures have no source.

Frequently asked questions

What percentage of adults are chronic procrastinators?

About 14% in the best multi-country data: 13.5% of men and 14.6% of women across 1,347 adults in six countries including the US, in a 2007 study. The widely quoted 20% comes from a 1996 study of 211 US adults at public meetings. Later studies in other countries found 15% to 17%.

What percentage of college students procrastinate?

At least half procrastinate consistently and problematically, per the summary in a 2023 JAMA Network Open paper. In a 2022 study of 732 university students, 53% scored above the severe threshold on the Pure Procrastination Scale and 71% considered their procrastination a problem.

Is procrastination linked to depression and anxiety?

Yes, prospectively. In a cohort of 3,525 university students, each standard deviation of procrastination at baseline predicted higher depression, anxiety and stress nine months later. The authors describe the associations as weak and not proof of cause.

Does procrastination affect physical health?

Modestly. A 2026 meta-analysis of 36 samples and 8,603 people found a correlation of 0.21 with poorer self-rated health. Students with higher procrastination had 27% higher risk of disabling arm or shoulder pain and worse sleep, and a 1997 study found procrastinators were sicker overall by the end of a semester.

Does procrastination cost you money?

It is associated with lower income. Across 22,053 adults, higher procrastination went with lower salaries, shorter employment and higher odds of unemployment or underemployment. A representative German sample found the same pattern.

Is procrastination genetic?

Partly. A twin study estimated procrastination at 46% heritable and found it genetically indistinguishable from impulsivity, with a genetic correlation of 1.0. The shared component was largely goal-management ability.

Do men procrastinate more than women?

Only when young. In a representative sample of 2,527 Germans, men procrastinated more than women at ages 14 to 29 with a small effect size of 0.25, and there was no difference in older groups. The six-country study found no sex difference in chronic procrastination.

Does procrastination ruin sleep?

It is associated with worse sleep. Among 8,742 students at eleven US universities, each point on a five-point procrastination scale meant 27% higher risk of insomnia symptoms and 32% higher risk of daytime sleepiness. Bedtime procrastination correlated at 0.61 with feeling under-slept.
