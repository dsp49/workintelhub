Mandatory Overtime Laws by State: Can You Be Forced to Work?

P Pavan
November 12, 2026 · 13 min read

Under federal law an employer can require an adult employee to work overtime, as much of it as the business needs, and can discipline or dismiss someone who refuses. The Fair Labor Standards Act sets no limit on hours for workers aged 16 and over; it only requires that non-exempt employees be paid time and a half for hours over 40 in a workweek. The limits that do exist come from state law and are narrower than most people expect: a ban on forcing nurses to stay past their shifts in 18 states, a general cap in Maine, a factory cap in Oregon, day-of-rest laws in a few states, and protections for minors everywhere.

This guide explains the federal rule and its exceptions (religious accommodation, disability, family leave, union contracts), the state statutes with citations, a 51-jurisdiction table, what the predictive scheduling laws add, and what an employee can do when told to stay. For how overtime is paid rather than whether it can be required, see overtime laws by state and the overtime calculator.

The federal rule: no cap, but pay

The Fair Labor Standards Act (29 U.S.C. 207) requires overtime pay at one and one-half times the regular rate for hours over 40 in a workweek for non-exempt employees. It says nothing about how many hours an employer may schedule, how much notice it must give, or whether an employee may decline. The Department of Labor's own summary is blunt: the Act "does not limit the number of hours in a day or days in a week an employee may be required or scheduled to work, including overtime hours, if the employee is at least 16 years old." Exempt salaried employees have neither a cap nor overtime pay.

Hours worked beyond the schedule must be recorded and paid, including work the employer "suffers or permits" without asking for it. Refusing to pay for required overtime, or pressuring employees to work off the clock, is the violation, not the requirement to work. The Wage and Hour Division recovers tens of millions of dollars a year in overtime back wages on exactly that basis; see overtime statistics.

Can you be fired for refusing overtime?

In an at-will state, generally yes. Refusing a lawful instruction to work is insubordination, and nothing in federal law makes it a protected act. Montana, the only state without at-will employment for established workers, requires good cause, and refusing required overtime without a protected reason would usually qualify. Union contracts almost always regulate overtime (rotation, seniority, caps, the right to refuse after a threshold), and a unionized employee's rights come from the agreement rather than the statute. Employment contracts that fix hours are enforceable on their terms.

The exceptions in the next section are the only ones that make a refusal protected. Everything else, including childcare, a second job, a class schedule or exhaustion, is a matter for the employer's discretion and the policy.

Exceptions: religion, disability, FMLA, unions

  • Religious accommodation. Title VII requires an employer to accommodate an employee's religious observance (a Sabbath, a holy day) unless doing so imposes a substantial increased cost on the business, the standard the Supreme Court set in Groff v. DeJoy (2023). Mandatory Saturday overtime for an employee whose faith forbids Saturday work must be accommodated if shift swaps or volunteers are available.
  • Disability. The Americans with Disabilities Act requires reasonable accommodation, which can include a limit on hours when a physician supports it, unless overtime is an essential function of the job or the accommodation is an undue hardship. Document the interactive process.
  • Family and medical leave. Under FMLA regulations (29 CFR 825.205), an employee on intermittent or reduced-schedule leave cannot be required to work the hours covered by the leave; mandatory overtime that the employee cannot work because of the certified condition counts against the FMLA entitlement rather than as a refusal.
  • Pregnancy. The Pregnant Workers Fairness Act (2023) requires accommodation of known limitations related to pregnancy, which the EEOC's regulations say can include schedule changes and limits on overtime.
  • Military service. USERRA protects time for drills and training.
  • Safety. OSHA's general duty clause does not cap hours, but an employee who refuses work because of a reasonable, good-faith belief in an imminent danger has limited protection under 29 CFR 1977.12; extreme fatigue alone has rarely qualified.
  • Collective bargaining. Overtime distribution and mandatory overtime are mandatory subjects of bargaining; unilateral changes in a union workplace are unfair labor practices.
  • Protected complaints. An employee fired after complaining about unpaid overtime is protected by the FLSA's anti-retaliation provision (29 U.S.C. 215(a)(3)), which applies to the complaint, not the refusal.

Nurse mandatory overtime bans in 18 states

Eighteen states restrict mandatory overtime for nurses, most of them enacted between 2000 and 2015 in response to patient-safety research linking shifts over 12 hours to error rates: Alaska, California, Connecticut, Illinois, Maine, Maryland, Massachusetts, Minnesota, Missouri, New Hampshire, New Jersey, New York, Oregon, Pennsylvania, Rhode Island, Texas, Washington and West Virginia. The models differ:

  • No work beyond the scheduled shift (New York Labor Law 167; Pennsylvania Act 102 of 2008; New Jersey): a nurse cannot be required to stay beyond the agreed hours except in a declared disaster, an unforeseen emergency after good-faith efforts to find other staff, or to complete an ongoing procedure. New York adds penalties of $1,000, $2,000 and $3,000 for successive violations and a reporting requirement when a hospital invokes the exception 15 or more days in a month. Pennsylvania adds 10 hours off after 12 consecutive hours worked.
  • A daily cap (California: 12 hours in 24 under Title 22 and Wage Order 5, except emergencies).
  • Written policies and committees (Texas Health and Safety Code chapter 258; Washington RCW 49.28.130 to 150, broadened in 2019 to cover more facilities and prohibit the use of prescheduled on-call to fill chronic gaps).

Most of these statutes cover registered and licensed practical nurses in direct patient care at hospitals and some other facilities; several exclude nursing homes, home care or public hospitals. None cap voluntary overtime, which is why the federal Nurse Overtime and Patient Safety Act has been reintroduced repeatedly without passing. Vermont's legislature considered a ban in 2026 (S.277), New York considered a consecutive-hours cap (A7702, 2025), and Texas added staffing protections in 2025. Check the current text before relying on a row in the table; the citations are to the statutes as summarized by nursing associations and the Wage and Hour bar in November 2026.

General caps: Maine and Oregon

Maine has the only general cap on mandatory overtime for adults: 26 MRSA 603 provides that no employer may require an employee to work more than 80 hours of overtime in any consecutive two-week period. Exceptions cover emergencies, essential services, seasonal employers operating fewer than 26 weeks a year, salaried executives and employees under collective agreements that address the issue. The cap is generous (it permits 120-hour weeks on average) but it exists, and Maine also limits nurses separately.

Oregon limits manufacturing employees to 10 hours a day and 55 hours a week under ORS 652.020, as rewritten by HB 3458 in 2017, with an employee able to consent in writing to up to 60 hours, and a $2,000 civil penalty for coercing consent; an undue-hardship procedure allows temporary higher limits in some circumstances. Oregon also has the only statewide predictive scheduling law, covering retail, hospitality and food service employers with 500 or more employees worldwide.

Daily overtime laws in California (after 8 hours), Alaska (after 8), Nevada (after 8 for lower-paid workers) and Colorado (after 12) do not cap hours but make long days expensive, and California adds double time after 12 hours in a day and after 8 on the seventh consecutive day.

Day-of-rest laws

A few states require a day off in seven, which indirectly limits forced overtime:

  • California Labor Code 551 and 552: one day's rest in seven, measured per workweek rather than any rolling seven days (Mendoza v. Nordstrom, 2017); the exemption for short shifts applies only if every shift in the week is six hours or less; an employer may not induce an employee to forgo rest but may permit a fully informed employee to choose to work.
  • Illinois One Day Rest in Seven Act (820 ILCS 140), amended effective January 1, 2023: 24 consecutive hours of rest in every consecutive seven-day period (previously calendar week), a 20-minute meal period within the first five hours of a shift over 7.5 hours plus another 20 minutes for every additional 4.5 hours, and penalties per week of violation. Permits for seven-day work are available from the Department of Labor.
  • New York Labor Law 161: 24 consecutive hours of rest in every calendar week for factories, mercantile establishments, hotels, restaurants, freight and passenger elevators and other listed industries.
  • Wisconsin 103.85: 24 consecutive hours of rest in every seven days in factories and mercantile establishments, with exemptions for janitors, security, dairies, canneries, bakeries, hotels and restaurants, emergencies and employees who sign a voluntary waiver.
  • Massachusetts: retail employees may not be required to work on Sundays or certain holidays (chapter 136), though the Sunday premium pay requirement was phased out by January 1, 2023.
  • Texas Labor Code 52.001: retail employers must allow one 24-hour period off in each seven days for employees who request it for religious observance.
  • Kentucky has no cap but requires time and a half on the seventh consecutive day of work in a week (KRS 337.050).

Minors

Federal child labor rules (29 CFR 570.35) cap 14- and 15-year-olds at 3 hours on a school day, 18 in a school week, 8 on a non-school day and 40 in a non-school week, between 7 a.m. and 7 p.m. (9 p.m. from June 1 through Labor Day). There is no federal hour limit at 16 and 17, but many states impose one, and several loosened their rules between 2023 and 2026 while others tightened them. The child labor laws by state table has the detail; the short version is that mandatory overtime for anyone under 16 is unlawful everywhere and for 16- and 17-year-olds depends on the state.

Predictive scheduling laws

Fair workweek laws do not ban mandatory overtime but make last-minute additions expensive. Oregon (statewide), New York City, San Francisco, Emeryville, Berkeley, Los Angeles and Los Angeles County (from July 1, 2025), Seattle, Philadelphia, Chicago and Evanston require covered retail, food service and hospitality employers to post schedules 10 to 14 days in advance and to pay predictability pay (typically one to four hours) when hours are added or changed with less notice, and most give employees a right to decline added hours. They also require 10 or 11 hours of rest between closing and opening shifts or premium pay for "clopening." The predictive scheduling laws by state guide covers the thresholds and penalties.

For employers: a mandatory overtime policy

  1. State the right and the limits. "Employees may be required to work overtime as business needs require. The Company will give as much notice as practicable, normally 24 hours, and will apply the limits required by law for nurses, minors and employees in [Maine, Oregon manufacturing]."
  2. Distribution. Volunteers first, then rotation by seniority or a documented list, so that the same people are not always compelled. Keep the record; it answers discrimination complaints.
  3. Accommodations. A sentence directing employees with religious, medical, pregnancy or family-leave needs to HR, and a commitment to the interactive process.
  4. Pay. All overtime is paid at the legal rate, including unauthorized overtime, which is a disciplinary matter rather than a pay matter.
  5. Caps you choose. Many employers voluntarily cap consecutive hours at 12 or 16 and consecutive days at 6, both because fatigue drives injuries (the NIOSH literature and the Dembe 2005 study found injury rates rising sharply past 12 hours) and because it keeps the rotation fair.
  6. Discipline. Progressive, documented, consistent, and never applied to a protected refusal.

If you are told to stay

Check the table for your state. If you are a nurse in one of the 18 states, the law may let you refuse once the exceptions (disaster, unforeseen emergency after good-faith efforts, ongoing procedure) are not met; document the request and the staffing situation, and report violations to the state health or labor department. If you are in Maine or in Oregon manufacturing, there is a cap. If you are under 18, there are hour limits. If you have a religious conflict, a medical restriction, a pregnancy-related limitation or approved intermittent FMLA leave, tell HR in writing before refusing and ask for accommodation; refusing first and explaining later loses the protection in most cases. Otherwise, you may be required to work, and you must be paid: time and a half for every hour over 40 if you are non-exempt, and daily overtime in California, Alaska, Nevada and Colorado. If overtime is required but not paid, the Department of Labor's Wage and Hour Division and the state labor department take complaints, and retaliation for complaining is unlawful. Key takeaways

  • Federal law sets no limit on hours for workers 16 and over; employers may require overtime and discipline or fire employees who refuse, as long as non-exempt employees are paid time and a half over 40 hours.
  • Protected refusals are narrow: religious observance (Groff v. DeJoy's substantial-cost standard), disability accommodation, pregnancy accommodation, approved FMLA intermittent leave, military duty, imminent-danger safety refusals and union contract terms.
  • Eighteen states ban or restrict mandatory overtime for nurses (AK, CA, CT, IL, ME, MD, MA, MN, MO, NH, NJ, NY, OR, PA, RI, TX, WA, WV), typically allowing exceptions only for disasters, unforeseen emergencies after good-faith efforts, and ongoing procedures.
  • Maine has the only general cap, 80 overtime hours in any two weeks; Oregon caps manufacturing at 10 hours a day and 55 a week (60 with written consent).
  • Day-of-rest laws in California, Illinois (every consecutive 7 days since 2023), New York, Wisconsin, plus retail rules in Massachusetts and Texas, indirectly limit forced overtime.
  • Predictive scheduling laws in Oregon and ten cities do not ban mandatory overtime but require notice and predictability pay for added hours and give employees a right to decline them.
  • Minors are capped everywhere under 16; limits at 16 and 17 depend on the state.

Frequently asked questions

Can my employer force me to work overtime?

In most cases, yes. Federal law sets no limit on hours for adults and does not give employees a right to refuse; it only requires overtime pay for non-exempt employees. Exceptions exist for nurses in 18 states, all employees in Maine beyond 80 overtime hours in two weeks, Oregon manufacturing workers beyond 55 hours, minors, and employees with religious, disability, pregnancy or FMLA protections.

Can I be fired for refusing to work overtime?

Generally yes in at-will states, unless the refusal is protected: a religious conflict the employer could accommodate, a medical restriction under the ADA, approved FMLA leave, a state nurse-overtime statute, a union contract or an employment contract. Montana requires good cause for established employees. Refusing because of childcare, a second job or fatigue is not protected by law.

Is there a legal limit to how many hours you can work in a day?

Not under federal law for workers 16 and over. California requires overtime after 8 hours and double time after 12; Alaska, Nevada (for lower-paid workers) and Colorado (after 12) have daily overtime; nurses in California are capped at 12 hours in 24; Oregon manufacturing is capped at 10 hours a day. Truck drivers, pilots and railroad workers have federal hours-of-service limits.

Which states prohibit mandatory overtime for nurses?

Alaska, California, Connecticut, Illinois, Maine, Maryland, Massachusetts, Minnesota, Missouri, New Hampshire, New Jersey, New York, Oregon, Pennsylvania, Rhode Island, Texas, Washington and West Virginia. Most allow exceptions for declared emergencies, unforeseen staffing shortages after good-faith efforts to find other staff, and completing a procedure in progress. None restrict voluntary overtime.

How much notice does an employer have to give for mandatory overtime?

None under federal law or most state laws. Predictive scheduling laws in Oregon and in New York City, San Francisco, Emeryville, Berkeley, Los Angeles, Los Angeles County, Seattle, Philadelphia, Chicago and Evanston require covered retail, food and hospitality employers to post schedules 10 to 14 days ahead and pay predictability pay for late changes. Union contracts often set notice rules.

Can an employer make you work 7 days a week?

In most states yes, with overtime pay. California requires one day's rest in seven per workweek; Illinois requires 24 consecutive hours off in every 7 days; New York and Wisconsin require it in listed industries; Massachusetts and Texas protect retail workers' Sundays or a weekly day off. Kentucky pays time and a half on the seventh consecutive day. Maine caps overtime at 80 hours in two weeks.

Do salaried employees have to work mandatory overtime?

Exempt salaried employees can be required to work any hours without extra pay. Non-exempt salaried employees can also be required to work overtime but must receive time and a half for hours over 40, calculated on their regular rate. The exemption depends on duties and a salary of at least $684 a week under federal law, higher in several states.

What should I do if I am forced to work overtime without pay?

Keep your own record of hours, then raise it in writing with payroll or HR. If unpaid, file a complaint with the Department of Labor's Wage and Hour Division or the state labor agency; you can recover two years of back wages (three if willful) plus an equal amount in liquidated damages, and retaliation for complaining is illegal. Being required to work overtime is lawful; not being paid for it is not.
