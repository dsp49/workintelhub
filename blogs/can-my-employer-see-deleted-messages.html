Can My Employer See Deleted Messages and Files at Work?

P Pavan
November 20, 2026 · 8 min read

Often, yes. Deleting a message or file on a work system usually removes it from your view, not from the company's systems. Microsoft 365, Slack and Google Workspace all let administrators set retention policies and legal holds that keep copies of deleted content for months or years, and compliance tools can search those copies. Where no retention policy applies, deleted items still sit in recoverable folders and recycle bins for weeks. On a company laptop, deleted files may be recoverable from backups or forensic images.

This guide explains what happens to deleted content in each major platform, what changes under a legal hold, what employers can and cannot recover, and why deleting work messages during a dispute can backfire. For what employers can see before you delete anything, see can my employer see my Teams or Slack messages.

Microsoft 365: Outlook, Teams and OneDrive

  • Outlook: when you delete an email and empty Deleted Items, it moves to the Recoverable Items folder, kept for 14 days by default and adjustable to 30. If your mailbox is on Litigation Hold or a retention policy applies, nothing is purged: deletions and edits are preserved, and the original version of an edited message is kept. Compliance staff can search Recoverable Items with eDiscovery.
  • Teams: Microsoft's documentation is direct: messages visible in the Teams app "are not an accurate reflection" of what is retained. When a retention policy or hold applies, a deleted chat message disappears from the app but a copy moves to a hidden folder and remains discoverable with eDiscovery. Call logs are kept indefinitely by default.
  • OneDrive and SharePoint: deleted files stay in the recycle bin for 93 days on work or school accounts unless an administrator changes it, and retention policies can keep them far longer.

Slack

Retention depends on the plan and settings. Free workspaces keep 90 days or one year of history. Pro and Business+ workspaces keep messages for the workspace's lifetime by default, and an administrator can choose a setting that keeps deleted and edited messages. Exports differ by plan: Free and Pro owners can export public channels, Business+ can export private channels and direct messages after Slack approves an application, and Enterprise owners can export all conversations and individual users' data. Enterprise plans also support legal holds, which preserve a person's messages and files even if they are edited or deleted, overriding retention settings (Slack Connect conversations are an exception). Tools connected through Slack's Discovery API can capture deleted content as it happens.

Google Workspace

Google Vault keeps data covered by a retention rule or hold "even if users delete messages and files or empty their trash." Holds take precedence over retention rules. Without a rule or hold, Drive items deleted permanently become unavailable to Vault, and Vault keeps other data for about 30 days before final purge. Whether your employer can recover a deleted Gmail message or Drive file therefore depends on how Vault was configured.

Company laptops and phones

  • Backups: endpoint backup tools copy files on a schedule, so a deleted file may survive in an earlier backup.
  • Forensic recovery: on traditional hard drives, deleted files are often recoverable until overwritten. On modern SSDs with TRIM, recovery is much less reliable; research has found results ranging from full recovery to none depending on the drive.
  • Synced data: files in OneDrive, Google Drive or Dropbox for Business are governed by the cloud retention rules above, not the laptop.
  • Phones: on a company-managed phone, business messages in managed apps are typically backed up or archived; regulated firms archive texts and approved messaging apps.

Legal holds, regulation and deleting during a dispute

When litigation or an investigation is reasonably anticipated, employers must preserve relevant data, so they put custodians on hold and automatic deletion stops for them. Regulated industries go further: broker-dealers must keep business communications under SEC Rule 17a-4 regardless of the channel, which is why deleting work messages is a serious violation there (see off-channel communications).

Employees are also bound by preservation duties once they are in a dispute. In Jones v. Riot Hospitality Group (9th Cir. 2024), an employee's discrimination lawsuit was dismissed and she was ordered to pay over $69,000 in fees because she deleted text messages and coordinated with witnesses to delete theirs. Courts can infer intent where someone with technical knowledge deletes data without a credible explanation. If you are in a dispute with your employer, do not delete anything relevant, on work or personal devices.

What to do

  1. Assume anything you send on work systems is kept, even after you delete it.
  2. Keep personal conversations on personal devices and accounts.
  3. Never use deletion to hide a mistake; report it. Deletion is visible to compliance and looks worse than the original message.
  4. If you receive a preservation notice, follow it exactly.
  5. If you are in a dispute, preserve your own relevant messages and get advice before deleting anything.
  6. Ask HR or IT what retention policies apply if you need to know; see how to ask HR what is monitored.

Key takeaways

  • Deleting work messages and files usually removes them from your view, not from the company's systems; retention policies and legal holds preserve copies that administrators can search.
  • Outlook keeps deleted mail in Recoverable Items for 14 days by default (up to 30) and indefinitely under a hold; Teams messages deleted under a retention policy stay discoverable; OneDrive keeps deleted work files 93 days.
  • Slack paid plans keep messages for the workspace's lifetime by default, and Enterprise legal holds preserve edited and deleted content; Google Vault keeps data under retention rules even after users empty their trash.
  • Deleted files on company laptops may survive in backups or be forensically recovered, though modern SSDs make recovery less reliable.
  • Deleting relevant messages during a dispute can be sanctioned: in Jones v. Riot Hospitality (2024) an employee's case was dismissed for deleting texts.

Frequently asked questions

Can my employer see deleted Teams messages?

Often, yes. If your organization has a Microsoft 365 retention policy or legal hold, a deleted Teams message disappears from the app but a copy is kept in a hidden folder and can be found with eDiscovery. Microsoft notes that what is visible in Teams does not reflect what is retained.

Can my employer recover deleted emails?

Usually. In Exchange Online, deleted email is kept in the Recoverable Items folder for 14 days by default, up to 30, and is never purged while a mailbox is on hold or covered by a retention policy. Compliance staff can search it.

Can my employer see deleted Slack messages?

It depends on the plan and settings. Paid plans keep messages for the workspace's lifetime by default and can be set to retain deleted messages. Enterprise plans support legal holds that preserve deleted content, and exports and the Discovery API can capture it.

Are deleted files on a work laptop really gone?

Not necessarily. They may be in the recycle bin, in endpoint backups, or in synced cloud storage with its own retention, and on hard drives they can often be forensically recovered. On modern solid-state drives recovery is less reliable, but cloud and backup copies usually remain.

Can my employer see deleted files in OneDrive or Google Drive?

OneDrive work accounts keep deleted files in the recycle bin for 93 days by default, and retention policies can keep them longer. Google Vault keeps Drive files covered by a retention rule or hold even after they are deleted from the trash.

Is it illegal to delete work messages?

Not in ordinary circumstances, but it can breach company policy, and once a dispute or investigation is anticipated, deleting relevant messages can lead to court sanctions, including dismissal of a lawsuit. In regulated industries such as securities, business communications must be retained by law.
