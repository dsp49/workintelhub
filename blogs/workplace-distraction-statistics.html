Workplace Distraction Statistics (2026)

P Pavan
October 16, 2026 · 12 min read

The average focused session at work lasted 13 minutes and 7 seconds in 2025, down 9% from 2023, across 163,638 employees whose activity ActivTrak logged. Knowledge workers check email or chat about every six minutes, and only 18.6% go twenty minutes without doing so. Heavy Microsoft 365 users are interrupted by a message, meeting or notification every two minutes during the working day. The median worker's longest uninterrupted stretch is about 40 minutes.

The distraction statistics that rank are built on a 23-minute "refocus" figure that measures something else, a $650 billion cost from a consultancy that closed a decade ago, and an attention-span-of-a-goldfish claim from an advertising report. This page uses logged data from Microsoft, RescueTime and ActivTrak, Gloria Mark's field studies, the open-office and phone experiments, and Slack's six-country index, states what each measured, and corrects the famous numbers. Our page on context switching covers the cost of a single switch; this one covers how often they happen, where they come from and what reduces them.

Two caveats apply. Microsoft's two-minute figure is for the most-pinged fifth of users and is computed across 24 hours, so the "275 interruptions a day" version overstates the average worker's day. And about 44% of interruptions in Mark's field studies were self-initiated, with no external trigger. People interrupt themselves about as often as they are interrupted. Microsoft's 2025 survey of 31,000 workers found 48% of employees and 52% of leaders describing work as chaotic and fragmented.

What an interruption costs, correctly stated

The 23 minutes and 15 seconds that every page quotes comes from Gloria Mark's 2006 Gallup interview about her field study of 36 knowledge workers. It is the average elapsed time before an interrupted task was resumed, during which workers handled on average 2.26 other tasks. It is not a measure of how long it takes the brain to refocus. The 2005 paper on 24 workers put the figure at 25 minutes 26 seconds, found 77% of interrupted work resumed the same day, and found self-interrupted work took longer to return to than externally interrupted work, 29 minutes against 23. The 2008 paper that pages usually cite for the 23-minute figure does not contain it; it is a 48-person lab study that found interrupted people finished an email task faster but with significantly more stress, frustration and effort.

The error cost is better established than the time cost. Altmann, Trafton and Hambrick found in a 2014 lab study that interruptions of 2.8 seconds doubled errors on a sequential task and 4.4 seconds roughly tripled them. Our context switching page covers the switch-cost literature.

Where distractions come from

RescueTime's 2018 survey of its users found 64% naming face-to-face interruptions as the most common and 51.5% the hardest to ignore, while email interrupted 50% regularly and was the easiest to ignore. In Microsoft's 2023 data, inefficient meetings ranked as the top productivity disruptor and 68% said they lacked enough uninterrupted focus time. Slack's 2023 index of 10,333 desk workers found 27% saying they spend too much time in meetings, two hours a day as the overburden point, and about four hours of focus time as the ideal. Mark's 2016 study of 40 workers with sensors found longer daily email time associated with lower self-rated productivity and higher measured stress, that people who checked email by choice rather than by notification reported higher productivity, and that batching email did not reduce stress. Our meeting statistics page covers the largest single source.

Phones and notifications

Two experiments answer the question of whether a phone you do not touch still costs you. Stothart, Mitchum and Yehnert found in 2015 that a notification alone, unanswered, impaired performance on an attention task by about as much as actively texting or taking a call. Ward, Duke, Gneezy and Bos found in their 2017 study of nearly 800 people that those whose phone was in another room outperformed those with the phone on the desk on working-memory and reasoning tasks, whether the phone was on or off, face up or down. Ophir, Nass and Wagner's 2009 Stanford study found heavy media multitaskers were more susceptible to irrelevant distractors and slower to switch tasks. On social media specifically, Pew's 2016 survey of 2,003 adults found 34% of workers used it at work for a mental break, 51% said their employer had rules about it, and 56% of those who used it for work agreed it distracted them from the work they needed to do.

Open offices and noise

Kim and de Dear's 2013 analysis of 42,764 occupants of 303 US office buildings found 49% to 59% of open-plan and cubicle occupants dissatisfied with sound privacy against 18% in private offices, and open plan scoring lowest on 13 of 15 indoor-environment factors. The ease-of-interaction benefit was smaller than the noise penalty. Bernstein and Turban's 2018 field experiments using wearable sensors at two Fortune 500 companies found face-to-face interaction fell about 70% after a move to open plan, from about 5.8 to 1.7 hours a day in one study, while email rose 56% and instant messages 67%. The open office reduced the interaction it was built to increase. Steelcase's survey of more than 10,500 workers found only 11% highly satisfied with their environment, and 98% of those could concentrate easily.

Remote against office

RescueTime's 2018 survey found 43.6% of remote workers against 55% of workers in offices of 50 or more reporting constant interruptions. Office workers were interrupted face to face at 80% against 43% for remote workers, who were instead interrupted by phone calls at 58% against 18% and by household members at 43%. In Bloom's randomised Ctrip trial, about four points of the 13% home-working performance gain came from more calls per minute, which the authors attributed to a quieter environment. Owl Labs' 2025 survey of 2,000 US workers found 51% saying their work arrangement made them more productive and 6% less. Remote workers trade one set of interruptions for another, and come out slightly ahead on measured output. Our remote work productivity statistics page covers the trials in full.

Cost in hours, and the dollar figures to avoid

The measured time costs are these: 60% of logged time is focused and 40% is not, in ActivTrak's data; the median longest stretch of focus is 40 minutes; 17% of workers never get fifteen uninterrupted minutes. Slack found 50% of desk workers rarely or never take breaks and those who do score 13% higher on productivity, and that workers who feel obliged to work after hours score 20% lower. Microsoft's 2025 survey found 80% of the global workforce saying they lack the time or energy to do their work. No opened primary source gives a defensible US dollar cost of distraction. The $650 billion figure comes from Basex, a consultancy that estimated in 2007 that 28% of the knowledge worker's day was lost to interruptions, with no public method; the $588 billion version is its 2005 estimate. If you need a dollar figure, multiply one of the hour figures above by your own loaded hourly rate and say so. Our employee cost calculator does that.

What reduces it

  • Phone out of the room. The only intervention with a controlled experiment behind it, in Ward's 2017 study.
  • Check email by choice, not notification. Associated with higher self-rated productivity in Mark's 2016 study; batching alone did not reduce stress.
  • Cap meetings at two hours a day and protect four hours of focus. Slack's thresholds from 10,333 workers.
  • Take breaks. Associated with 13% higher productivity scores in the same data.
  • Signal unavailability. In RescueTime's survey, 48.5% used headphones as a do-not-disturb signal, 29% used do-not-disturb mode, fewer than half had changed notification settings, and only 13% had raised interruptions with a boss or colleague.

Figures to stop using

  • "It takes 23 minutes to refocus after an interruption." It is the time before an interrupted task was resumed, spent on other tasks. The 2005 paper says 25 minutes; the 2008 paper does not contain the figure.
  • "The average attention span is 8 seconds, less than a goldfish." A 2015 Microsoft Canada advertising report citing a statistics website with no study. The measured screen-attention figure is 47 seconds.
  • "275 interruptions a day." Top 20% of users, over 24 hours. Quote "every two minutes during the working day" for heavy users.
  • "Distractions cost the US $650 billion a year." A 2007 estimate from a defunct consultancy with no public method.
  • "15 interruptions an hour" and "56 a day." No source exists.
  • "Multitasking cuts productivity 40%." An APA paraphrase of a 2001 lab study that reports switch costs in milliseconds.
  • "Open offices boost collaboration." The sensor study found face-to-face interaction fell 70%.
  • "70% of workers feel distracted (Udemy)." A 2018 panel survey whose report is no longer hosted.

Key takeaways

  • The average focused session is 13 minutes 7 seconds, down 9% since 2023, and 60% of logged time is focused, across 163,638 employees.
  • Knowledge workers check email or chat every six minutes and the median longest uninterrupted stretch is 40 minutes. Heavy Microsoft 365 users get a ping every two minutes.
  • The famous 23-minute figure is the time before an interrupted task was resumed, during which workers did 2.26 other things. It is not a refocusing time.
  • Face-to-face interruptions are the most common and hardest to ignore for 64% of workers; email is the easiest to ignore.
  • A phone on the desk, even off, lowers working-memory performance, and an unanswered notification impairs attention as much as a call.
  • Moving to an open-plan office cut face-to-face interaction by about 70% and raised email 56%. Half of open-plan occupants are dissatisfied with sound privacy against 18% in private offices.
  • Remote workers report constant interruptions less often, 43.6% against 55% in large offices, but more phone and household interruptions.
  • The $650 billion cost figure is a 2007 estimate from a defunct consultancy. No measured US dollar cost exists.

Frequently asked questions

How often are employees interrupted at work?

About every two minutes during the working day for the most-messaged fifth of Microsoft 365 users, per Microsoft's 2025 telemetry, and knowledge workers switch into email or chat roughly every six minutes in RescueTime's logged data on 50,000 users. The average focused session lasts about 13 minutes.

How long does it take to get back on task after an interruption?

Gloria Mark's field studies found interrupted work was resumed after 23 to 25 minutes on average, but most of that time was spent on two or more other tasks, not refocusing. The 2008 lab study often cited for this found interrupted people worked faster with more stress.

What is the biggest distraction at work?

Other people. 64% of workers in RescueTime's survey named face-to-face interruptions as their most common interruption and 51.5% as the hardest to ignore. Meetings ranked as the top productivity disruptor in Microsoft's survey of 31,000 workers.

Do phone notifications hurt focus if you do not check them?

Yes. A single unanswered notification impaired attention-task performance about as much as actively texting in a 2015 experiment, and having a phone on the desk lowered working-memory scores in a study of nearly 800 people, whether it was on or off.

Are open offices more distracting?

Yes. 49% to 59% of open-plan and cubicle occupants are dissatisfied with sound privacy against 18% in private offices, across 42,764 respondents. A sensor study found moving to open plan cut face-to-face interaction by about 70% while email rose 56%.

How much time do distractions cost per day?

Logged data show 60% of work time is focused and 40% is not, and the average focused session is 13 minutes. The median worker's longest uninterrupted stretch is about 40 minutes, and 40% never get 30 straight minutes.

Are remote workers less distracted?

Partly. 43.6% of remote workers against 55% of workers in large offices report constant interruptions, and a randomised trial attributed about four points of a 13% home-working performance gain to a quieter environment. Remote workers get more phone and household interruptions.

What reduces workplace distraction?

Keeping the phone out of the room, checking email on your own schedule rather than by notification, taking breaks, capping meetings at two hours a day and protecting about four hours of focus time all have supporting data. Only 13% of workers have raised interruptions with a boss or colleague.
