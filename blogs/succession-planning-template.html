Succession Planning Template: Readiness and Bench Strength

P Pavan
November 13, 2026 · 11 min read

Only about one in five organizations has a formal succession plan, according to SHRM's research, and the cost of not having one is high: a 2021 Harvard Business Review analysis estimated that badly managed CEO and C-suite transitions wipe out close to $1 trillion of market value a year in the S&P 1500. Leadership turnover is running at record levels: Challenger, Gray and Christmas counted a record 446 public-company CEO exits in 2025, and Russell Reynolds found 86% of incoming CEOs were first-timers. Yet DDI's 2025 Global Leadership Forecast found only 20% of HR leaders rated their leadership bench as strong.

This template covers the five parts of a working succession plan: identifying critical roles, naming successors with readiness ratings, scoring bench strength, planning development, and naming emergency successors. It includes a bench strength calculator, the 9-box grid and its limits, governance, legal cautions, and a version for small-business owners. Bench strength calculator

Bench strength is the share of critical roles with a ready successor. A common target is at least one Ready Now and one Ready in 1 to 2 years for every critical role. The score below weights Ready Now at 1.0 and Ready in 1 to 2 years at 0.5.

Why it matters: the numbers

  • 21% of HR professionals reported having a formal succession plan, 24% an informal one and 56% none (SHRM).
  • Close to $1 trillion a year in S&P 1500 market value is lost to badly managed CEO and C-suite transitions (Fernández-Aráoz, Nagel and Green, Harvard Business Review, May 2021).
  • 2,032 US CEO exits in 2025, down 9% from 2,221 in 2024, but a record 446 at public companies (Challenger, February 4, 2026).
  • 234 global CEO departures tracked by Russell Reynolds in 2025, up 16%; average outgoing tenure 7.1 years; 86% of incoming CEOs were first-time CEOs.
  • 13% CEO succession rate in the S&P 500 in 2025, up from 10%, with 33% of new CEOs hired externally, up from 18% (Conference Board, as reported by HR Executive).
  • 20% of HR leaders rated their leadership bench strong in 2024, up from 11% in 2020; 80% still lacked confidence in their pipelines; organizations with strong benches were 2.9 times more likely to fill leadership roles internally successfully (DDI Global Leadership Forecast 2025).
  • High-potential individual contributors who intended to leave rose from 13% in 2020 to 21% in 2024 (DDI), which is the risk of naming successors and then not developing them.

Five steps to a succession plan

  1. Identify critical roles. Not only the C-suite: roles whose sudden vacancy would stop revenue, operations or compliance, roles that take more than six months to fill, and roles held by one person with unique knowledge. Most organizations end up with 5 to 15% of positions.
  2. Define success in each role. The three to five capabilities and experiences a successor needs, written down, so assessment is against criteria rather than impressions.
  3. Assess candidates and rate readiness. Ready Now (could step in today with normal onboarding), Ready in 1 to 2 years (needs specific development), Ready in 3 or more years. Use performance data, assessments and multiple raters, not one manager's view.
  4. Plan development. For each successor, two or three concrete actions with dates: a stretch assignment, a rotation, a mentor, a course. Development without assignments rarely changes readiness.
  5. Review and act. Review the plan at least annually and after any leadership change; track whether named successors are actually promoted when roles open.

The 9-box grid

The 9-box plots each person on performance (low, medium, high) against potential (low, medium, high). It descends from the McKinsey and General Electric business-portfolio matrix of the 1970s and was adapted for talent reviews later; its exact talent origin is poorly documented.

High potentialEnigma: develop or reassignGrowth employee: stretch assignmentsFuture leader: succession candidate

Use it as a conversation aid in calibration, not as a label. "Potential" is the weakest rating in HR because it is a prediction made by managers with their own biases; define it with observable indicators (learns quickly, takes on broader scope, leads without authority) and calibrate across managers. Emergency succession

Every critical role needs an interim successor who could take over tomorrow for 90 days, even if they are not the long-term choice. For the CEO, boards typically name an interim (often the CFO, COO or a director), document the communication plan for employees, customers and investors, and pre-agree delegations of authority. Emergency coverage is the first gap to close because it is cheap: it requires a name, a briefing and access to the right systems, not years of development.

Governance and review

  • Board oversight of CEO succession is a core governance duty. Public companies disclose human capital information under SEC Regulation S-K Item 101(c) (effective November 9, 2020) to the extent material, and proxy advisers and investors routinely ask about succession planning.
  • Talent reviews at least annually, by business unit, with HR facilitating calibration.
  • Metrics: bench strength (the calculator above), percentage of critical roles with emergency successors, internal fill rate for leadership roles, and the success rate of internal placements after 12 months.
  • Transparency: whether to tell successors they are on the plan is a judgment call; telling them improves retention and engagement in development, but creates expectations that must be managed.

Legal and fairness cautions

Succession decisions are employment decisions under Title VII and the Age Discrimination in Employment Act. Avoid proxies for age such as "runway" or "long-term potential" applied only to younger candidates, document the criteria and the assessment, review candidate pools for disparate impact, and make development opportunities available on the same criteria to everyone in the eligible pool. Plans that consistently pick successors who resemble the incumbent are a fairness and a performance problem.

Small business and owner succession

For owner-managed businesses, succession means ownership as well as leadership. The Exit Planning Institute's 2023 national survey found only 42% of owners had a written transition plan, while 49% wanted to exit within five years and 75% within ten. A small-business plan should name who could run the business if the owner were unavailable for three months, document key relationships and passwords, set out the intended exit (family, management buyout, sale) and its timeline, and be reviewed with an accountant and attorney. Writing down the processes only the owner knows is the single most valuable step.

Key takeaways

  • Only about 21% of organizations have a formal succession plan (SHRM), while poorly managed CEO and C-suite transitions cost the S&P 1500 close to $1 trillion a year (Harvard Business Review, 2021).
  • CEO turnover is high: a record 446 public-company CEO exits in 2025 (Challenger), 86% of new CEOs first-timers (Russell Reynolds), and a third of S&P 500 successors hired externally.
  • DDI found only 20% of HR leaders rated their leadership bench strong in 2024; strong-bench organizations were 2.9 times more likely to fill leadership roles internally.
  • A plan has five steps: identify critical roles, define success criteria, rate successors Ready Now, Ready in 1 to 2 years or Ready in 3+ years, plan concrete development, and review annually.
  • Name an emergency successor for every critical role first; it is cheap and closes the biggest risk.
  • Use the 9-box as a calibration aid, define potential with observable indicators, and guard against age and other bias, since succession decisions are employment decisions.
  • Only 42% of small-business owners have a written transition plan; documenting owner-only knowledge is the most valuable first step.

Frequently asked questions

What is succession planning?

The process of identifying critical roles, assessing and developing internal candidates who could fill them, and naming emergency successors, so the organization can replace leaders and key specialists without disruption. It covers the CEO but should also include any role whose sudden vacancy would harm operations.

What should a succession plan include?

For each critical role: the incumbent and their risk of leaving, an emergency successor, one or two longer-term successors with readiness ratings (Ready Now, 1 to 2 years, 3+ years), the key gaps for each successor, dated development actions, and a review date. Supporting talent profiles and a skills matrix help.

What is bench strength?

The share of critical roles that have a ready successor. A common target is at least one Ready Now and one Ready in 1 to 2 years for each critical role. DDI found only 20% of HR leaders rated their organization's leadership bench as strong in 2024.

What is the 9-box grid?

A talent review tool that plots each person on performance (low, medium, high) against potential (low, medium, high), producing nine categories from underperformer to future leader. It is useful for calibration discussions but should not be used as a permanent label, and potential should be defined with observable indicators.

How many companies have a succession plan?

SHRM's research found 21% of HR professionals reported a formal succession plan, 24% an informal one and 56% none. Among small-business owners, the Exit Planning Institute found 42% had a written transition plan in 2023.

How often should a succession plan be reviewed?

At least once a year in a formal talent review, and whenever a critical role changes hands, a successor leaves or the organization restructures. Emergency successors should be confirmed more often, since they must be ready immediately.

Should employees know they are in a succession plan?

Practices differ. Telling successors tends to improve their retention and commitment to development, but creates expectations of promotion that must be managed. Many organizations tell people they are in a development pool for broader roles without naming the specific role.

What is an emergency successor?

A person who could take over a critical role immediately for a limited period, typically 90 days, if the incumbent suddenly left or became unavailable. They need not be the long-term successor. Naming one for every critical role, with a briefing and system access, is the cheapest way to reduce succession risk.
