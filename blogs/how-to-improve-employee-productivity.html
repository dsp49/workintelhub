How to Improve Employee Productivity

A Admin
August 21, 2026 · 9 min read

Productivity is a ratio: useful output divided by the input it took. That means there are only two ways to improve employee productivity, and one of them is almost always the wrong one. You can raise the output, which is slow and depends on skill, tooling, and demand. Or you can lower the input, which is fast and mostly depends on decisions a manager already has the authority to make. Most advice in this category quietly assumes the input is fixed and pushes on effort instead. That is how teams end up working longer for the same result.

This article works through the levers that change the ratio, ordered by how quickly they act and how much evidence sits behind them. It names what each one changes, and how you would know it worked. Where a number is load bearing, the source and year are attached, because this category is full of figures that trace back to nobody.

Remove friction before adding effort

The single largest drag on knowledge work right now is not effort. It is fragmentation. Microsoft's Work Trend Index published in June 2025, which combined Microsoft 365 telemetry with a survey of 31,000 knowledge workers across 31 markets, reported that people are interrupted roughly every two minutes during work hours, about 275 times a day. In the same research, 48% of employees and 52% of leaders described their work as chaotic and fragmented, and one in three said the pace makes it impossible to keep up.

A day chopped into two minute pieces cannot hold anything that takes an hour. This is why activity data so often looks busy while delivery does not move: the busyness is real, and it is the problem rather than the evidence of progress. Before you change anything about how hard people work, count the things that break their concentration and remove the ones nobody would defend out loud.

Three usually come off without argument. The recurring status meeting that reports what is already written down. The approval step that has never once resulted in a rejection. The expectation that chat messages get answered inside a few minutes, which in Microsoft's telemetry amounted to 117 emails and 153 Teams messages on a typical weekday. None of these cost money to remove, and all three act on the same week you decide.

Give the week a shape

Interruptions are not evenly distributed, which is what makes them fixable. Microsoft found that 50% of meetings fall between 9 to 11 in the morning and 1 to 3 in the afternoon, which is precisely when most people concentrate best, and that 57% of meetings are ad hoc with no calendar invite at all. The working day is being consumed at its most valuable hours by events that were never planned.

The fix is structural rather than motivational. Move recurring meetings out of the two peak windows and defend those windows as team wide focus time, so it does not depend on individuals declining invitations. Timeboxing and time blocking both work here, and our guide to focus blocks covers how to make them survive contact with a real calendar. The test of whether it worked is not whether people feel better. It is whether anything that needs two uninterrupted hours now gets two uninterrupted hours.

Watch the edges of the day while you do it. Microsoft recorded meetings after 8 pm rising 16% year over year, 29% of people still checking the inbox at 10 pm, and 40% of those online at 6 am triaging email. Work that leaks into the evening is usually work that was displaced from the middle of the day, not extra output.

Fix the manager layer

Gallup's State of the Global Workplace, published in April 2026, reported that employee engagement declined for the second consecutive year in 2025 and reached its lowest level since 2020, and that the decline was most acute among managers. That detail matters more than the headline. A depleted manager is the mechanism by which everything else on this list fails to happen, because every lever here needs somebody with authority and attention to implement it.

Gallup has also argued, drawing on research covering 27 million employees, that managers account for at least 70% of the variance in employee engagement scores across business units. That figure is widely quoted and worth handling carefully: Gallup does not publish the statistical method behind it, and variance explained is not the same as cause. Treat it as a strong signal about where to spend attention rather than a law.

Practically, this means the first productivity intervention on most teams is reducing what the manager is carrying, not what the team is carrying. A manager with no uninterrupted time cannot remove anyone else's interruptions.

Stop treating hours as the lever

The instinct when output looks low is to ask for more time. The evidence points the other way. The American Psychological Association's 2024 Work in America survey, conducted by The Harris Poll among 2,027 employed US adults with a margin of error of 3.1 percentage points, found that 45% already work more hours per week than they want to, and that 67% had experienced at least one outcome associated with burnout in the previous month, such as low motivation, lack of interest, or reduced effort at work.

Reduced effort at work is a burnout symptom, which means it is frequently mistaken for the problem it is actually caused by. If a team's output has fallen and its hours have risen, the productivity ratio has already collapsed and more hours will accelerate it. Our guide to spotting burnout in workload data covers the signals that appear before anybody says anything out loud.

There is also decent evidence that flexibility is not the thing costing you output. A randomised controlled trial of 1,612 employees at Trip.com, published in Nature in June 2024, compared five days in the office against three days in with two at home over six months. Quit rates in the hybrid group were about a third lower, with no measurable impact on performance reviews or promotions.

Measure without targeting

You cannot improve what you refuse to look at, and you will corrupt whatever you turn into a target. Goodhart's Law is the compact version: once a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure. Counts are the most vulnerable, because they are the easiest to inflate. Tickets get split, commits get padded, calls get shortened. Our piece on why productivity metrics fail covers how each of those breaks in practice.

Measure to notice change rather than to rank people. Frameworks such as SPACE and DORA exist because single numbers do not survive contact with knowledge work, and both deliberately combine several dimensions so no one of them can be gamed alone. If you need a starting point for what to watch, measuring productivity when you cannot see the work sets out a practical shortlist.

It is worth knowing what engagement research actually claims here, because it is routinely overstated. Gallup's Q12 meta-analysis, tenth edition, covering 112,312 business units in 276 organisations across 96 countries, compared units in the top quartile for engagement against those in the bottom quartile. The top quartile saw 18% higher productivity measured as sales, 23% higher profitability, 81% lower absenteeism, and 41% fewer quality defects. Those are differences between quartiles and the relationship is correlational. It is not a promise that an engagement programme will produce a 23% profit increase.

What not to do

Three interventions are popular, expensive, and generally counterproductive.

Buying surveillance and calling it a productivity strategy. Activity monitoring answers where time goes. It does not make the work faster, and the trust cost arrives on day one while any benefit arrives slowly. If you have a specific question that needs the data, write the question down first, then read our employee productivity monitoring playbook before you install anything. There are also things it should never collect, which we cover in the privacy line.

Ranking individuals on activity scores. A score built from clicks, keystrokes, or hours logged rewards visible motion and penalises reading, thinking, and time on the phone, which is most of what senior work looks like.

Running a wellbeing programme instead of fixing the workload. The APA found that 59% of workers believe employers overestimate how healthy their workplace is. A resilience workshop scheduled on top of an unmanageable week confirms that gap rather than closing it.

Expect the numbers to get worse first

When you stop rewarding visible busyness, visible busyness falls before real output rises. Throughput counts often dip in the first weeks because people stop splitting work artificially and stop rushing low value items to keep a number moving. If you judge the change on those counts you will reverse it at exactly the wrong moment. Judge it on quality, on delivery predictability, and on whether the work that matters is now getting finished.

Key takeaways

  • Productivity is a ratio. Lowering the input is faster and more reliable than raising the output, and it is the half most plans ignore.
  • Fragmentation is the binding constraint for most knowledge teams. Microsoft recorded interruptions roughly every two minutes, about 275 a day, in 2025.
  • Half of all meetings land in the two windows where people concentrate best. Moving them is structural, cheap, and reversible.
  • More hours makes the ratio worse. The APA found 45% of US workers already work more hours than they want, and 67% reported a burnout outcome in the last month.
  • Managers are where interventions succeed or die, and Gallup found the 2025 engagement decline was sharpest among them.
  • Measure to notice change, not to rank people. Anything you target, you will eventually corrupt.
  • Monitoring software tells you where time went. It does not make the work faster, and it costs trust immediately.

Frequently asked questions

What is the fastest way to improve employee productivity?

Cut recurring meetings and the expectation of instant replies, in that order. Both are decisions a manager can make this week without budget or software, and both act directly on the interruption load that Microsoft's 2025 telemetry describes. Every other lever on this page takes longer to show up.

How can I increase employee productivity without adding hours?

Adding hours is usually the thing making it worse. The American Psychological Association's 2024 Work in America survey found 45% of employed US adults already work more hours per week than they want to. Productivity is output divided by input, so shrinking the input while protecting the output improves the ratio. Removing interruptions, meetings, and duplicated approvals does that. Longer days do the opposite.

Does employee monitoring software improve productivity?

It can tell you where time goes, which is genuinely useful for diagnosis. It does not make the work faster by itself, and it carries a cost in trust that lands immediately while any benefit arrives slowly. Use it to answer a specific question you have written down in advance, then act on the answer. Buying it as a general productivity fix is the most common way this category disappoints.

What is the biggest cause of low employee productivity?

Fragmentation, on the current evidence. Microsoft's June 2025 Work Trend Index reported that knowledge workers are interrupted roughly every two minutes during work hours, about 275 times a day, and that 48% of employees and 52% of leaders describe their work as chaotic and fragmented. Under those conditions the constraint is rarely effort. It is the absence of any uninterrupted stretch long enough to finish something.

How long does it take to see productivity improvements?

Calendar changes show up within two or three weeks, because they change the shape of the day immediately. Changes to how work is assigned or reviewed take a quarter. Changes to management behavior take longer still and are the hardest to attribute. Expect a dip first: people stop performing busyness before they start producing more, and the numbers reflect that order.

Should I set productivity targets for my team?

Measure without targeting where you can. Goodhart's Law describes what happens otherwise: once a measure becomes a target it stops being a good measure, because people optimise the number rather than the thing it stood for. Counts of tickets, commits, or calls are especially easy to inflate. Track them to notice change, and judge outcomes on quality and predictability instead.

How do I improve productivity for remote employees?

The same levers apply, with one addition: write things down. Remote teams lose more to waiting for an answer than to anyone working less. A randomised controlled trial of 1,612 employees at Trip.com, published in Nature in June 2024, found that hybrid working cut quit rates by about a third with no measurable impact on performance reviews or promotions, which suggests the arrangement itself is not the problem worth solving.

Does improving engagement improve productivity?

They travel together, though the direction of cause is not settled. Gallup's Q12 meta-analysis, covering 112,312 business units across 96 countries, found that units in the top quartile for engagement saw 18% higher productivity measured as sales and 23% higher profitability than units in the bottom quartile. That is a correlation between quartiles, not a promise that running an engagement programme produces those numbers.

What should I stop doing first?

The status meeting that exists so someone can report progress that is already written down somewhere. It costs the whole team a fragment of their morning, it usually sits inside the hours people concentrate best, and cancelling it is reversible. If nobody notices within a month, that is your answer.
