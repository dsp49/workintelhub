Recording Conversations at Work: Consent Laws by State

P Pavan
November 12, 2026 · 14 min read

Whether a recording made at work is legal depends on one question in most states and two in a few: did at least one party to the conversation consent, or did every party have to? Federal law and 38 states plus the District of Columbia follow the one-party rule, under which a person may record a conversation they are part of. Eleven states require everyone's consent for private conversations, and a handful apply different rules to phone calls and in-person talk. The answer decides whether an employee's phone in a pocket during a performance meeting is evidence or a felony, and whether a company's call recording needs a disclosure to the customer as well as the agent.

This guide covers the federal rule, the full state table with statutes and penalties, the National Labor Relations Board's position on employees recording managers and on no-recording policies, how employers record calls and video meetings lawfully, what the 2025 and 2026 legislative sessions changed (nothing, so far), and policy language. For how cameras fit in, see workplace video surveillance laws by state; for AI meeting bots specifically, see AI notetaker recording consent.

The federal rule

Title III of the Omnibus Crime Control and Safe Streets Act, as amended by the Electronic Communications Privacy Act, makes it a crime to intentionally intercept any wire, oral or electronic communication (18 U.S.C. 2511). Two exceptions matter at work. The consent exception, 2511(2)(d), permits interception when one party to the communication has given prior consent, so a person may record their own conversations. The business-extension exception, 2510(5)(a)(i), excludes ordinary telephone equipment used in the ordinary course of business, which is the basis for employer call monitoring. Penalties are up to five years in prison and a civil action for the greater of actual damages or $100 a day or $10,000, plus punitive damages and fees (18 U.S.C. 2520).

Federal law is a floor. States may be stricter, and when a conversation crosses state lines the recorder is exposed to the law of either state; courts have applied the law of the place where the recording was made and the law of the place where the other party was located, so the safe rule for interstate calls is the stricter of the two.

One-party and all-party states

All-party consent for private conversations (11 states): California, Delaware (under its privacy statute), Florida, Illinois, Maryland, Massachusetts, Michigan (by statute text, with a judge-made participant exception), Montana, New Hampshire, Pennsylvania and Washington. In these states every participant must consent before a private conversation is recorded, whether in person or by phone. Consent can be express or shown by continuing to talk after a clear announcement; Washington's statute says a recorded announcement suffices.

Mixed rules: Connecticut is one-party criminally but gives a civil action for phone calls recorded without all parties' consent. Nevada is one-party in person and all-party for phone calls. Missouri and Hawaii are one-party for phone calls but all-party for in-person conversations in private places. Oregon is one-party for phone, and its in-person rule requiring all participants be informed was struck down by a Ninth Circuit panel in 2023 and then reheard by the full court; check the current status. Maine is one-party except in bathrooms and dressing rooms.

One-party (the rest): 38 states and the District of Columbia allow a participant to record. Vermont has no state statute, so federal law governs. In every state, recording a conversation you are not part of, without any party's consent, is a crime.

"Private" matters in all-party states. Most of their statutes protect only communications in which the parties reasonably expect privacy, so a shouted argument on a shop floor or a company all-hands may fall outside them, while a one-on-one in a closed office is squarely inside. California's Penal Code 632 uses the word "confidential"; Illinois's 2014 rewrite uses "private conversation" after the earlier blanket statute was struck down.

When an employee records a manager

In a one-party state, an employee who records a meeting they attend has not broken the wiretap law. Whether they have broken a workplace rule, and whether the recording helps them, are separate questions.

The National Labor Relations Board held in Whole Foods Market (363 NLRB No. 87, December 2015) that a blanket ban on recording in the workplace violated the National Labor Relations Act because employees could read it as prohibiting recordings of protected concerted activity, such as documenting unsafe conditions or inconsistent application of rules. The Second Circuit enforced that order in June 2017 but noted that an employer may "craft a policy that places some limits on recording." The Board's Boeing decision (2017) then made no-recording rules presumptively lawful, and its Stericycle decision (August 2023) swung back to asking whether a reasonable employee, economically dependent on the employer, would read the rule as chilling protected activity. Stericycle was still the standard in late 2026, although the General Counsel asked the Board in August 2026 to overturn it in a pending case. For now, a no-recording policy needs a legitimate justification and should be written narrowly.

Two limits apply even in one-party states. First, the NLRA protects concerted activity, so an employee recording solely for a personal grievance is on weaker ground than one documenting conditions on behalf of colleagues. Second, a recording made in violation of state law is generally not protected by the NLRA, so in an all-party state the employee can be lawfully disciplined for the recording itself.

Courts in employment cases have admitted employee recordings made lawfully and have excluded or penalized recordings made in violation of all-party statutes; in California, an unlawful recording is inadmissible under Penal Code 632(d) and exposes the employee to the $5,000-per-violation civil penalty, which employers have counterclaimed. In the Vrdolyak Law Group litigation in Illinois, by contrast, it was former employees suing the firm for allegedly recording their calls; the court in May 2026 sent to a jury the question whether the interceptions were intentional.

No-recording policies and the NLRB

A policy that survives Stericycle typically: (1) states the business reasons (confidentiality of customer and health information, trade secrets, open discussion in meetings, privacy of coworkers); (2) prohibits recording of conversations, meetings and phone calls without the consent of all participants, in the workplace and in work-related communications; (3) expressly says it does not restrict employees' rights under Section 7 of the NLRA to discuss terms and conditions of employment or to document them for that purpose; (4) excludes recordings required by law or authorized for business purposes (call-center QA, training); and (5) applies to managers as well as employees. In all-party states the policy can simply track the statute. In one-party states it is a workplace rule, enforceable by discipline but not by the criminal law.

Non-union employers are covered by the NLRA too; only supervisors and managers fall outside Section 7, so a policy may lawfully restrict a manager's recording in ways it cannot restrict an hourly employee's.

When the employer records calls and meetings

Phone calls. Under federal law the employer needs the consent of one party; the employee's consent is established by a written, acknowledged policy and periodic reminders, not by the mere knowledge that monitoring is possible (Watkins v. L.M. Berry, 1983; Deal v. Spears, 1992). The business-extension exception allows monitoring in the ordinary course of business, but under Watkins a supervisor must stop listening once a call is clearly personal. In the eleven all-party states, and in Connecticut and Nevada for phone calls, the person on the other end must also consent, which is why recorded-line announcements exist. California's Kight v. CashCall (2011) held that a supervisor silently monitoring a customer call can violate Penal Code 632 even though employed by the same company, and that a one-time warning at the start of the relationship did not cover later calls. CIPA class actions over call recording have produced large settlements in 2025, including a $19.5 million Wells Fargo settlement, so an announcement on every recorded inbound and outbound call to or from California is the norm. For the detailed rules, see can my employer listen to my phone calls.

Video meetings. Zoom, Teams and Meet recordings are governed by the same statutes; there is no meeting-specific law. The in-app banner that a recording has started, combined with the participant's choice to remain, is treated as consent in one-party states and as adequate notice in all-party states if it is visible to everyone, including dial-in participants, who do not see the banner and should hear an announcement. AI note-takers that join as a participant raise the same issue and have drawn class actions in California; a July 2026 survey found a third of US workers had an AI note-taker in a meeting and most were not consistently asked first.

Voicemail and stored recordings. Reading stored voicemail on the company's own system falls under the Stored Communications Act's provider exception (18 U.S.C. 2701(c)(1)). Accessing an employee's personal account from a returned device does not; Lazette v. Kulmatycki (2013) allowed a claim where an employer read an ex-employee's personal Gmail from a returned company phone.

Remote workers and which state's law applies

A remote employee in Pennsylvania on a call with a manager in Texas is a conversation between an all-party state and a one-party state. The manager who records relying on Texas law can be liable under Pennsylvania law if the employee did not consent, because Pennsylvania courts apply their statute to recordings of people located in Pennsylvania. The practical rules for distributed teams: treat every call as if the strictest participant's state applies; announce recording on every recorded call and meeting; and in employee-facing policies, obtain written consent to call and meeting recording at hiring and again when the policy changes. Employers in Connecticut, Delaware and New York must also give the statutory notice of electronic monitoring before monitoring phone calls at all; see employee monitoring laws by state.

What changed in 2024 to 2026

No state adopted a new all-party rule in 2024, 2025 or 2026. Bills to move to all-party consent died in New Jersey (A6186, January 2026), Alaska (SB 85 and HB 86) and New Hampshire (HB 1508, February 2026); New York's S5070 remained in committee; a New Jersey bill was reintroduced in 2026. Maryland legislators discussed loosening the state's all-party law to account for doorbell cameras and phones but did not pass a change. Oregon's in-person rule remained in litigation. The movement was in enforcement rather than legislation: California CIPA suits over call recording and AI call-handling (ConverseNow's motion to dismiss was denied in August 2025) and over AI meeting bots, and the NLRB General Counsel's August 2026 request to revisit Stericycle.

If you are the employee

Before you press record in a meeting, check your state in the table. In a one-party state you may record a conversation you are part of, but a workplace no-recording policy can still be enforced by discipline, so weigh what the recording is for. If it documents unsafe conditions, discrimination or inconsistent discipline on behalf of coworkers, the NLRA may protect you; if it is for a personal dispute, it may cost you your job even where it is legal. In an all-party state, do not record without announcing it; the recording is likely inadmissible and you may face a civil penalty or a criminal charge, and the employer may lawfully fire you for it. Written notes made during or immediately after the meeting, emailed to yourself with a timestamp, are admissible everywhere and carry no legal risk. If you believe the employer is recording you without consent in an all-party state, the phone call guide explains the remedies, and the how to ask HR guide has a template for asking what is recorded.

Key takeaways

  • Federal law and 38 states plus DC follow the one-party rule: a participant may record their own conversation. Eleven states require all parties' consent for private conversations: California, Delaware (privacy statute), Florida, Illinois, Maryland, Massachusetts, Michigan (disputed), Montana, New Hampshire, Pennsylvania and Washington.
  • Mixed rules: Connecticut (civil liability for phone calls), Nevada (all-party for phone only), Missouri and Hawaii (all-party in person in private places), Oregon (in-person rule in litigation), Maine (bathrooms and dressing rooms). Vermont has no statute.
  • Penalties are serious in all-party states: California up to $2,500 and a year in jail per violation plus $5,000 civil per violation; Florida and Pennsylvania felonies up to 5 and 7 years; Illinois a Class 4 felony.
  • An employee who records a manager in a one-party state has not broken the law but may breach a no-recording policy; the NLRB's Stericycle standard (still in force in late 2026, under challenge) requires such policies to be narrow and to carve out protected concerted activity.
  • Employers may record calls with employees' written, acknowledged consent and the business-extension exception, but must stop listening to clearly personal calls, and in all-party states must notify the other party on every call; California CIPA suits produced a $19.5 million settlement in 2025.
  • For interstate calls and remote teams, apply the stricter state's rule, announce recording on every call and meeting, and get written consent at hiring.
  • No state changed its consent rule in 2024 to 2026; bills in New Jersey, Alaska, New Hampshire and New York died or stalled, and the action has been in litigation over AI call handling and meeting bots.

Frequently asked questions

Is it legal to record a conversation at work?

In 38 states and DC, yes, if you are a participant in the conversation, under the one-party rule. In California, Florida, Illinois, Maryland, Massachusetts, Michigan, Montana, New Hampshire, Pennsylvania, Washington and under Delaware's privacy law, every participant must consent to recording a private conversation. Recording a conversation you are not part of is illegal everywhere. A legal recording can still violate a workplace policy.

Which states are two-party consent states?

The usual list is California, Delaware, Florida, Illinois, Maryland, Massachusetts, Michigan, Montana, New Hampshire, Pennsylvania and Washington; the accurate term is all-party, since every participant must consent. Connecticut and Nevada apply all-party rules to phone calls only, and Missouri and Hawaii to in-person conversations in private places. Michigan's statute reads as all-party but its courts let a participant record.

Can I secretly record my boss?

In a one-party state you may lawfully record a conversation you are part of, but your employer can discipline you under a no-recording policy unless the recording documents matters protected by the National Labor Relations Act, such as unsafe conditions or discrimination raised on behalf of coworkers. In an all-party state a secret recording is a crime, is usually inadmissible, and can be grounds for termination and a civil penalty.

Can my employer fire me for recording a meeting?

Often, yes. A reasonable no-recording policy is lawful if it is narrow and does not prohibit protected concerted activity, and violating it is misconduct. The NLRB has found blanket bans unlawful (Whole Foods, 2015) and judges policies under its 2023 Stericycle standard, so an employee recording to document terms and conditions of employment with coworkers has a defense; an employee recording for a purely personal dispute generally does not.

Can an employer record employee phone calls?

Yes, with notice. Under federal law one party's consent is enough, and a written, acknowledged policy supplies the employee's consent; the business-extension exception also allows monitoring in the ordinary course of business, but supervisors must stop listening once a call is clearly personal. In all-party states, and in Connecticut and Nevada for phone calls, the customer or other party must also be notified, which is why recorded-line announcements are used.

Do Zoom and Teams recordings require consent?

They are governed by the same wiretap laws as any recording. In one-party states the host's consent suffices, though a visible banner is standard. In all-party states every participant must consent; the recording banner plus a verbal announcement for dial-in participants is treated as adequate notice, and participants who stay are taken to consent. AI note-takers that join meetings have drawn lawsuits in California for recording without consent.

What is the penalty for recording someone without consent?

It varies. Federal law: up to 5 years and civil damages of at least $10,000 or $100 a day. California: up to $2,500 and a year in jail per violation plus $5,000 civil per violation. Florida: third-degree felony up to 5 years. Pennsylvania: up to 7 years. Illinois: Class 4 felony. Washington: gross misdemeanor plus civil damages. Many one-party states also criminalize recording conversations you are not part of.

Which state's law applies to a call between two states?

Potentially both. Courts have applied the law of the state where the recording was made and the law of the state where the other party was located, so a recorder in a one-party state can be liable under the all-party law of the person on the other end. The safe rule for interstate calls and remote teams is to follow the stricter state and announce the recording.
