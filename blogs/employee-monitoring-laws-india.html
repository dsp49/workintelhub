Employee Monitoring Laws in India: DPDP Act and Rules 2025

P Pavan
November 20, 2026 · 10 min read

India has no law written specifically for workplace monitoring. The rules come from the Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023 (DPDP Act), which applies to digital personal data including employees' data, and from the Digital Personal Data Protection Rules 2025, notified on November 13, 2025. The Act lets employers process employee data without consent for employment purposes, but every other duty still applies: notice where required, security safeguards, accuracy, purpose limits, erasure, breach notification and respect for employees' rights. Most of these substantive duties take effect 18 months after the Rules were notified, in May 2027, so employers are in a transition period. Until then, the older rules under section 43A of the Information Technology Act 2000 continue to apply.

This guide explains the DPDP framework as it applies to monitoring, the timeline, the constitutional right to privacy, what employers typically do in practice, and a readiness checklist. For other countries, see the UK, EU and US guides.

The DPDP Act and employee data

The DPDP Act applies to the processing of digital personal data in India, and of data processed outside India in connection with offering goods or services to people in India. It has no carve-out for employee data. An employer processing employees' personal data is a data fiduciary and employees are data principals. Monitoring tools that capture activity, screenshots, keystrokes, browsing, location or biometrics are therefore processing personal data and must comply with the Act. Paper records that are never digitized fall outside it.

The employment legitimate use (section 7(i))

The Act generally requires consent (section 6) given after a notice (section 5), but section 7 lists "certain legitimate uses" for which consent is not needed. Section 7(i) covers processing "for the purposes of employment or those related to safeguarding the employer from loss or liability, such as prevention of corporate espionage, maintenance of confidentiality of trade secrets, intellectual property, classified information or provision of any service or benefit sought by a Data Principal who is an employee."

This is broader than the legitimate interest test in Europe, because there is no balancing test written into the provision. But it has limits:

  • "Employment purposes" is not defined, so monitoring must be plausibly connected to managing employment or protecting the employer, not to unrelated purposes such as tracking employees' personal lives.
  • Processing beyond those purposes needs consent, and consent must be free, specific, informed, unconditional and unambiguous; a clause in an appointment letter is unlikely to qualify.
  • The Data Protection Board may still examine whether processing claimed under section 7(i) was genuinely for those purposes.

Duties that still apply

  • Security safeguards (section 8(5)): reasonable safeguards to prevent breaches; failure carries the highest penalty, up to ₹250 crore.
  • Breach notification (section 8(6)): notify the Board and affected individuals of personal data breaches; penalty up to ₹200 crore.
  • Accuracy, purpose and erasure: data must be complete and accurate where used for decisions, and erased when the purpose is served unless retention is required by law.
  • Rights of data principals: access to a summary of data processed (section 11), correction and erasure (section 12), grievance redress (section 13) and nomination (section 14). Employees can use these to learn what monitoring data is held about them.
  • Significant data fiduciaries, if designated, have further duties including data protection impact assessments and audits (penalty up to ₹150 crore).
  • Other breaches: up to ₹50 crore.

The Data Protection Board of India, a four-member body operating digitally from the National Capital Region, adjudicates complaints, with appeals to the Telecom Disputes Settlement and Appellate Tribunal.

Sources differ by a day or two on the exact commencement dates; check the Gazette notification. Section 72A of the IT Act, which penalizes disclosure of personal information in breach of a lawful contract, is unaffected. Until May 2027 the 2011 rules require a privacy policy and written consent for collecting sensitive personal data such as passwords, financial information, health data and biometrics, which already affects biometric attendance and monitoring tools.

The constitutional right to privacy

In Justice K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017), a nine-judge Supreme Court bench held that privacy is a fundamental right under Article 21, and that intrusions must meet tests of legality, legitimate aim and proportionality. The judgment binds the state directly; how far it constrains private employers is unsettled, though it informs how courts read statutes such as the DPDP Act. There is little Indian case law squarely on private-employer monitoring, and no statutory retention period for monitoring data such as screenshots or activity logs.

Common practice and where it falls short

Indian employers, particularly in IT services, business process outsourcing and global capability centers serving foreign clients, commonly monitor heavily: endpoint security, screen and activity tracking, USB and print controls, and biometric attendance, often required by client contracts. In ADP's 2025 People at Work survey, India had the highest share of workers worldwide who said they felt monitored. The typical legal basis has been a consent clause in the appointment letter and an IT or acceptable use policy. Under the DPDP Act that approach is weak: a contractual clause is not free and specific consent. The better footing is section 7(i), supported by a clear notice of what is monitored and why, and limits that keep monitoring within employment and security purposes.

A readiness checklist

  1. Inventory every tool that processes employee data: HRMS, payroll, attendance and biometrics, endpoint security, activity monitoring, email and chat archiving, CCTV with digital storage.
  2. Map each to a purpose and legal basis: section 7(i) for employment and loss-prevention purposes; separate consent for anything else.
  3. Publish a clear employee privacy notice covering monitoring, even where consent is not needed.
  4. Minimize: disable features not needed for the purpose, such as webcam capture or continuous screenshots.
  5. Set retention periods and erase data when the purpose is served.
  6. Implement security safeguards and a breach-notification process to the Board and employees.
  7. Prepare to answer access, correction and erasure requests and to run a grievance process.
  8. Review vendor contracts for processor obligations, and client contracts that require monitoring.
  9. Until May 2027, keep complying with the 2011 sensitive data rules for biometrics and passwords.

Key takeaways

  • India's DPDP Act 2023 applies to employee data with no carve-out; employers are data fiduciaries and monitoring is processing of personal data.
  • Section 7(i) allows processing without consent for employment purposes and to protect the employer from loss or liability, such as preventing espionage and protecting trade secrets, but processing beyond those purposes needs free, specific consent.
  • Security safeguards, breach notification, accuracy, erasure and employees' rights of access, correction, erasure and grievance still apply; penalties reach ₹250 crore for security failures.
  • The DPDP Rules were notified November 13, 2025; most substantive duties apply from May 2027, and IT Act section 43A and the 2011 rules apply until then.
  • Puttaswamy (2017) made privacy a fundamental right; its effect on private employers is unsettled and there is little case law on workplace monitoring.
  • Consent clauses in appointment letters are weak under the DPDP Act; rely on section 7(i) with clear notice, minimization, retention limits and security.

Frequently asked questions

Is employee monitoring legal in India?

Yes. The DPDP Act 2023 permits employers to process employee personal data without consent for employment purposes and to protect against loss or liability, under section 7(i). Monitoring must stay within those purposes and comply with the Act's security, accuracy, erasure and rights obligations.

Do Indian employers need employee consent for monitoring?

Not for monitoring that genuinely serves employment purposes or protects the employer against loss or liability, which section 7(i) treats as a legitimate use. Processing for other purposes needs consent that is free, specific, informed and unambiguous, which a clause in an appointment letter usually is not.

When does the DPDP Act apply to employers?

The Rules were notified on November 13, 2025. Board provisions applied immediately, consent manager provisions after 12 months, and the main substantive duties, including notice, consent, legitimate uses and penalties, after 18 months, in May 2027. Until then, IT Act section 43A and the 2011 rules apply.

What are the penalties under the DPDP Act?

Up to ₹250 crore for failing to take reasonable security safeguards, ₹200 crore for failing to notify a breach or for breaching children's-data duties, ₹150 crore for significant data fiduciary breaches, and ₹50 crore for other breaches, imposed by the Data Protection Board.

Can employees ask what monitoring data their employer holds?

Yes, once the substantive provisions apply in May 2027. Section 11 gives data principals a right to a summary of their personal data being processed, and sections 12 and 13 give rights to correction, erasure and grievance redress.

Is biometric attendance legal in India?

Yes, with care. Until May 2027 the 2011 sensitive personal data rules require a privacy policy and written consent for collecting biometrics. Under the DPDP Act, biometric attendance can rely on the employment legitimate use but must be secured, limited to its purpose and erased when no longer needed.
