Best Clock In Clock Out Apps: 12 Compared (2026)

P Pavan
October 21, 2026 · 17 min read

A clock in clock out app records when an hourly employee starts and stops work, from a phone, a browser or a shared tablet, and turns the punches into a timesheet for payroll. The twelve below differ on four things that matter more than their feature lists: how people punch (GPS on their own phone, a PIN or face at a kiosk, or only inside a geofence), whether breaks and overtime are enforced or merely reported, which payroll system the hours reach, and whether the price is per employee, per location or a base fee plus per user. Jibble is free for unlimited users with a face kiosk and GPS. OnTheClock is the cheapest paid clock with geofencing and seven named payroll integrations. Homebase is free for a single site of ten.

The pages that rank for this search are written by Connecteam, Timeero and Clockify, each ranking itself first, with prices that disagree with the vendors' own pages and no compliance section. This guide gives October 2026 pricing read from each vendor's pricing page, free-plan limits, a chooser by crew and payroll, and the four legal rules that decide how a time clock is configured: FLSA recordkeeping and retention, the rounding regulation, state meal-break laws and Illinois' biometric law. This page covers the apps people punch on, from a phone or a shared tablet; our attendance tracking software review covers the wider systems, hardware clocks and payroll suites included, that those punches feed. There are no vendor links and nothing paid for its place.

What the law requires of a time clock

A clock in clock out app is a legal record for non-exempt employees. Four federal and state rules decide how you configure it.

Records: 29 CFR 516.2 and retention

The Fair Labor Standards Act's recordkeeping regulation, 29 CFR 516.2(a), requires employers to keep, for each non-exempt employee, the hours worked each workday and the total hours each workweek, alongside pay rates, earnings and deductions. It does not prescribe a method; an app is fine, a notebook is fine, an inaccurate record is not. Payroll records must be kept for three years under 29 CFR 516.5 and the time cards themselves for two years under 29 CFR 516.6. Choose an app whose export you will still be able to open in three years, and export at each year end.

Rounding: 29 CFR 785.48

The Department of Labor's regulation allows recording time to the nearest five minutes, tenth of an hour or quarter hour, provided the practice does not, over time, fail to compensate employees for all the time they actually worked. The widely quoted seven-minute rule is the arithmetic of quarter-hour rounding, not a legal requirement, and rounding is never mandatory. Several apps round by default; the safest setting is no rounding, and if you round, round neutrally and audit the net effect each quarter. Our time card calculator covers the arithmetic.

Breaks: six states that make break punches matter

Federal law requires no meal break, but California (30 minutes for work over five hours, with an hour's pay owed for each missed break), Illinois (20 minutes within the first five hours of a 7.5-hour shift), New York (30 minutes for shifts over six hours spanning midday), Washington (30 minutes for over five hours), Oregon (30 minutes for six to eight hours) and Colorado (30 minutes over five hours) do, and a timestamped break record is the defense. Apps that auto-deduct a break without a punch create liability in those states; Workyard, Timeero and Deputy enforce break rules, Jibble calculates paid breaks, and Clockify and Homebase record them.

Biometric kiosks: Illinois BIPA

A facial recognition or fingerprint kiosk collects a biometric identifier. Illinois' Biometric Information Privacy Act, 740 ILCS 14, requires a written retention and destruction policy, informed written consent before collection and destruction when the purpose ends or within three years of the last interaction. Damages are $1,000 per negligent and $5,000 per intentional violation, and employers have paid settlements in the tens of millions over time clocks. Since August 2, 2024 a person can recover once per method of collection rather than per scan. Texas and Washington have their own biometric laws. Jibble, Deputy, Timeero and QuickBooks Time offer face recognition; our biometric time clock entry covers the consent forms.

GPS and monitoring notice

GPS punches are location data. New York Civil Rights Law 52-c requires written notice, acknowledged on hiring, of electronic monitoring of telephone, email and internet use; whether it reaches GPS in a time clock app is unsettled, and the notice costs nothing, so give it. Connecticut and Delaware have similar notice laws. Configure the app to capture location only at punch time or only while clocked in, and say so in the policy; our employee GPS tracking guide and monitoring laws by state table cover the rest.

Buddy punching and time theft, with the numbers that exist

The statistics that circulate on vendor pages, that 75% of businesses are affected by time theft or that buddy punching costs 2.2% of payroll, are attributed to an American Payroll Association survey that no page links and that we could not locate. Treat them as folklore. One figure with a stated method exists: OnTheClock's survey of more than 800 US hourly workers, fielded July 1 to 9, 2026, found about one in four admitting to clocking in for a coworker, with a stated margin of error of about three points. It is a vendor survey of its own audience, unweighted, and still the best number available. The product answer is the same regardless of the number: a photo or face capture at the kiosk, GPS or geofencing on mobile punches, and an edit trail managers can see. The behavioral answer is a schedule people can keep and a manager who notices.

Key takeaways

  • Decide the punch method first: GPS on personal phones, a kiosk with PIN, photo or face, or geofenced clock-in. It narrows twelve apps to four.
  • Free: Jibble (unlimited users, face kiosk, GPS), Homebase (10 employees, one location), Connecteam (10 users), Clockify (5 users). Sling's free plan schedules but does not clock.
  • Cheapest paid: Sling $1.70, When I Work $2.50, Clockify $3.99, OnTheClock $4 plus a $5 base, Timeero and Deputy $5. QuickBooks Time is $20 or $40 base plus $10 or $12.
  • Payroll: OnTheClock names QuickBooks, Gusto, ADP, Paychex, Paylocity, Sage and SurePayroll; Workyard exports to QuickBooks, ADP and Gusto on Starter; Homebase and Deputy run their own payroll; QuickBooks Time is native to QuickBooks.
  • Breaks enforced rather than reported: Workyard on Starter, Timeero's California policy on Pro, Deputy's compliance rules on Lite.
  • The FLSA requires daily and weekly hours kept two years for time cards and three for payroll; rounding to the quarter hour is allowed only if neutral over time; six states mandate meal breaks; Illinois requires written consent and a retention policy before a face or fingerprint kiosk.
  • The only buddy-punching figure with a stated method is a July 2026 vendor survey of 800 hourly workers in which about one in four admitted it; the 75% and 2.2% figures have no traceable source.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best clock in clock out app?

Jibble for most small teams: free for unlimited users with GPS stamps, a facial recognition kiosk, overtime rules and QuickBooks export. OnTheClock if you want geofencing, a kiosk and named payroll integrations for $5 plus $4 per employee. Homebase for a single-site shift business. Timeero or Workyard for crews that drive. The chooser above ranks all twelve.

Is there a free clock in clock out app?

Yes. Jibble is free for unlimited users including the kiosk and GPS. Homebase is free for up to 10 employees at one location, Connecteam for up to 10 users, and Clockify for 5 users with a kiosk from its $3.99 plan. Sling is free for 30 users but its free plan does not include time tracking.

Is a time clock app legal for FLSA recordkeeping?

Yes. 29 CFR 516.2 requires accurate records of hours worked each day and week for non-exempt employees but does not prescribe a method. Keep time cards for two years and payroll records for three, and export the app's data at least yearly so you can open it after the subscription ends.

Can I round clock-in times?

Yes, under 29 CFR 785.48, to the nearest five minutes, tenth of an hour or quarter hour, provided the practice does not over time fail to pay employees for all time worked. The seven-minute rule is quarter-hour rounding's arithmetic, not a requirement. Rounding is never mandatory and no rounding is the safest setting.

Is facial recognition clock-in legal?

In most states yes, but Illinois' Biometric Information Privacy Act requires a written retention and destruction policy and informed written consent before collection, with damages of $1,000 to $5,000 per violation, and Texas and Washington have biometric laws of their own. Jibble, Deputy, Timeero (Premium) and QuickBooks Time offer face recognition; get the consent form signed first.

Can my employer track my GPS through a time clock app?

Employers can require GPS at punch time or while clocked in on a work app. New York requires written notice of electronic monitoring on hiring, Connecticut and Delaware have notice laws, and tracking should stop when you are off the clock. The app's settings determine whether location is captured only at punches or continuously; ask which.

Which clock in apps enforce meal breaks?

Workyard enforces state, federal or custom break and overtime rules from its Starter plan, with automatic meal-break handling on Autopilot. Timeero has a California break policy on Pro. Deputy lists labor law compliance on Lite. Jibble calculates paid breaks and overtime. Homebase and Clockify record breaks on paid plans. QuickBooks Time sends overtime alerts rather than enforcing rules.

How common is buddy punching?

The one survey with a stated method, OnTheClock's July 2026 poll of more than 800 US hourly workers, found about one in four admitting to clocking in for a coworker. It is a vendor survey of its own audience. The 75% of businesses and 2.2% of payroll figures repeated on vendor pages have no traceable primary source. Photo or face capture at a kiosk and GPS on mobile punches are the product answers.
