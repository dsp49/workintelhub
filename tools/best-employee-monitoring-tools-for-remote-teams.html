Best Employee Monitoring Tools for Remote Teams (2026)

P Pavan
October 6, 2026 · 17 min read

Monitoring a remote team is a different problem from monitoring an office. The devices are on home networks, sometimes personal, sometimes Linux; the hours cross time zones; the people are often contractors paid for the hours they log; and the manager's real worry is less often "are they working" than "are they working all night". The tools that do well here are the ones built for distributed teams from the start: they track offline and sync later, they put payroll and invoicing next to the time, they let each team decide its own screenshot policy, and they report on overwork as readily as on idleness.

I reviewed eight tools against that list, with pricing checked in October 2026. The first three are the ones I would trial first; the remaining five each fit a particular kind of remote team. The main monitoring review covers the full field of twelve including the security products; this page is about the remote case.

The tools in detail

Hubstaff

Time, proof of work and payment across every platform
Runs on Windows, macOS, Linux, Chromebook, iOS, Android Pricing (October 2026) Starter $4.99, Grow $7.50, Team $10, Enterprise $25 per seat per month billed annually ($7, $9 and $12 monthly); 2-seat minimum; 14-day trial

What it is

A time tracker for distributed teams with screenshots, activity levels, GPS, payroll and invoicing, on every desktop and mobile platform.

Why it is on this list

Hubstaff tops the remote list because remote teams are where it was born and where its compromises make sense. Time is tracked against projects whether the person is online or not and synced later; screenshots are taken at an interval each team sets, blurred if policy requires; activity is measured without keystroke content; and the hours flow to payroll in the contractor's currency. It runs on every platform a distributed team is likely to have, including Linux and Chromebook, and its mobile apps cover the people on the road. The activity percentage is the thing to de-emphasise in how you manage with it.

Where it is strong

  • Time tracking, screenshots, GPS, payroll and invoicing in one product
  • Runs everywhere, including Linux and Chromebook, with mobile apps
  • Over 2,300 verified reviews and a long track record with distributed teams
  • Clear per-seat pricing with a cheap entry plan

Where it falls short

  • Activity percentages reward mouse movement, not work, and people know it
  • Two-seat minimum and add-ons on the lower plans
  • Monitoring depth is modest by design; not a security tool

Time Doctor

Remote tracking with work-life balance reporting
Runs on Windows, macOS, Linux, Chromebook, Android Pricing (October 2026) Basic $6.70, Standard $11.70, Premium $16.70 per user per month billed annually ($8, $14, $20 monthly); Enterprise by quote; 14-day trial

What it is

Remote-team time tracking with screenshots, web and app use, payroll, work-life balance reporting and an Office versus Remote comparison.

Why it is on this list

Time Doctor is second because it was built for the same situation from the employer's side, outsourcing companies managing remote staff, and has two features nobody else matches for a remote team: work-life balance reporting that flags late nights and overlong days, and the Office versus Remote comparison. Both change the conversation from "are they working" to "are they working too much", which is the more common remote problem. The real price is the Standard plan, where payroll and integrations live.

Where it is strong

  • Built for remote and outsourced teams, with payroll and 60-plus integrations
  • Work-life balance and Office versus Remote reporting
  • Screen video and two years of history on Premium
  • Chromebook and Android agents alongside the desktop platforms

Where it falls short

  • Integrations and payroll start at the $11.70 Standard plan
  • Screenshots and activity scoring draw the usual objections from staff
  • Interface shows its age in places

Insightful

Analytics with location insight and long retention
Runs on Windows, macOS, Linux Pricing (October 2026) Workforce Analytics $8, Workflow Optimization $12 (beta), both together $16 per seat per month billed annually; Enterprise by quote; screen recording and on-demand screenshots $4 add-ons; 7-day trial

What it is

Workforce analytics with utilisation, capacity and work location insights, 24 months of history, and screen recording as an optional add-on.

Why it is on this list

Insightful is third for remote knowledge teams that want to see utilisation and capacity rather than screenshots. Its work location insights tell you which days were remote without anyone reporting it, retention is 24 months, the Linux agent covers engineers, and screen recording is an add-on you can decline. It is the analytics choice for a team that is remote by design rather than by circumstance.

Where it is strong

  • Work location insights that separate office, remote and hybrid days
  • Capacity and utilisation reporting, with screen recording as an add-on rather than a default
  • 24 months of retention and on-premise at scale
  • Among the highest user ratings in the category

Where it falls short

  • Screen recording and on-demand screenshots are $4 add-ons each
  • Workflow Optimization is still in beta
  • On-premise needs 250 employees

ActivTrak

Workload and focus data without keystrokes
Runs on Windows, macOS, Chromebook Pricing (October 2026) Free for up to 3 people (30-day retention); Work Activity Tracking $10, Workforce Management $15, Productivity Optimization $17, Workflow $21 per user per month, billed annually

What it is

Productivity analytics from application, website and activity data, with optional screenshots and no keystroke logging; free for three people.

Why it is on this list

ActivTrak is on the remote list for the manager who wants to know whether a remote team is overloaded or underused and does not want screenshots in the building. Its focus and workload reports work as well remotely as in an office, and the free tier for three people is a reasonable way to see whether the data changes any decision. The gap is Linux, and the temptation to buy the $10 plan and find the useful reports are on the $15 one.

Where it is strong

  • A free plan for three people, which is the best way to see the data before buying
  • Productivity, utilisation and focus analytics that managers can act on without reading screenshots
  • No keystroke logging and optional screenshots: the least invasive serious tool here
  • Workforce planning, capacity and coaching features on the upper plans

Where it falls short

  • No Linux agent and no keystroke logging, so it cannot serve investigations
  • The useful features sit on the $15 and $17 plans; the $10 plan is thin
  • Data retention of 6 to 12 months unless you pay for more

DeskTime

Automatic tracking with a private time switch
Runs on Windows, macOS, Linux, iOS, Android Pricing (October 2026) Pro $7 and Premium $10 per user per month ($6.42 and $9.17 billed annually); Enterprise by quote; free Lite plan for one user; 14-day trial

What it is

An automatic time tracker with productivity scoring, a private time switch, shift scheduling and an absence calendar; screenshots only on Premium.

Why it is on this list

DeskTime is here as the budget remote option that treats people like adults: automatic tracking, a private time toggle, no screenshots unless you choose Premium and switch them on, and an absence calendar so remote leave is visible. Small remote teams that want productivity data without a monitoring culture choose it, and the Linux and mobile apps help.

Where it is strong

  • Automatic tracking with a private time toggle employees can use
  • Shift scheduling and absence calendar built in
  • Low price with a free single-user plan
  • Windows, Mac, Linux and mobile apps

Where it falls short

  • Screenshots only on Premium
  • Productivity scoring is simple and needs tuning per role
  • Reporting is lighter for large organisations

Monitask

Cheap screenshots-as-proof for hourly billing
Runs on Windows, macOS, Linux Pricing (October 2026) Pro $6.49, Business $8.99, Business Premium $12.99 per user per month billed annually ($7.99, $10.99, $15.99 monthly); Enterprise for 100-plus; 10-day trial

What it is

A time tracker with adjustable screenshots, activity and project tracking for small teams and agencies, with short data retention.

Why it is on this list

Monitask is here for the remote agency or team of freelancers where screenshots are the agreed proof of billable work. It is cheap, fast to deploy, and lets screenshot frequency be set per team or disabled. Retention of one to two months on the main plans is enough for billing disputes and not for much else.

Where it is strong

  • Inexpensive and quick to set up for a small team or agency
  • Screenshot frequency adjustable per team, or off
  • Project time, timesheets and payroll exports
  • Full URL and search query tracking on the top plan

Where it falls short

  • Data kept for only one or two months on the main plans
  • Website and app tracking only from the Business plan
  • Not built for large organisations

Time Champ

Attendance, shifts and monitoring at the lowest price here
Runs on Windows, macOS, Linux; iOS and Android for GPS Pricing (October 2026) Starter $3.90, Professional $6.90, Enterprise $13.90 per user per month billed annually; add-ons for screen recording, keylogger, project management, HR and DLP; free trial without a card

What it is

Attendance, time tracking and monitoring in one agent from $3.90 a seat, with configurable screenshots, blurring and visible or silent tracking.

Why it is on this list

Time Champ earns its place for remote teams that also need to know who was on shift, from where, and whether they started on time: automatic attendance with time zone support, multiple shifts and manager hierarchies on Professional, GPS on mobile, and screenshots that can be blurred or switched to visible mode so the person tracked knows what is captured. For a remote team in several time zones it is the cheapest way to get attendance and productivity data from one agent. History on Professional is short, which is the trade.

Where it is strong

  • Attendance, shifts and monitoring in one agent from $3.90 a seat
  • Screenshot frequency, blurring and silent or visible tracking are all configurable
  • Live screen view, real-time alerts and suspicious activity detection on Enterprise
  • Windows, Mac and Linux with GPS for mobile teams

Where it falls short

  • Detailed activity history is short on Professional; longer history means Enterprise
  • Screen recording, keylogging and DLP are paid add-ons
  • A younger vendor with a smaller review base than the leaders

Teramind

Security-grade capture for the cases that need it
Runs on Windows (full); macOS and Linux with reduced features Pricing (October 2026) Starter $14, UAM $28, DLP $32 per seat per month billed annually ($15, $30, $35 monthly); 5-seat minimum; Enterprise by quote

What it is

A security-grade monitoring and data loss prevention platform: screen video, keystrokes, OCR, behaviour rules that block, cloud or on-premise.

Why it is on this list

Teramind is the last entry and a conditional one: remote teams handling regulated data, customer records or source code sometimes need security-grade capture, and Teramind provides it with a stealth agent and DLP rules that work off the office network. It is not a remote productivity tool and should not be bought as one.

Where it is strong

  • The deepest capture in the category: screen video, keystrokes, OCR, files, email, chat
  • Behaviour rules that can block or lock, not just report
  • DLP, private cloud and on-premise for regulated environments
  • Revealed, standard and stealth agents for different policies

Where it falls short

  • Five-seat minimum and the highest entry price here
  • Mac and Linux agents lack many Windows features
  • Capable of collecting far more than most employers should; needs policy discipline

What remote monitoring needs that office monitoring does not

  • Offline tracking and later sync. Home internet drops; a tool that loses the hours loses the payroll. Hubstaff, Time Doctor, Time Champ and DeskTime all track offline.
  • Time zones. Attendance, shift and "late" reports have to understand that 9 a.m. is not one time. Time Champ and Time Doctor handle this explicitly; the others handle it by not reporting attendance at all.
  • Every platform. Remote teams have Linux developers and Chromebook contractors. ActivTrak has no Linux agent; Teramind's is limited.
  • Payment attached to time. For hourly and contractor teams the time record is the invoice; Hubstaff, Time Doctor and Monitask close that loop in the product.
  • Self-view. People working alone should see what is collected about them. Hubstaff, Time Doctor, DeskTime and Time Champ's visible mode all give the employee their own dashboard.
  • Overwork signals. Remote staff are more likely to work too long than too little. Time Doctor's work-life balance report and ActivTrak's workload balance view are the features that address it; the burnout data explains why it matters.

Screenshots: the policy decision

Screenshots are the feature remote teams argue about. They are proof of work for an hourly contractor and an intrusion for a salaried engineer, and the same tool can be either depending on settings. Three positions work in practice. None: ActivTrak, Insightful without the add-on, or DeskTime Pro; the data is applications, websites and activity. Blurred or infrequent: Hubstaff and Time Champ can blur, every tool here can reduce frequency; the screenshot proves a person was in a work application without reading their screen. Full, by agreement: for hourly billing where the client requires it, with the frequency and retention written into the contract. Whatever the position, it belongs in the policy and in the remote work policy, and the people tracked should be able to see their own screenshots and delete the ones that caught something personal.

Contractors are not employees

Many remote teams are mixed, and the monitoring rules differ. An independent contractor controls how the work is done; a client that dictates hours, requires monitoring software on the contractor's own machine and scores their activity is behaving like an employer, which is evidence for misclassification under the tests on the 1099 versus W-2 page. For contractors, monitoring should be limited to what the contract specifies as proof of billable time, on the contractor's terms; the heavier tools on this list are for employees on company devices. The monitoring remote employees guide covers the practical setup for the employee side.

The law for remote teams

The notice laws in New York, Connecticut and Delaware apply based on where the employee works, not where the company is, so a remote team can trigger three states' rules at once. Monitoring a personal device needs consent and should be limited to a work profile; recording audio needs all-party consent in about a dozen states; and in California the CCPA gives employees the right to see what has been collected about them. The state-by-state page has each rule.

How these tools were evaluated

I evaluated each tool against the same checklist: what it captures and how configurable that is, which platforms it runs on, how it treats the employee (visible or silent agent, access to their own data, screenshot blurring and private time), what the published pricing is and what the minimum commitment really costs, how long data is kept, where it can be deployed, and what the vendor's own documentation and verified user reviews say about reliability and support. Pricing was taken from each vendor's pricing page in October 2026; where a vendor publishes no price, the page says so rather than guessing. Free plans and trials were used where they exist. I did not run a formal benchmark, and the page does not claim one.

Key takeaways

  • Remote monitoring needs offline tracking, time zone awareness, every platform, payment attached to time, a self-view for the person tracked, and overwork signals.
  • Hubstaff for hourly distributed teams, Time Doctor where overwork is the worry, Insightful for analytics without screenshots.
  • Decide the screenshot policy (none, blurred, or full by agreement) before choosing the tool; most tools can do all three.
  • Contractors are not employees: monitoring their own devices and scoring their activity is misclassification evidence.
  • Notice laws follow the employee's state, so a remote team can trigger several at once.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best employee monitoring software for remote teams?

Hubstaff for distributed teams paid by the hour, because it puts time, screenshots, GPS and payroll in one product across every platform. Time Doctor for remote and outsourced staff where the concern is overwork as much as idleness. Insightful for remote knowledge teams that want utilisation and location analytics without screenshots. Time Champ is the value choice where attendance and shifts across time zones also matter.

Can an employer monitor remote employees on their personal computers?

Only with consent and only within a work profile or work accounts; installing monitoring software on a personal device without agreement is unlawful, and even with agreement it should not reach personal data. The safer practice is a company device, or a work container on the personal one. Company email and chat are visible to administrators from any device.

Should remote employee monitoring include screenshots?

It depends on the relationship. For hourly contractors screenshots are often the agreed proof of billable work; for salaried employees they are usually unnecessary and resented. Where used, reduce the frequency, enable blurring (Hubstaff and Time Champ support it), let people see and delete their own, and write the rule into the policy.

How do I monitor remote employees without destroying trust?

Tell them exactly what is collected and why, give them access to their own data, collect the minimum that answers your actual question, never let activity scores drive pay or discipline on their own, and use the data to fix workload rather than to catch people. Tools with employee dashboards and overwork reporting make this easier; the ethical monitoring playbook on this site sets out the full design.

Which monitoring tools work on Linux for remote developers?

Hubstaff, DeskTime, Insightful, Monitask, Time Champ and Time Doctor have Linux agents. ActivTrak does not; Teramind's Linux agent has reduced features. Chromebook is covered by Hubstaff, Time Doctor and ActivTrak.

Is it legal to monitor remote employees in other states?

Yes, subject to the law of the state where the employee works. New York, Connecticut and Delaware require written notice; roughly a dozen states require all-party consent for audio; California gives employees access rights to the data collected. A remote team spread across states should meet the strictest rule for everyone.
